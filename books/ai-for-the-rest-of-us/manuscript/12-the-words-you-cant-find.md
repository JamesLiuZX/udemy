# The Words You Can't Find

Linda's father-in-law died on a Thursday, and by Saturday she'd been asked to say a few words at the service, the kind of ask that arrives already assuming yes. She had forty years of memories and not one sentence. Every draft she tried on paper came out either too formal, a stranger's eulogy for a stranger's grandfather, or too raw, more grief than the room could hold at ten in the morning on a Saturday. She sat with a blank page for two hours the night before and produced nothing she could stand to read out loud.

This chapter exists for exactly that two hours, and for every smaller version of it: the sympathy card where "sorry for your loss" feels too thin, the wedding toast that needs to be funny and sincere in the same three minutes, the birthday message for someone you love where "happy birthday" alone feels like showing up empty-handed. These are not tasks with a right answer to look up. They're tasks where you know exactly what you want to say and the words simply won't come, which is a completely different kind of stuck than anything else in this book.

## What Linda actually did, and what she didn't

She didn't ask an AI tool to write her father-in-law's eulogy. That would have handed the hardest, most personal task to a machine that never met him, and read as exactly that at the service, generic warmth standing in for forty years nobody else in that room had lived. What she did instead was closer to what a good editor does: she talked through three specific memories out loud into her phone, unstructured, some of it barely making sense, the way grief talks. Then she asked the AI tool to help her find the shape underneath what she'd said: which memory to open with, how the three pieces might connect, whether four minutes of rambling could become ninety seconds that actually landed.

What came back wasn't her eulogy. It was scaffolding built entirely from her own words, rearranged and tightened, not one new sentence of content added. She rewrote most of it again in her own voice before the service, cutting a transition that felt too smooth for how she actually talks, keeping the structure. What she read out loud on Saturday was unmistakably hers, about her father-in-law, in a voice only someone who'd known him forty years could have written. The tool didn't supply the memories. It helped her find the shape she couldn't see in her own exhausted, grieving 11 p.m. draft.

## The line, for this chapter specifically

Every chapter in this book has drawn some version of a line, and this one matters as much as any of them: an AI tool can help you find the shape of something you already feel and can't organize. It should never generate the feeling itself. A eulogy, a wedding toast, a sympathy message, these only mean something because they came from someone who actually knew the person, actually feels the thing being said. Ask the tool to invent a fond memory you never had, and even if nobody in the room could technically prove it was borrowed, you'd know, and that knowledge would sit wrong under every future memory of the moment. The honest use is always the same shape: you supply the real material, spoken, messy, unstructured if that's how it comes out. The tool helps you find where it wants to go.

## Marisol's version: smaller, still real

Marisol's best friend from high school had a miscarriage, and Marisol wanted to send something better than the card-aisle default, something that actually said she remembered how badly her friend had wanted this. She typed, into the chat, three true things: how long her friend had been trying, a specific conversation they'd had about names, and the fact that Marisol didn't know the right thing to say and didn't want to pretend she did. She asked for help turning those three true things into a short message, not a poem, not something that sounded like a greeting card, something that sounded like her.

What she sent was four sentences. It named the actual conversation about names. It didn't try to make anything better, because nothing could, and it said that plainly instead of reaching for comfort it didn't have. Her friend called her that night, not to talk about the message specifically, just to talk, which is probably the actual measure of whether four sentences did their job.

## When it's a happy occasion instead of a hard one

Not every version of this chapter is grief. A toast, a birthday message, a note tucked into a graduation gift all hit the same blank-page wall for a happier reason: the words matter enough that the ordinary, generic version feels like it undersells how much you actually mean it. Hal's retirement-dinner toast from chapter fifteen used exactly this three-true-things method, three specific memories of a forty-year friendship rather than a generic "he'll be missed," and the room laughed at the right moment because the memory was real and specific, not because a joke had been engineered to land. The method doesn't change with the occasion. Only the true things being gathered do.

## Try this: the three-true-things method

Whatever the occasion, a toast, a card, a hard message you can't find the opening line for, start the same way Linda and Marisol both did without planning it that way: say or write down three specific, true things, unpolished, in whatever order they come out. A memory. A detail only you would know. The thing you actually feel, stated plainly, even if it's "I don't know what to say." Then ask the AI tool to help you find the shape connecting those three things into something you could actually say out loud or send. Rewrite the parts that don't sound like you. The true things were always the hard part, and they were always yours. This just finds the shape faster than a blank page at 11 p.m. usually lets you.
