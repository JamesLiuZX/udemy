# Caring for a Parent

Marisol's mother's doctor had recently added a fourth medication to a list that already ran to three, and Marisol had started keeping track on a sticky note stuck to her own refrigerator, two states away from where her mother actually lived, because there wasn't a better system and the stakes of getting it wrong felt too high to leave to memory alone. She wasn't a nurse. She wasn't trained for any of this. She was just the daughter who happened to live close enough to visit twice a month and far enough to worry the rest of the time about what she wasn't seeing.

[KEY-INSIGHT: 63 million Americans, nearly one in four adults, provided unpaid care for an adult family member or friend in the past year, a number that's grown by 20 million in just the past decade. Marisol's sticky note isn't a sign she's disorganized. It's the ordinary toolkit of an enormous, largely invisible workforce that mostly improvises its own systems, because almost nobody hands new caregivers one. || Source: AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving, "Caregiving in the US 2025."]

## What actually helped, and what stayed entirely hers

Marisol used the same appointment-prep habit from chapter six, scaled to an ongoing situation instead of a single visit: after each of her mother's appointments, she'd type up the doctor's notes and any new medication into a chat window and ask, "Here's an update on my mother's care: [she pasted the details]. In plain English, are there any common interactions or timing issues I should ask her regular doctor about, and help me build a simple daily medication schedule I can share with her." What came back wasn't medical advice about what her mother should take, it explicitly wasn't that, and Marisol knew not to treat it that way. It was a clearly organized schedule, morning and evening columns, and a specific, well-formed question to bring to her mother's actual doctor about two medications with a known common interaction worth asking about directly.

The schedule replaced the sticky note. The actual medical judgment, what her mother should take and when, stayed exactly where it belonged: with her mother's doctor and pharmacist, the only people with her mother's full chart in front of them.

## The line, drawn as firmly as chapter six drew it

An AI tool can help organize information you already have: a medication list, an appointment history, a shared calendar with siblings who live further away and want to help but don't know how. It cannot replace a doctor's judgment about your parent's specific health, and it should never be asked to weigh in on whether a symptom is serious enough to call someone about. When in doubt about anything urgent, call the doctor or, for anything that feels like an emergency, call emergency services, the same non-negotiable line chapter fourteen drew around a genuine crisis.

## Linda's version: further along the same road

Linda had been the one managing Hal's father's care in his final years, before this book's own story begins, and what she wished she'd had back then was something closer to what Marisol has now: a plain-English way to understand a new diagnosis fast, at eleven at night, when a specialist's letter arrived full of terms nobody had explained. She uses it differently now, further along, mostly for the logistics: a shared document, built with an AI tool's help organizing scattered notes into one place, that she and her siblings all update after a parent's appointment, so nobody's flying blind and nobody has to be the single point of failure holding every detail in their own head alone.

## Caregiving from a distance, specifically

A real slice of caregiving happens exactly the way Marisol's does, from another state, coordinated in fragments between two jobs and two kids, and it's worth naming what changes when you're not the one physically in the room. She built a running document, an AI tool's help organizing months of scattered texts, calls, and appointment notes into one chronological summary she could hand to a sibling visiting in person, or read from herself on a call with a new specialist who'd never met her before. "Here are my notes from the past three months of my mother's care, in whatever order I have them. Organize this into a clean timeline I can reference or share." turned a scattered pile of half-remembered details into something a doctor unfamiliar with the case could actually use in the first two minutes of an appointment, instead of Marisol trying to reconstruct months of history from memory, under time pressure, in a room she wasn't even in.

## Try this: the caregiving organizer prompt

"I'm helping care for [family member]. Here's the current situation: [medications, appointments, or concerns, described honestly]. Help me organize this into a clear, shareable [schedule / summary / calendar] I can use myself and share with other family members helping out." Use the result to stay organized and to sharpen the questions you bring to the actual doctor. Never let it replace that doctor's judgment, and never treat a symptom question as anything but a reason to call a real person.

[TAKEAWAYS]

- Nearly one in four American adults provides unpaid care for a family member. If this feels overwhelming and improvised, that's the ordinary experience of an enormous, largely invisible group, not a sign you're doing it wrong.
- An AI tool is genuinely useful for organizing scattered information: medication schedules, appointment histories, a shared record other family members can see.
- It cannot replace a doctor's medical judgment, and any symptom question belongs to a real doctor or, for anything urgent, emergency services, not a chat window.
- Sharing a clear, organized record with other family members helping out means no single person has to hold every detail alone.
