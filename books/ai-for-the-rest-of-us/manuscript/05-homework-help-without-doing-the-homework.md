# Homework Help Without Doing the Homework

Marisol's son Diego, ten years old, slid his math worksheet across the counter the way he always does when he's stuck: not asking for help exactly, just leaving it there and hoping. Fractions. Word problems about pizza, which is always pizza, as if no other food divides. She used to be fine at this kind of math. She is less fine at it after a twelve-hour shift, at eight at night, with dinner dishes still in the sink.

Her first instinct was to type the whole problem into a chat window and read Diego the answer. She stopped with her thumb over the screen. That felt like cheating, not for her, for him, and she couldn't quite say why except that it felt true the way a lot of parenting worries feel true before you've thought them all the way through.

What she typed instead was this: "My ten-year-old is stuck on a fractions word problem about splitting pizza between friends. Don't give me the answer. Ask him one question at a time to help him figure it out himself, like a patient tutor would." She read the first question out loud to Diego. He got quiet, thought about it, and answered it. The AI's next question built on his answer. Four questions later, he solved it himself and looked almost annoyed that it had been that simple.

## The line, stated plainly

Here is the actual line, the one Marisol was reaching for without the words for it yet: using AI to give your kid the answer is doing the homework for them. Using it to ask your kid the questions a good tutor would ask, so they arrive at the answer themselves, is not. The difference isn't the tool. It's which side of the desk the tool is sitting on.

## What this looks like with an older kid

Linda's granddaughter Sofia, fifteen, was stuck on an essay about the causes of the American Revolution, not because she didn't understand the material, but because she'd been staring at a blank document for forty minutes and every draft she started felt wrong. Linda, watching her granddaughter's frustration build over a Sunday visit, suggested trying the same question-first approach Marisol had used with Diego, adjusted for a teenager who needed a different kind of help: not tutoring on the facts, but a way past the blank page.

Sofia typed: "I'm writing a history essay on the causes of the American Revolution. I understand the material but I'm stuck starting. Ask me questions about what I think the strongest cause was and why, don't write any of the essay itself, just help me find my argument." Fifteen minutes of back and forth later, she had an argument she actually believed (that the taxation disputes mattered less on their own than the sense of being governed without any say at all) and she wrote the essay herself, in her own words, starting from an idea she'd found through the questions rather than been handed.

The essay was hers. Every sentence in it came from her. What the AI tool did was closer to what a good writing tutor does in office hours: ask the questions that help a stuck student find their own argument, then get out of the way.

## The honest exception: explaining something once, plainly

Not every homework moment is about building an argument or solving a fraction step by step. Sometimes a kid just needs a concept explained once, clearly, the way you'd explain it if you remembered the material yourself. "Explain what a noun clause is, with three simple examples, the way you'd explain it to a ten-year-old" isn't tutoring toward an answer they need to produce themselves. It's the AI equivalent of pulling out a reference book, and there's nothing dishonest about a kid using a reference book. The line stays in the same place either way: explaining a concept is fine, generating the actual assignment text for them to copy is not.

## Try this: the tutor prompt, adapted to your kid

The exact wording that worked for both Diego and Sofia, adaptable to any age and any subject: "My [age]-year-old is working on [subject/assignment]. Don't give the answer directly. Ask one question at a time to help them figure it out themselves, and adjust the difficulty based on how they respond." Keep that sentence somewhere you'll actually find it again, a notes app, a sticky note by the computer, because it's the one sentence in this whole chapter worth reusing verbatim, every time, for years.

## What to say if the school has a policy

Many schools now have explicit rules about AI use on assignments, and those rules vary school to school and even teacher to teacher. Before using any of this on a specific assignment, especially with an older kid, check what's actually allowed for that class. The tutoring approach in this chapter, using AI to ask questions rather than produce answers, is honest by design, but "honest by design" and "matches your kid's specific teacher's specific policy" aren't automatically the same thing, and it's worth five minutes to make sure they line up before homework night, not during it.
