# Money Questions You're Embarrassed to Ask

Marisol's new job offered two health insurance options during open enrollment, a page of terms she half-recognized and half-didn't: deductible, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum. She'd had insurance for years and still couldn't have told you, with confidence, what the difference between a deductible and a copay actually was. Everyone around her at work seemed to just know, comparing plans on their lunch break like it was nothing, and asking out loud felt like announcing something about herself she didn't want announced.

She didn't ask a coworker. She opened a chat window on her break and typed: "Explain deductible, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximum like I've never seen an insurance form before. Then look at these two options [she typed out the numbers from the enrollment page] and tell me what questions I should be asking myself to pick between them, not which one to pick, just what actually matters here." Nobody watched her type it. Nobody knew it was a question she felt she should already know the answer to. Ten minutes later she understood the difference well enough to compare the two plans against what she actually needed: fewer doctor visits, but her younger kid saw a specialist twice a year, and that changed which plan made sense.

If a plain money question feels embarrassing to ask out loud, it's worth remembering that a real, sizable share of the room would be in the same position. || Source: Federal Reserve Board, "Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2024," May 2025.]

That number is worth sitting with for a second, because the shame around not understanding money is almost always private and almost never accurate. More than a third of adults would be caught out by the same $400 surprise Marisol has budgeted around for years. Financial confusion isn't a sign you're behind. It's close to the median experience, just one nobody says out loud at the office.

## What this actually helps with, and what it doesn't

An AI tool is genuinely useful for exactly the thing Marisol used it for: explaining a term you're embarrassed not to know, running the numbers on a comparison you could technically do yourself but keep putting off, or helping you understand a bill or a form well enough to ask the right follow-up question. It is not a financial advisor, it doesn't know your full financial picture unless you tell it everything relevant, and it should never be the final word on a decision with real consequences, how to invest retirement savings, whether to refinance a house, how to handle a tax problem that's already escalated. For those, "helps me understand the options well enough to talk to a real advisor" is the right frame, the same not-a-replacement line chapter six draws around doctors, drawn here around anyone whose actual job is giving financial advice.

## Linda's version: the question she'd been avoiding for a decade

Linda and Hal had a will, drafted years ago, before their grandkids were born and before a piece of property changed hands in the family. She'd meant to ask a lawyer whether it still made sense. She hadn't, for the same reason a lot of these questions get avoided: it felt like it should be a simple yes-or-no question, and admitting she didn't already know the answer felt like admitting she'd been careless about something important for a decade.

She typed: "Our will was drafted twelve years ago, before two grandkids were born and before we sold a rental property. In plain English, what are the general reasons a will might need updating after changes like that, and what specific questions should I bring to a lawyer so the meeting isn't wasted figuring out what to even ask?" What came back wasn't legal advice, and it said so. It was a plain-English orientation to why timing and life changes matter for a will, and a genuinely useful list of questions that turned a vague, dreaded "I should probably talk to someone about the will sometime" into an actual appointment booked for the following Tuesday, with a list in hand instead of a fuzzy sense that something might be out of date.

That's the real value here: not skipping the lawyer, getting to the lawyer's office already oriented, so the paid hour goes toward the parts only a lawyer can actually do.

## When the number itself feels shameful, not just confusing

Some money questions aren't about jargon at all, they're about a number that already feels like a verdict on how well you've managed things: credit card debt that's grown quietly for years, a retirement account that never got started. Marisol carried a balance she'd stopped opening the statement for, the shame of the number itself bigger than any confusion about what it meant. "I have [amount] in credit card debt at roughly [rate]. In plain English, what are the realistic ways people typically pay this down, and what should I understand before choosing one?" didn't erase the number or the discomfort of it. It turned a feeling of vague, paralyzing shame into an actual short list of real approaches, worth bringing to a nonprofit credit counselor, several of which exist specifically to help without charging for the conversation, rather than a number she kept avoiding entirely because looking at it felt worse than not knowing.

## Try this: the plain-English money prompt

Whatever the specific question is, a form, a bill, a comparison between two options, an unfamiliar term on a pay stub, the same shape of prompt works: "Explain [the specific term or document] like I've never seen this before. Then help me think through [the specific decision or comparison], not by telling me what to pick, but by telling me what actually matters here." That second half matters as much as the first. It keeps the tool in the role of clarifying your options rather than making the decision for you, which is exactly the role it's actually good at.

## Why "just ask a friend" doesn't fully solve this

It's a fair question: couldn't Marisol have just asked a coworker, and couldn't Linda have just called her sister? Sometimes, yes, and a real conversation with someone who knows you is still worth more than any chat window, especially for a decision this personal. But the private, judgment-free version matters for a specific reason: it removes the moment of exposure, the flush of admitting out loud that you don't already know something everyone else seems to. For some people that moment isn't a big deal. For plenty of others, it's exactly the thing that's kept a real, answerable question unanswered for years, quietly, at a real cost.
