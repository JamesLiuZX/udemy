# When It Gets It Wrong

Linda asked a simple question about Hal's new blood pressure medication: could he take it with his regular allergy pill. The answer came back fast, specific, and confident, naming an interaction between the two drugs that sounded exactly like the kind of precise detail a trustworthy answer would have. She almost just told Hal what she'd read. Something made her pause, the same instinct that once made her double-check a recipe that seemed to be missing a step, and she asked their pharmacist instead. The pharmacist had never heard of that interaction. It didn't exist. The AI tool hadn't hedged, hadn't said "I'm not certain," hadn't sounded any different from the dozen other times it had been exactly right. It had simply been wrong, with total confidence, and Linda had almost acted on it.

This is the chapter every other chapter in this book has been quietly pointing toward. Being specific, chapter two's whole argument, gets you a much better answer. It does not get you a guaranteed one. No chapter in this book, including this one, can promise you an AI tool will never be confidently, plausibly, specifically wrong. What this chapter can do is give you the habit that catches it when it happens, before it costs you something.

## The pattern, and why your instincts alone won't catch it

[KEY-INSIGHT: Wharton researchers ran experiments with over 1,300 participants and found people accepted a correct AI answer 93 percent of the time, and accepted a wrong AI answer, delivered with the same confident tone and no caveats, 80 percent of the time. The researchers call this "cognitive surrender": people don't just trust AI, they borrow its confidence, without checking whether that confidence is earned. || Source: Shaw and Nave, "Thinking, Fast, Slow, and Artificial," Wharton School research, 2026.]

Read that gap again: 93 percent versus 80 percent. If an AI tool sounded less certain when it was wrong, the way an honest person's voice sometimes wavers on a guess, this would be a much smaller problem. It doesn't sound less certain. A wrong answer and a right answer can sound, word for word, identically sure of themselves. That's the actual trap, and it's not a character flaw in the people who fall for it. It's a completely reasonable response to a tool that has been right often enough to earn trust, delivering a wrong answer in exactly the voice of a right one.

## Marisol's version: smaller stakes, same pattern

Marisol asked whether the library near her apartment was open on Sundays, planning to take Diego there after his soccer game. The answer said yes, ten to four, specific hours, no hesitation. They drove over. The library was closed on Sundays, and had been for over a year, since a budget cut Marisol vaguely remembered hearing about and had forgotten. Diego was disappointed. It cost them forty minutes and a wasted trip, not a medication error, but the exact same failure: confident, specific, wrong, and nothing about how it was said that would have tipped her off.

That's worth noticing on purpose: the pattern doesn't only show up on high-stakes questions. It shows up on library hours and restaurant addresses and whether a store still carries something, just as easily as it shows up on a drug interaction. The habit that catches it has to be a habit, not a special alarm you only switch on when the topic feels important, because by the time a wrong answer sounds important, you've usually already half-believed it.

## The habit that actually works

Here's what would have caught both of Linda's and Marisol's mistakes, and it's a genuinely small habit, not a research project every time you ask a question: for anything where being wrong would cost you money, time, or your health, check the specific, checkable fact against one more source before you act on it. Not the whole answer, usually. Just the specific claim doing the load-bearing work: the drug interaction, the library hours, the price, the deadline. A second's search, a phone call, a pharmacist, a real hours listing.

This isn't "don't trust AI." It's the same habit you'd already use with any other single source: one confident voice, checked once, before it changes something real.

## A quick way to tell when you're at risk

The moment to be most careful is exactly the moment an answer feels most reassuring: specific, confident, exactly what you were hoping to hear, and delivered fast. That combination feels like relief, and relief is precisely when people stop checking. Slow down for one extra question in that specific moment, not because the answer is more likely to be wrong then than any other time, but because it's the moment you're least likely to notice if it is.

## Try this: the two-question check

Before acting on anything specific and consequential an AI tool told you, ask two questions of yourself, not the tool: What's the one fact in this answer that, if wrong, would actually matter? And have I checked that one fact anywhere else? If you can answer both honestly, in most cases the fact is small enough that being wrong costs you little, or you've already checked it. When you can't answer either one, that's the moment to check before you act, not after.

[TAKEAWAYS]

- AI can be confidently, specifically, plausibly wrong, and nothing about how it sounds will tip you off. A wrong answer and a right one can be delivered in the exact same tone.
- Research on "cognitive surrender" found people accept wrong AI answers 80 percent of the time when delivered with confidence, only slightly less than the 93 percent acceptance rate for correct answers.
- The fix isn't distrust, it's one habit: for anything that would cost you money, time, or health if wrong, check the one load-bearing fact against a second source before you act.
- The moment you're most at risk is the moment an answer feels most reassuring. That's exactly when people stop checking, and exactly when it's worth one more question.
