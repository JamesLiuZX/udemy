# The Ordinary Emergency

The power went out at 9:40 on a Tuesday night, no storm, no warning, just dark, and Marisol's ten-year-old's insulin needed to stay refrigerated. Not a life-or-death emergency in the next five minutes, but a real one on a clock, the specific kind of ordinary crisis that doesn't wait for a calm Sunday afternoon and doesn't come with a manual. She didn't have her utility company's number memorized. She didn't know how long a closed fridge actually stays cold enough, or what her actual options were at ten at night in a house that had just gone dark.

She typed into her phone, one-handed, the porch light from a neighbor's house the only light in the kitchen: "Power just went out, no storm. I have insulin in the fridge that needs to stay cold. How long does a closed fridge actually stay safely cold, and what should I do right now if it goes longer than that?" The answer came in seconds: a closed refrigerator generally holds a safe temperature for about four hours, longer if you avoid opening the door, and if the outage looked likely to run longer than that, a cooler with ice was the right next move, along with the general safe temperature range to keep the insulin within. She didn't need to know any of that before the outage. She needed it in the next ninety seconds, and got it faster than any phone call, any hold music, any search through old paperwork for a customer service number would have.

## Why this is a different chapter than the rest of the book

Every other chapter in this book has room to slow down: pause, think, ask a follow-up, walk away and come back. This one doesn't, by definition, and it's worth saying plainly what changes when the clock is the actual constraint. Speed matters more than the careful verification chapter eight and chapter eleven both insisted on. Chapter eight is still right that AI can be confidently wrong, and that risk doesn't disappear here. What changes is the tradeoff: at 9:40 on a dark Tuesday, a fast, generally-reliable answer about refrigerator safety beats a slower, more thoroughly verified one you don't have time to get. This is the one place in the book where "good enough, right now" is a genuinely correct choice, not a shortcut you're settling for.

## Where the real emergency line sits

Said as plainly as chapter six said it about a doctor's office: for anything that's actually a medical emergency, chest pain, difficulty breathing, a serious injury, a child who's unresponsive, call emergency services first, every time, before typing anything into anything. This chapter is about the enormous category of situations underneath that line: real, urgent, stressful, but not a call-emergency-services situation, where a fast, plain-English answer genuinely helps and a chatbot can give you one faster than searching through old paperwork or waiting on hold.

## Linda's version: locked out, cold, and out of ideas

Linda locked her keys in the car outside the pharmacy, in February, with Hal waiting at home expecting her back for dinner and no way to reach her. She didn't have a locksmith's number saved, didn't know if her car insurance covered this, and didn't want to just stand in a parking lot being cold and stuck. She typed: "Locked my keys in my car, [make and model], I have AAA membership. What's the fastest way to get help right now, and is this something AAA typically covers?" The answer confirmed AAA roadside assistance almost always covers a lockout, gave her the general number to call, and mentioned it's worth checking her specific membership tier since coverage sometimes varies. She called AAA directly from that answer, in under a minute, instead of digging through her purse for a membership card with a number on the back she could have squinted at just as easily, except she was cold and flustered and the phone answer got her moving faster.

Nothing about that exchange required a chatbot specifically. It required something fast, plain, and immediately actionable, in a moment where digging for the actual answer herself would have cost real minutes standing in the cold.

## What makes an ordinary emergency prompt work

The pattern across both examples: state the actual situation in one sentence, state the actual constraint (a clock, a specific need, what you already have access to), and ask for the next step, not a general explanation. "What should I do right now" beats "tell me about power outages" the same way every specific question in this book has beaten its vague version, just compressed into fewer seconds of typing because that's what the moment allows.

## Try this, before you need it

The best time to think about this chapter is not during the emergency. Take five minutes sometime this week to note, in your phone, the handful of things you'd want fast answers about if something ordinary went wrong tonight: what medications in your house need refrigeration and for how long, your insurance and roadside assistance basics, the non-emergency number for your local police department for something urgent but not a 911 situation. None of that requires an AI tool to gather. It just means that when a Tuesday night goes dark, you're not starting completely cold.
