# Looking for Work Again

Marisol hadn't updated her résumé since the job she was currently trying to leave, six years of the same bullet points sitting untouched at the top of a document she barely recognized as describing her actual work anymore. The new posting she wanted, a step up, better hours, asked for a cover letter too, and the blank page for that felt worse than the résumé: how do you sell yourself in three paragraphs without sounding like every other cover letter the hiring manager had already skimmed and forgotten that morning.

She'd applied to jobs before by copying the same résumé everywhere and hoping. This time she tried something different, prompted by nothing more sophisticated than exhaustion with the old way. She pasted her rough, outdated résumé and the actual job posting into a chat window and typed: "Here's my current résumé and the job posting I'm applying to. Help me rewrite my bullet points to actually speak to what this posting is asking for, using only things that are true about my real experience, nothing invented. Then help me draft a short cover letter that sounds like a real person, not a form letter."

[KEY-INSIGHT: Jobscan's 2025 survey of job seekers found the search dragging on longer than most people expect, with roughly 14 percent of applicants submitting 100 or more applications before landing an offer. A generic résumé blasted out unchanged to every posting is a big part of why: it asks a hiring manager to do the work of translating your experience into their specific need, and most won't bother. || Source: Jobscan, "The State of the Job Search in 2025."]

## What actually got better, and what didn't

The rewrite didn't invent anything. It couldn't, and she made sure not to let it: every bullet point still described work she'd actually done. What changed was translation, the same six years of experience reframed in the specific language the posting used, "team coordination" instead of the vaguer "worked with others" her old résumé had settled for, because the posting itself used that exact phrase and her actual work matched it precisely. That's not dishonesty. It's the same skill a good career coach charges for: hearing what a specific employer is actually asking for and showing them the truest, most relevant version of your real experience.

## The line, and why it matters more here than almost anywhere else

Nowhere in this book does the never-invent rule matter more than here. A résumé is a factual document, and a fabricated skill or an inflated title isn't a style choice, it's the kind of lie that gets discovered in the first real interview question and ends the conversation immediately, sometimes worse than that. Use an AI tool to reframe, translate, and tighten what's true. Never let it add a certification you don't hold, a title you didn't have, or a skill you can't actually back up if asked about it in the room. The tool doesn't know your real work history. Only you can vouch for what's actually true, and every sentence in the final document has to be something you could defend, in detail, to someone's face.

## Linda's version: coming back after a longer gap

Linda hadn't worked outside the home in eleven years, since before Hal retired, and a friend at church had mentioned a part-time bookkeeping role at a local nonprofit that sounded like a good fit for the skills she'd kept sharp managing the household's finances for decades. The gap felt like the whole problem. How do you explain eleven years on a résumé without it reading as a red flag before anyone's even met you.

She typed: "I haven't worked outside the home in 11 years but have managed complex household finances, budgets, and bookkeeping that whole time. I'm applying for a part-time bookkeeping role. Help me frame this experience honestly on a résumé, and suggest how to address the gap in a cover letter without it sounding like an apology." The answer didn't hide the gap or dress it up as something it wasn't. It reframed the actual, real skill underneath it, eleven years of real budgeting and financial management, just not inside a company, and suggested language for the cover letter that named the gap plainly in one sentence and moved straight to what she'd bring to the role, rather than lingering on an apology nobody needed from her.

## Interview prep, the same tutoring shape as chapter five

The interview itself brought a different kind of nerves, less about what to write and more about freezing up under a question she half-expected but hadn't rehearsed answering out loud. She used the same question-first approach chapter five used for Diego's homework, adapted for herself: "I have an interview for [the role] on [date]. Ask me common interview questions for this kind of position, one at a time, and after each answer, give me brief, honest feedback on what worked and what I could tighten." It wasn't a script to memorize. It was practice, the same way a good manager might run a mock interview, except available at ten at night after the kids were asleep, as many times as she actually needed it, without anyone's schedule but her own to work around.

## Try this: the translate-don't-invent prompt

"Here's my résumé and the job posting I'm applying for. Rewrite my experience to speak directly to what this posting asks for, using only things that are actually true about my background. Then help me draft a short cover letter in a genuine, non-generic voice." Read every line back afterward and ask yourself the interview-room test: could you explain this exact claim, in detail, to someone's face. If the honest answer is no, cut it before you send it, no matter how good it sounds.

- A generic résumé sent unchanged to every posting is one of the biggest reasons a job search drags on. Translating your real experience into each posting's specific language is a skill, not dishonesty, as long as everything stays true.
