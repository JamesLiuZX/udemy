# The Scams and the Sketchy Stuff

Linda's phone rang at 6:40 in the morning, her grandson's number on the screen, and a voice that was unmistakably his, panicked, saying he'd been in a car accident and needed bail money wired immediately, please don't call Mom, he'd explain later. Linda's hand was already reaching for her purse before a second thought caught up with the first: her grandson was fifteen. He didn't have a car. She hung up and called her daughter, who confirmed he was asleep upstairs, safe, and had never made that call.

Nothing about the voice had sounded wrong. That's the part worth sitting with. It wasn't a bad impression, or a stranger's voice pretending, the way an old scam might have sounded slightly off if you listened closely. It sounded like him, panic and all, because it had been built from him: pulled from a video he'd posted online, run through a tool that can now clone a convincing voice from three seconds of audio. Linda didn't catch this one because the voice gave anything away. She caught it because the story didn't add up, and she stopped to check before she acted, the same habit chapter eight just spent a whole chapter building.

The same technology helping you write a letter or plan a trip is available, right now, to people using it to convince someone's grandmother that her grandson is in a jail cell. Saying that plainly is the only honest way to write this chapter.

## Why this specifically targets older adults, and what to do with that

It's not an accident that older adults are targeted harder. Scammers running these calls are optimizing for exactly two things: someone likely to have money set aside, and someone less likely to have already heard about this specific scam. Neither of those is about intelligence or carelessness. Being newer to a piece of technology, or less exposed to the latest version of a scam, is not a character flaw, and if you're reading this chapter as the older adult in your own family, nothing about falling for this once, or almost falling for it, says anything about you except that you hadn't heard about this particular trick yet. Now you have.

## The one habit that catches almost all of it

Here it is, stated as plainly as chapter eight's habit: if anyone calls, texts, or emails asking for money, gift cards, wire transfers, or account information urgently, especially tied to a family emergency, hang up or stop replying, and contact that person directly through a number or method you already know and trust, not the number that called you. Every version of this scam depends on you acting inside the panic, before you have time to verify. The moment you step outside that call and check independently, the scam almost always falls apart in seconds, exactly the way Linda's daughter's sleeping son settled it in one text.

That's it. That's the whole defense, the same shape as chapter eight's habit aimed at a sharper danger: don't verify the story from inside the call. Verify it from outside, through a channel the caller doesn't control.

## Marisol's version: protecting the other direction

Marisol's worry ran the other way. Her mother, seventy-eight, lives alone two states away and has started getting calls Marisol only hears about after the fact, a "grandson" asking for money, a "bank" asking her to confirm her account number. Marisol couldn't be in the room for every call. What she could do was have one direct conversation, and she used an AI tool to help her prepare it, the same way chapter six used one to prepare for a doctor's appointment rather than to replace the doctor.

She typed: "Help me write a short, warm, non-alarming script I can use to talk to my 78-year-old mother about phone and text scams, especially the kind where a caller's voice sounds exactly like a family member's. I don't want to scare her or talk down to her. I want her to know the one thing to do if it happens: hang up and call me directly." What came back gave her a starting point she reshaped in her own words, warmer, shorter, with an actual joke her mother would recognize as coming from her. The script wasn't the point. Having one, instead of winging a hard conversation in the moment, was.

## The sketchy stuff beyond phone calls

Voice cloning is the sharpest version of this, but it's not the only one. Fake customer service numbers that show up first in a search result. Texts claiming to be a delivery company needing you to "confirm your address" through a link. AI-written emails that no longer have the spelling mistakes that used to be the easy tell. The underlying rule is the same across every version: urgency plus a request for money, access, or personal information is the pattern, regardless of the specific costume it's wearing that week. Learn the pattern once, and it covers scams that haven't been invented yet.

## What a real message from your bank actually looks like

One more specific pattern worth naming, because it's common enough to deserve its own line: a text or email claiming to be your bank, asking you to click a link and "verify" your account. A real bank does not ask you to confirm your password, PIN, or full account number by text or email, ever. If a message claims to be your bank and asks for exactly that, it isn't your bank. Close it, and if you're genuinely unsure, call the number printed on the back of your actual card, never a number or link inside the suspicious message itself.

## Try this: the family password

A specific tool worth setting up before you need it, not after: agree on a word or short phrase with the people you'd trust to call you about a real emergency, something a scammer voice-cloned from a public video would have no way to know. If a "family emergency" call doesn't include it, that's your answer before you've said a word back.
