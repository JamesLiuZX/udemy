# The One Sentence That Changes Everything

A few weeks after the insurance letter, Linda tried something new. She wanted help planning what to cook for a dinner with her son's family, six people, one of them newly vegetarian. She typed: "Give me dinner ideas."

The answer came back fine. Generic, but fine. A list of pasta dishes, a stir-fry, a salad. Nothing wrong with any of it. Nothing that felt like it was actually for her Sunday, either. It read like it could have been written for anyone, because it had been. She'd given almost no information, so there was almost nothing for the answer to be built from.

She tried again. This time: "I'm cooking dinner for six people this Sunday. One of them just went vegetarian and I don't want to make her a separate meal. Give me one main dish everyone can eat, nothing too spicy because my husband can't handle heat."

Same tool. Completely different answer. A specific dish. A reason it worked for a mixed table. A note about where to find a milder version of one ingredient. The second version wasn't a better tool. It was one sentence longer, and that one sentence was the entire difference.

## Why one sentence does that much

Think about what happened between those two questions. In the first one, Linda knew she was cooking for six people, that one of them was vegetarian, that her husband doesn't like spicy food. All of that was true and sitting in her head the whole time. None of it made it into the question. The tool never had it, so it couldn't use it, the same way a friend couldn't help plan the meal if Linda called and only said "give me dinner ideas" and hung up before saying anything else.

This is really the whole chapter, so it's worth saying it plainly: **the AI can only work with what you actually tell it.** Not what you're thinking. Not what seems obvious to you. What you typed. When an answer feels generic, that's almost never the tool being bad at its job. It's the question being a smaller version of what you actually meant, and the answer coming back exactly as small.

[KEY-INSIGHT: Usability research on how people interact with AI chatbots consistently finds the same pattern: a vague, open-ended question produces a generic, one-size-fits-all answer, while a specific request, one that includes the actual goal and a bit of real context, produces an answer people rate as noticeably more useful and on target. || Source: aggregated usability findings on AI chatbot prompting, multiple published guides and research summaries, 2025-2026.]

## The one sentence, broken down

Linda's second try wasn't complicated. It just answered three quiet questions that the first version left blank.

**Who's it for?** Six people, one vegetarian. Not "dinner," dinner for this specific table.

**What actually matters here?** One dish everyone can eat, nothing separate to cook. That's the real goal, not just "ideas."

**What should it avoid?** No heavy spice, because of her husband. A boundary the tool would never have guessed on its own.

That's the whole method. Not a formula to memorize, just a habit: before you hit send, picture explaining the same thing to a person standing next to you, and notice what you'd naturally add that you almost left out of the typed version.

## You don't need a perfect first try

It's worth saying clearly: Linda didn't get the good version on her first attempt, and that's completely normal, not a sign she did it wrong. The first, vague question wasn't a failure. It was a first draft, the same as anyone's first draft of anything. The fix wasn't starting over. It was adding the three things she already knew and just hadn't said yet.

This matters because a lot of people quietly decide, after one generic answer, that the tool "doesn't really work for this." Usually what happened is closer to Linda's first try: a real question, just a smaller one than the real question in her head. One more sentence, and the same tool answered the actual question instead.

## Where this stops helping

Being specific isn't a magic trick that fixes everything, and it's worth saying where its limits are before moving on. If you genuinely don't know what you want yet, "help me figure out what I want" is itself a fine, honest place to start, and a good back-and-forth conversation can help you get more specific as you go. You don't need to arrive with a perfect, fully-loaded question. You just need to notice, a few messages in, whether you're still being vague about something you actually do know, and fill that in when you notice it.

And no amount of specificity turns a wrong answer into a right one. Chapter eight is entirely about catching the times an AI tool states something confidently that just isn't true, which happens regardless of how well you asked. Specificity gets you a much better answer. It doesn't get you a guaranteed one, and the next few chapters build the habit of checking, not just asking better.

[TAKEAWAYS]

- A generic answer almost always means a small question, not a bad tool. The AI can only use what you actually typed, not what you were thinking.
- Add the three things you already know: who or what it's actually for, what really matters here, and anything it should avoid or watch out for.
- Your first try doesn't need to be perfect. A vague first question is a normal first draft, not a mistake, fixable with one more sentence.
- Being specific gets you a much more useful answer. It doesn't guarantee a correct one, that's a separate skill covered later in this book.

[/TAKEAWAYS]
