# You Don't Need to Be a Tech Person

Linda sat at her kitchen table with her phone propped against the fruit bowl. A cursor blinked in an empty text box. Her granddaughter had set the app up for her the week before, typed in a cheerful example question to show how it worked, and left for college the next morning. Linda had opened the app four times since then. She had typed nothing four times since then.

What she wanted to ask was small. A letter had come from her insurance company, three pages of numbers and terms she didn't recognize, and she wanted to know what it actually meant before she called anyone. But every time she put her thumbs on the screen, the same thought stopped her: what if she asked it wrong. What if there was a correct way to do this that everyone else already knew, and typing the wrong way would somehow show.

Linda isn't unusual, and neither is the week she spent avoiding that blank box. She's just a little behind a curve that's moving faster than most people realize.

There is no wrong way to ask. That single fact would have saved Linda a week of a blank text box, and it's the reason this book exists.

## Where the fear actually comes from

Nobody sits down at a landline and worries about dialing it wrong. Nobody hesitates before asking a librarian a question because they're not sure they're "a library person." The hesitation Linda felt at her kitchen table isn't really about the technology. It's about a specific, learned fear: that technology comes with hidden rules, that everyone else got the manual, and that using it wrong will expose something embarrassing about you.

That fear has a real source. A lot of software actually does punish the wrong click. Menus hide the feature you need three folders deep. One wrong setting breaks something else. Years of that experience teaches a completely reasonable lesson: be careful, or you'll make a mess you can't undo.

That lesson doesn't apply here, and it's worth saying exactly why. Typing a question to AI isn't like navigating a settings menu. There's no wrong folder. There's no setting to break. The worst thing that can happen is the answer isn't very good, in which case you say so and ask again, the same way you'd tell a friend "no, that's not quite what I meant" and keep talking. That's not a mistake. That's most of how it's supposed to work.

## What Linda actually typed

Eventually, on the fifth try, Linda typed this: "I got a letter from my insurance company and I don't understand it. Can I show you what it says and you explain it in plain English?"

That's it. Not a special format. No technical terms. She was worried about doing it wrong and what she did was type the exact question she would have asked a friend who worked in insurance, if she happened to have one sitting at her kitchen table. She pasted in a few lines from the letter. The explanation that came back wasn't perfect. One term it defined a little too generally for her specific policy, and she had to ask a follow-up to pin it down. But within ten minutes she understood the letter well enough to know she didn't actually need to call anyone. The thing she'd been dreading for a week took less time than making the tea she'd been drinking while she dreaded it.

Nothing about that exchange required Linda to know anything about how the technology works. She didn't need to know what a model is, or a token, or a prompt. She needed to know how to describe what she wanted, the same skill she already uses every time she asks a pharmacist a question or explains a problem to a mechanic. That skill was never missing. It was just never pointed at this before.

## Why "not a tech person" is the wrong test

Somewhere along the way, being comfortable with new technology got treated as a personality trait. Some people "are" tech people. Some people "aren't." It sounds like a fact about you, permanent and a little embarrassing to admit, like being bad at directions.

It isn't a trait. It's exposure. The people who look effortlessly comfortable with a new app aren't smarter or more naturally wired for it, they've just failed at using ten other apps before this one and shrugged it off each time instead of deciding it meant something about them. That's the entire difference. Not aptitude. Just fewer bad experiences logged as evidence against themselves.

This book asks you to borrow that shrug, on purpose, starting now. The first few questions you ask might come back a little off, the way Linda's first answer was slightly too general. That's not a sign you did it wrong. It's the completely normal first round of a conversation, and every chapter after this one exists to make those rounds shorter and the answers more useful, one ordinary task at a time.

## What this book will not do

A book that promises this will solve everything isn't a book you should trust, so here's the honest limit before you read any further.

This book will not replace a doctor, a lawyer, or an accountant, and any chapter that touches health, legal questions, or money says so again in its own words, not just here. What it will do is help you walk into those appointments and conversations with better questions and a clearer sense of what's actually being asked of you, which is a real and different thing from replacing the professional in the room.

It also won't make a confusing situation simple if the situation is genuinely complicated. Some insurance letters really are that complicated, some family situations really do need a human who knows your specific history. What changes is that you'll have a first pass at understanding before you spend money or a favor on someone else's time, not instead of it.

And it won't remove every ounce of the hesitation Linda felt at that kitchen table. New things feel new for a while no matter how many times someone tells you they're simple. What it will do is give you enough small wins early that the hesitation gets quieter each time, the same way it did for Linda somewhere around her third real conversation.

[TAKEAWAYS]

- There is no wrong way to ask. The worst outcome is an answer that isn't quite right, which you fix by saying so.
- "Not a tech person" describes fewer good experiences, not a fixed trait. It changes with practice, not with a personality change.
- You already have the underlying skill: describing what you want in plain language. It just hasn't been pointed at this before.
- This book doesn't replace a doctor, lawyer, or accountant. It helps you walk into those conversations better prepared.

## Where this goes next

Chapter two slows down and looks closely at exactly what Linda typed, and the one habit underneath it that makes every other chapter in this book work: being specific about what you actually want, the way you'd be specific with a person and not a search box. Three through seven each take on one ordinary task, a trip, a difficult letter, a kid's homework, a doctor's appointment, a confusing bill, in enough detail that you can do the real version the same week you read it. Eight is about the times it gets something wrong, which will happen, and exactly how to catch it. Nine is about staying safe, because the same technology that helps you also gets used against people, especially people who are new to it, and that chapter doesn't flinch from saying so plainly. Ten is the last one, and it's not really about AI at all. It's about becoming the person your own family calls, the way you might already call someone else, and handing this book to them when they ask how you learned.
