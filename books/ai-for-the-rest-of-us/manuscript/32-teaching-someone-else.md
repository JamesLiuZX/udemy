# Teaching Someone Else

Linda's sister Rose called on a Tuesday, the way she does every couple of weeks, and somewhere in the middle of the usual catching-up she mentioned, almost apologetically, that her doctor's office had sent a confusing letter about a billing change and she didn't understand it and didn't want to bother anyone about it. Six months earlier, Linda would have said "oh, that sounds annoying" and moved on, because six months earlier Linda was the one avoiding a blank text box at her own kitchen table.

Instead she said, "Send me a picture of it. I'll walk you through what I do." Not "I'll figure it out for you." Walk you through. She talked Rose through pasting the letter's confusing sentence into a chat window and asking for a plain-English explanation, the exact habit from chapter one, and stayed on the phone while Rose typed her own version of the question, in her own words, and read her own answer back. Twenty minutes later Rose understood the letter, and something else had happened alongside that: Rose had done it herself, with Linda narrating rather than doing it for her, and the blank box that had intimidated her a week earlier didn't feel like a locked door anymore.

## The actual shape of this chapter

This isn't a chapter about a new skill. Every skill in this book has already been covered. This is a chapter about a shift that happens naturally, usually without anyone planning it: at some point, a few real wins into using these tools, you stop being the person who needed the fear talked down and start being the person someone else calls when they're stuck at their own kitchen table. Linda's granddaughter set up the app for her back in chapter one, then went back to college the next morning, and Linda spent a week alone with a blinking cursor before she found the first sentence. This chapter is about not leaving Rose alone with hers.

[KEY-INSIGHT: Among U.S. adults 65 and older, only 23 percent use AI chatbots, compared with 63 percent of adults under 50, a 40-point gap. Roughly three-quarters of adults 65 and older say they never use chatbots at all. That gap doesn't close itself. It closes one kitchen-table conversation at a time, the way most real technology adoption in a family always has. || Source: Pew Research Center, "How Americans' opinions and use of AI differ by age," June 2026.]

That gap is this book's whole reason for existing, stated as a number instead of a story. Somebody has to be the one who sits down next to Rose, or Linda, or whoever the Rose is in your own family, and narrates instead of doing it for them. This chapter is about how to do that well, because "well" and "badly" produce very different outcomes from the exact same good intention.

[PULLQUOTE: You are now the person who can make that call.]

## The mistake that undoes all of it

The single most common way this goes wrong is the most well-meaning one: taking the phone or the keyboard and just doing it for them, because it's faster, because you can see they're struggling, because it's genuinely quicker to type the question yourself than to walk someone through typing it. It is faster. It's also exactly Linda's granddaughter's original mistake, six months earlier, the one that left Linda alone with a blank box for a week. Doing it for someone teaches them that the task is something only you can do. Walking them through it teaches them the task is something they can do. Those produce completely different people six months later.

The fix is almost annoyingly simple: whoever has the problem holds the phone. You can look over their shoulder, suggest the words, catch a typo, but their thumbs do the typing. It takes longer in the moment. It's the only version that actually transfers anything.

## Marisol's version: the same fix, aimed backward

Chapter nine's script gave Marisol a way to talk to her mother about scams. What came after that first conversation surprised her: her mother, once the immediate fear of the scam call was addressed, got curious about the rest of it, what else this thing could actually help with. Marisol found herself doing with her own mother exactly what Linda did with Rose, minutes at a time on video calls, letting her mother hold her own phone and type her own words while Marisol narrated. Her mother's first real, unprompted use of it, weeks later and entirely on her own, was working out a substitution for an ingredient she didn't have for a recipe she'd made a hundred times without ever needing help before. Small. Also entirely hers, unprompted, and that's the actual measure of whether the teaching worked.

## What to actually say the first time

If you're looking for a place to start with your own Rose, here's a version worth borrowing, close to what actually worked for both Linda and Marisol: "Show me something you've been wanting to ask or figure out, doesn't matter how small. Let's type it together, you hold the phone, I'll just help if you get stuck." Small and low-stakes on purpose, the same instinct as chapter three's advice to start with dinner, not a trip: the goal of a first lesson is one real win, not a comprehensive tour.

## The book's actual last argument

Back near the start, this book opened by saying the hesitation at Linda's kitchen table wasn't really about the technology, it was a learned fear that everyone else already had the manual. That fear moves through families exactly like this: someone who's never been shown assumes everyone else already knows, stays quiet about it, and the gap gets treated as a permanent fact about who they are instead of what it actually is, a temporary gap in exposure that one twenty-minute phone call closes. You are now the person who can make that call. That was always the actual point of everything before this page.
