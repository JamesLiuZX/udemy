# The Toolkit

This chapter has no story in it. It's the reference page this book has been quietly promising all along: every exact prompt and template from every chapter, collected in one place, so you never have to flip back through thirty-three chapters hunting for the one sentence you actually needed. Photocopy it, screenshot it, tear these pages out if this is a physical copy. It's meant to be used, not admired.

Every template below follows the same underlying rule, chapter two's rule, restated one final time because it's the one thing worth remembering if you remember nothing else: be specific about who it's for, what actually matters, and what to avoid or watch out for, the way you'd be specific with a person standing next to you and not a search box. Fill in the brackets with your own real situation. The words around the brackets are a starting point, not a script to recite exactly.

## The one sentence underneath everything (Chapter 2)

"I need [specific thing], for [who it's actually for / the real situation], and it needs to [what actually matters here], but not [what to avoid]."

## Planning something (Chapter 3)

"We want [a trip / an event] for [timeframe], [key details: number of people, budget, dates]. We care about [what actually matters to you]. Give me [2-4] options and why each one fits."

## The stuck letter (Chapter 4)

Before typing anything: who is this going to, what outcome do you actually want, and what tone are you going for. Then: "I need to write a [firm/formal/warm] message about [situation, with dates and facts in order]. I want [the specific outcome]. Write it [tone], and end with [a specific next step or deadline]."

## The homework tutor (Chapter 5)

"My [age]-year-old is working on [subject/assignment]. Don't give the answer directly. Ask one question at a time to help them figure it out themselves, and adjust the difficulty based on how they respond."

## Before a doctor's appointment (Chapter 6)

"I have an appointment about [symptom or finding, described specifically]. Help me understand what I've described in plain English if there's anything confusing about it, and give me [4-5] specific questions to make sure I ask so I don't leave more confused than I came in."

## A plain-English money question (Chapter 7)

"Explain [the specific term or document] like I've never seen this before. Then help me think through [the specific decision or comparison], not by telling me what to pick, but by telling me what actually matters here."

## The two-question check, before you act on anything (Chapter 8)

Ask yourself, not the AI tool: What's the one fact in this answer that, if wrong, would actually matter? Have I checked that one fact anywhere else? If you can't answer both honestly, check before you act, not after.

## The scam-safety rule (Chapter 9)

If anyone calls, texts, or emails asking for money, gift cards, or account information urgently, especially a family emergency: hang up or stop replying, then contact that person directly through a number or method you already know and trust, never the number that contacted you. Agree on a family password in advance for genuine emergencies.

## Setting up your tool, and talking instead of typing (Chapter 10)

Download one well-known free app. Find the privacy setting (usually under Settings, Privacy, or Data Controls) and turn off training use if it bothers you. Fill in the "memory" or "custom instructions" setting once with a few standing facts about your situation. Look for a microphone or waveform icon near the message box for voice typing or a full spoken conversation. Search "voice typing" or "voice mode" in your phone's settings if neither is obvious.

## A big purchase (Chapter 11)

"I need to buy [item] within [timeframe], budget around [amount], mainly for [how you'll actually use it]. What actually matters for my situation, what's just marketing, and what do people commonly get wrong buying this kind of thing?" Then verify the finalists yourself: recent reviews, actual current price, any relevant recall.

## The words you can't find (Chapter 12)

Start with three specific, true, unpolished things: a memory, a detail only you would know, what you actually feel, stated plainly. Then: "Help me find the shape connecting these three things into something I could actually say out loud or send." Rewrite anything that doesn't sound like you.

## The weekly reset (Chapter 13)

"Here's what's actually on my plate this week: [list it messily]. Help me sort this into what actually needs to happen this week versus what can wait, and suggest an order to tackle it in."

## The ordinary emergency (Chapter 14)

State the situation, the constraint (a clock, what you have access to), and ask for the next step: "[Situation], I have [time constraint / what you have]. What should I do right now?" For an actual medical emergency, call emergency services first, always.

## When someone you love won't try (Chapter 15)

Stop pitching it as a topic. Use it visibly in your own life. When they ask, answer only the specific question in front of them and stop there. If the door never opens, being the person they can still call is a real, acceptable outcome.

## The job search, translate-don't-invent (Chapter 16)

"Here's my résumé and the job posting I'm applying for. Rewrite my experience to speak directly to what this posting asks for, using only things that are actually true about my background. Then help me draft a short cover letter in a genuine, non-generic voice." Apply the interview-room test to every line before sending: could you defend this claim, in detail, to someone's face?

## The fine print, five things (Chapter 17)

"This is a [lease/contract/agreement] I'm considering. In plain English, what are the five most important things I should understand, especially anything unusual, before I sign?"

## The exact-symptom tech fix (Chapter 18)

"My [device/app] was working fine until [what changed]. Now [exactly what happens, word for word if there's an error message]. What's the most likely cause and what should I try first?"

## Something just for you (Chapter 19)

"I've never [tried this] before and want to learn just for fun, no pressure. What's the minimum I need to get started, and walk me through one simple first attempt I could try this week."

## The plan-comparison prompt (Chapter 20)

"I'm comparing [insurance/Medicare plan options]. My situation is [honest specifics: age, health needs, current plan]. In plain English, what's the real difference between these options, and what questions should I bring to [a counselor / HR / the plan's help line] to actually decide?"

## A big gathering's timeline (Chapter 21)

"I'm hosting [event] for [number] people, including [any dietary restrictions]. Help me build a full timeline for the day, what to cook or do when so everything's ready together, and a shopping or supply list organized by what needs to happen ahead versus at the last minute."

## The caregiving organizer (Chapter 22)

"I'm helping care for [family member]. Here's the current situation: [medications, appointments, or concerns, described honestly]. Help me organize this into a clear, shareable [schedule / summary / calendar] I can use myself and share with other family members helping out."

## The financial aid process-orientation prompt (Chapter 23)

"We're starting to think about college and financial aid for [student's grade level]. In plain English, what's the realistic timeline, what do the major terms mean, and what should we actually be doing now versus later?"

## The natural-translation prompt (Chapter 24)

"Translate this explanation into natural, conversational [language], the way you'd actually explain it to someone, not a stiff, literal translation." For anything with real legal or medical weight, treat the result as a starting point and ask for a professional human interpreter.

## The pet-symptom prompt (Chapter 25)

"My [pet, age, general health] has [symptom, how long, anything else notable]. I have/don't have a vet appointment [timeframe]. Is this the kind of thing worth an earlier appointment, and what questions should I bring?" For anything that reads as a possible emergency, call an emergency vet line directly instead.

## The paycheck and withholding prompt (Chapter 26)

"Here's my pay stub, explain each line in plain English." Or: "I got a raise and my tax refund was unusually large this year. In plain English, what does a W-4 form actually control, and what questions should I answer for myself before adjusting it?"

## The plain-English estate-planning prompt (Chapter 27)

"I'm [your situation: age, family structure, whether you have existing documents]. In plain English, what's the difference between a will, a healthcare directive, and [other relevant document], and what personal decisions should I think through before meeting with a lawyer?"

## The repair-estimate prompt (Chapter 28)

"My mechanic gave me this estimate for [the symptom, described specifically]: [list the actual line items]. In plain English, what do these mean, does this fix match my symptom, and is this price reasonable?"

## The bare-bones-budget prompt (Chapter 29)

"I was just laid off, [notice period], [number of dependents], [rough monthly expenses]. In plain English, help me build a bare-bones budget for the next three months assuming no income, and walk me through what unemployment benefits typically cover and how to apply in [your state]."

## Teaching someone else, the first lesson (Chapter 30)

"Show me something you've been wanting to ask or figure out, doesn't matter how small. Let's type it together, you hold the phone, I'll just help if you get stuck." Whoever has the problem holds the phone and does the typing, every time.

## The three questions to ask yourself before any of the above

Regardless of which template you reach for, these three questions, first named in chapter two, sit underneath every one of them:

- Who is this actually for, or what's the real situation?
- What actually matters here, stated as a specific goal?
- What should it avoid, or what boundary matters?

Answer those three honestly, in your own words, before you type anything, and most of what makes a request vague instead of useful disappears on its own.
