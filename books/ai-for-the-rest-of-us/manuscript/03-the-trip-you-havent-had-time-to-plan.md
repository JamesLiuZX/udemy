# The Trip You Haven't Had Time to Plan

Linda's daughter had been asking for two months: when are you and Dad finally taking that anniversary trip you keep talking about. Linda kept saying soon. What she meant was that she'd opened a travel site four different times, gotten overwhelmed by a wall of hotel options she had no way to compare, and closed the tab each time without booking anything. The trip wasn't stalled because she didn't want to go. It was stalled because planning it felt like a part-time job she hadn't signed up for.

[KEY-INSIGHT: Research from Priceline found the average traveler spends the equivalent of two full working days, around sixteen hours, researching and booking a single trip. Other surveys put the number even higher once comparison shopping and reading reviews are included. || Source: Priceline Press Center, "New Priceline Research Finds Average Traveler Spends Two Full Work Days To Plan and Book Trips."]

Sixteen hours is a lot to ask of anyone's evenings, and it explains exactly why Linda's tab kept closing. This chapter isn't about replacing the trip itself, the choosing of where to go and what matters to you about it. It's about the sixteen hours of comparing, cross-referencing, and re-reading the same five hotel descriptions that stand between "we should go" and an actual booking.

## What Linda actually did

She started smaller than a whole trip. One evening, she typed: "We want to go somewhere for our anniversary in October, five nights, warm weather, budget around three thousand dollars for two people including flights from Chicago. We like walking around a city more than sitting on a beach all day, and we don't want anywhere that requires a long layover. Give me three destination options and why each one fits."

That single message did the job of the first several hours of research, the part that isn't really about any one hotel, it's about narrowing an entire world of possible trips down to three real options worth actually comparing. She picked one of the three, then went back for the next layer: "For [the city she picked], find us a hotel in walking distance of the old town, under two hundred a night, with real recent reviews mentioning it's quiet at night." Another few minutes, another few hours of scrolling avoided.

[PULLQUOTE: The sixteen hours were never really about any one hotel. They were about narrowing an entire world of options down to a few worth actually comparing.]

## Why this works the same way chapter two described

Notice Linda's message wasn't just "plan our anniversary trip," the vague version that would have produced the same generic overwhelm she was already drowning in. It had the three things from chapter two: who it's for (two people, a specific budget), what actually matters (walkable city, not a long layover), and what to avoid (long layovers, beach-only destinations). The trip-planning version of a good question isn't a new skill. It's the same one sentence habit, aimed at a bigger, messier decision than dinner.

## What to still do yourself

This chapter will not tell you to book a hotel sight unseen because a chatbot recommended it, and it's worth saying exactly where the line sits. Use it to narrow a wall of hundreds of options down to a short, sensible list. Then do what you'd always do with a short list: look at real photos, check the reviews yourself on a site you trust, confirm the actual price on the booking site directly before you pay anything. The tool is excellent at cutting sixteen hours of undifferentiated searching down to twenty minutes of focused comparing. It's not a replacement for your own eyes on the final choice, and treating it as one is exactly the kind of over-trust chapter eight covers in detail.

And never hand over a credit card number or personal details inside the chat itself. Use it to research and narrow down, then go to the actual airline, hotel, or travel site to book and pay. Chapter nine goes further into why that boundary matters, but it's worth planting here, on the first task in this book that involves real money.

## Why this isn't cheating or lazy

If narrowing a big decision down with a few typed sentences feels like it should require more effort to count as "real" planning, that instinct is worth questioning directly. Nobody thinks asking a knowledgeable friend "where should we go for a week in October, we like walking around cities" is lazy. It's exactly what people have always done, they just usually didn't have a friend who happened to know hundreds of cities' walkability and weather patterns off the top of their head. The travel agent industry existed for exactly this reason for decades before anyone had a chat window. This is that same help, available on a Tuesday night at 9 p.m. instead of during a travel agency's business hours.

[TAKEAWAYS]

- Planning a single trip takes the average traveler around sixteen hours. Most of that time is spent narrowing an overwhelming set of options, not deciding between two final choices.
- The same one-sentence habit from chapter two applies here: who it's for, what matters, what to avoid, aimed at a trip instead of a dinner.
- Use the tool to narrow hundreds of options to a short list. Do the final check yourself: real photos, reviews on a trusted site, the actual price on the actual booking site.
- Never enter payment details inside the chat itself. Research and narrow there, then book and pay on the real airline, hotel, or travel site directly.
