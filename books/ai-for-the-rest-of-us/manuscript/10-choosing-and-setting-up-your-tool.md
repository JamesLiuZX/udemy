# Choosing and Setting Up Your Tool

This book has quietly assumed something for nine chapters: that you already have some AI chat app open on your phone or computer. For Linda, her granddaughter set it up before leaving for college. For Marisol, a coworker mentioned an app one lunch break and she downloaded it that afternoon without thinking much about it. Neither of them chose their tool the way you'd choose a car or a phone plan, weighing options. They just started with whatever landed in front of them, and it worked out fine, which is itself worth knowing before this chapter gets into any specifics: the tool matters much less than the habits this book has spent nine chapters building. Still, if you're starting from nothing, or wondering whether the one you have is the right one, this chapter is the plain-English version of that decision, with no seventeen-tab comparison chart.

## The short answer, if you want to stop reading here

Download one well-known AI chat app, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Copilot are the four you'll see mentioned most, made by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft respectively, use the free version, and start with chapter two's one-sentence habit on something small. That single sentence is 90 percent of what this chapter has to tell you. The rest is for the specific questions that come up once you've started.

## The four, honestly compared

None of these four requires a card on file to try, and every one of them changes its exact free-plan numbers often enough that any figure printed here will drift, so treat what follows as the shape of the honest trade-off, not a number to memorize. What's stable enough to rely on:

**ChatGPT** (OpenAI) has the largest free tier of the four and, as of this writing, gives free accounts unlimited ordinary text conversation, with separate, smaller daily limits on things like image uploads and generated pictures. It's the one most people have already heard of, which makes it a reasonable default if you have no other reason to pick.

**Claude** (Anthropic) caps free use by a rolling daily message count rather than by feature, and is frequently noted for producing longer, more carefully organized answers on the free tier, useful for the kind of multi-step help this book leans on, an appointment-prep list, a full trip itinerary. The tighter daily cap is the real trade-off against ChatGPT's more generous one.

**Gemini** (Google) signs in with the same account as your email if you already use Gmail, which is a genuine convenience, not a marketing point, and integrates directly with Google Docs, Sheets, and Drive if you use those. Its free tier is generous for ordinary chat.

**Copilot** (Microsoft) is the only one of the four that works with no account or sign-in at all, at copilot.microsoft.com or built into some Windows computers already. That makes it the fastest way to try this book's habits on a borrowed or public computer, with the trade-off that skipping sign-in also means no memory of past conversations between sessions.

[KEY-INSIGHT: All four major AI chat tools offer a genuinely usable free tier as of 2026, not a crippled trial: unlimited or near-unlimited text conversation is the current norm rather than the exception it was a few years earlier. Every specific limit named above is the kind of detail that changes with the next product update, on all four tools, without warning. || Source: verified against each provider's own current pricing and help documentation at writing time; re-verify before relying on a specific number, not just the general shape.]

None of that is a strong enough difference to lose sleep over. If you already have one because a family member set it up, or your phone came with one built in, that's a perfectly good reason to just use that one rather than researching further. Pick one, use it for a month, switch if something about it genuinely bothers you.

## Free versus paid, in plain terms

Every major AI chat app has a free version, and for everything this book covers, a trip, a letter, homework help, appointment prep, a budget question, the free version is genuinely enough. The paid versions, usually $20 a month, mostly buy you faster responses, higher usage limits if you're using it heavily every day, and access to the company's newest, most capable model a little sooner. None of that changes whether the tool can help Linda write a complaint letter or help Marisol understand a pay stub. Start free. If you find yourself hitting a daily limit because you're genuinely using it constantly, that's the signal to consider paying, not a guess made in advance.

## What to actually check before you type anything personal into it

This is the one piece of setup worth taking seriously, not skipping past: before you paste anything sensitive, a full account number, a medical record number, a child's full name and school, into any AI chat tool, know two things. First, most of these tools, by default, may use what you type to improve their systems, which is not the same as a stranger reading it, but is also not the same as a private conversation with a person who forgets it the next day. Second, every major tool has a setting, usually under "Settings," "Privacy," or "Data Controls," to turn this off. Take five minutes the first time you set up any of these tools to find that setting and turn it off if it bothers you. It doesn't cost anything and it doesn't make the tool work any worse.

None of the tasks in this book require typing in a full account number or a full legal name and address together. Chapter three's trip-planning example never needed a real credit card number, because the tool only narrows options, you book on the real airline or hotel site. Keep that same instinct everywhere: type what a helpful stranger would need to help you, not what only your bank needs to know.

## Setting it up so it remembers what matters

Most of these tools have a "memory" or "custom instructions" feature, a place where you can tell it a few standing facts about yourself once instead of repeating them every time: your general location, that you prefer short, plain-English answers, that you have two kids of a certain age if homework help is a regular thing. Find that setting once, spend five minutes filling it in, and every future conversation starts a little further ahead. This is the single most useful five minutes of setup in this whole chapter, and it's the one people skip most often because it looks like an optional extra rather than the actual time-saver it is.

## Talking instead of typing

Linda's sister Rose has arthritis in both thumbs, the kind that makes a full afternoon of typing feel like a chore before it's even a task. When Linda taught her the letter-writing habit from chapter four, she watched Rose wince at the third sentence and finally said, "Why are you typing all this? Just talk to it." Rose looked at her like she'd suggested something faintly ridiculous. Then she tried it, out loud, describing the mattress-company problem the way she'd describe it to a friend on the phone, and the app turned her spoken words into text on the screen as fast as she could talk, no thumbs involved at all.

A real number of people reading this book will find that talking is not a workaround for typing. It's the better version, full stop, and nobody tells you that up front because typing is what the demos always show. Stiff fingers and hand fatigue are the obvious reasons, but they're not the only ones: plenty of people simply talk faster than they type, small phone screens make typing genuinely harder for anyone, and some of the tasks in this book, describing a health symptom, working through a hard message, come out more naturally spoken than typed.

[KEY-INSIGHT: A controlled usability study comparing five ways of getting text into a phone found that older adults (60 to 84) typed noticeably slower than younger adults on a touchscreen keyboard, tracing, and handwriting, but the two groups performed equally fast and equally accurately using voice input. Both age groups also rated voice among their most preferred methods, on par with a physical keyboard. || Source: Amanda L. Smith and Barbara S. Chaparro, "Smartphone Text Input Method Performance, Usability, and Preference With Younger and Older Adults," Human Factors, 2015.]

All four tools from earlier in this chapter now offer a genuine spoken conversation mode, not just voice-to-text, one where you talk and the app talks back, hands and eyes free the whole time: look for a waveform or headphone icon near the message box, separate from the keyboard's own microphone key, usually labeled "Voice" or "Live." Every phone's own keyboard also has a plain voice-typing microphone, present system-wide on both iPhone and Android, that turns speech into text you can review before sending, a smaller step than a full voice conversation but available everywhere typing is, not just inside an AI app.

Nothing about chapter two's core habit changes when you talk instead of type, but talking naturally pulls you toward doing it well without trying. Rose's spoken version of her mattress complaint came out as a full, natural account, dates and all, because that's how people talk when they're explaining a real problem to another person.

Voice input struggles in a loud room or anywhere with real background noise, and it occasionally mishears a name or unusual word, worth a two-second glance before sending rather than assuming it heard you perfectly. Neither is a reason to abandon it. The other awkwardness is purely social: talking to your phone out loud in a waiting room can feel exposing in a way typing doesn't. There's no fix for that beyond permission to skip it in public and save it for somewhere private.

## If you're setting this up for someone else

If you're the family member doing what Linda's granddaughter did, here's the one adjustment worth making that she didn't: don't just install the app and type one example question to show it works. Sit down with the actual person for their first real question, the way chapter thirty describes doing it properly, later in this book. A single demo question proves the app opens. It doesn't teach anyone how to use it, and Linda spent a week alone with a blank box because a demo question was all she got.

## Try this

Whichever of the four you land on, the next time you catch yourself avoiding a task in this book because typing it out feels like too much, tap the microphone instead and just talk through it the way you'd explain it to a person standing next to you.

[TAKEAWAYS]

- One well-known free app is enough to start. The specific choice among ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Copilot matters far less than actually using it, and each has one honest, stable trade-off worth knowing rather than a specific number worth memorizing.
- Paid tiers buy speed and higher limits, not better help with the tasks in this book. Start free; upgrade only if you're genuinely hitting a daily limit.
- Find the privacy setting that controls whether your conversations train the system, and turn it off if it bothers you. Fill in the "memory" or "custom instructions" setting once; it's the highest-value five minutes of setup most people skip.
- Voice input isn't a fallback for people who struggle with typing. Research shows people of all ages rate it among their most preferred ways to get text into a device. All four tools now offer a full spoken conversation mode, not just voice-to-text.
