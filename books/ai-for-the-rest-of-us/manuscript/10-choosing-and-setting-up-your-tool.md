# Choosing and Setting Up Your Tool

This book has quietly assumed something for nine chapters: that you already have some AI chat app open on your phone or computer. For Linda, her granddaughter set it up before leaving for college. For Marisol, a coworker mentioned an app one lunch break and she downloaded it that afternoon without thinking much about it. Neither of them chose their tool the way you'd choose a car or a phone plan, weighing options. They just started with whatever landed in front of them, and it worked out fine, which is itself worth knowing before this chapter gets into any specifics: the tool matters much less than the habits this book has spent nine chapters building. Still, if you're starting from nothing, or wondering whether the one you have is the right one, this chapter is the plain-English version of that decision, with no seventeen-tab comparison chart.

## The short answer, if you want to stop reading here

Download one well-known AI chat app (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Copilot are the four you'll see mentioned most, made by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft respectively), use the free version, and start with chapter two's one-sentence habit on something small. That single sentence is 90 percent of what this chapter has to tell you. The rest is for the specific questions that come up once you've started.

## Free versus paid, in plain terms

Every major AI chat app has a free version, and for everything this book covers, a trip, a letter, homework help, appointment prep, a budget question, the free version is genuinely enough. The paid versions, usually $20 a month, mostly buy you faster responses, higher usage limits if you're using it heavily every day, and access to the company's newest, most capable model a little sooner. None of that changes whether the tool can help Linda write a complaint letter or help Marisol understand a pay stub. Start free. If you find yourself hitting a daily limit because you're genuinely using it constantly, that's the signal to consider paying, not a guess made in advance.

## Does it matter which one?

Less than the marketing around all four would have you believe. They're more alike than different for the kind of plain-language help this book is about: explaining a confusing letter, drafting a firm but polite complaint, walking through a symptom list before an appointment. If you already have one because a family member set it up, or your phone came with one built in, that's a perfectly good reason to just use that one rather than researching further. If you're choosing cold, ChatGPT and Gemini are the two most widely used and have the largest free tiers; Claude is often mentioned for writing that sounds a little less robotic; Copilot comes built into some Windows computers already. None of that is a strong enough difference to lose sleep over. Pick one, use it for a month, switch if something about it genuinely bothers you.

## What to actually check before you type anything personal into it

This is the one piece of setup worth taking seriously, not skipping past: before you paste anything sensitive, a full account number, a medical record number, a child's full name and school, into any AI chat tool, know two things. First, most of these tools, by default, may use what you type to improve their systems, which is not the same as a stranger reading it, but is also not the same as a private conversation with a person who forgets it the next day. Second, every major tool has a setting, usually under "Settings," "Privacy," or "Data Controls," to turn this off. Take five minutes the first time you set up any of these tools to find that setting and turn it off if it bothers you. It doesn't cost anything and it doesn't make the tool work any worse.

None of the tasks in this book require typing in a full account number or a full legal name and address together. Chapter three's trip-planning example never needed a real credit card number, because the tool only narrows options, you book on the real airline or hotel site. Keep that same instinct everywhere: type what a helpful stranger would need to help you, not what only your bank needs to know.

## Setting it up so it remembers what matters

Most of these tools have a "memory" or "custom instructions" feature, a place where you can tell it a few standing facts about yourself once instead of repeating them every time: your general location, that you prefer short, plain-English answers, that you have two kids of a certain age if homework help is a regular thing. Find that setting once, spend five minutes filling it in, and every future conversation starts a little further ahead. This is the single most useful five minutes of setup in this whole chapter, and it's the one people skip most often because it looks like an optional extra rather than the actual time-saver it is.

## If you're setting this up for someone else

If you're the family member doing what Linda's granddaughter did, here's the one adjustment worth making that she didn't: don't just install the app and type one example question to show it works. Sit down with the actual person for their first real question, the way chapter thirty-two describes doing it properly, later in this book. A single demo question proves the app opens.
