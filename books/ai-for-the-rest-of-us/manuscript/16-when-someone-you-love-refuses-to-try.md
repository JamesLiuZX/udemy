# When Someone You Love Refuses to Try

Hal wanted no part of any of it. Linda had been using her new habit for months by the time she tried to show him the letter-writing trick from chapter four, hoping to save him the same hour of drafting and redrafting he usually spent on anything official. He waved her off before she'd finished the sentence. "I've written letters my whole life without a computer telling me what to say," he said, not unkindly, just final, the tone of a decision that had already been made a while ago and wasn't up for a rerun. Linda tried twice more over the following month, each time a little more insistent, and each time Hal got a little more dug in. By the third try, it wasn't really about the letter anymore. It was about Linda pushing and Hal resisting, a pattern with a life of its own separate from the actual technology.

This chapter is about that pattern, because it's common, and because pushing harder, the thing that feels most natural when you're certain you're right and certain it would help, is almost always the exact move that makes it worse.

## What's actually happening when someone refuses

It's rarely about the technology itself. Hal's specific words, "I've written letters my whole life," were doing something underneath their literal meaning: protecting a piece of identity, competence with words, that he wasn't willing to hand over to something that might do it faster or better. Refusing to try isn't usually stubbornness for its own sake. It's much more often a reasonable-feeling defense of a specific fear: that trying and needing help would confirm something the person doesn't want confirmed about themselves, being behind, being old, being less capable than they used to be. Understanding that changes what the right response looks like, because you're not actually arguing about a chat window. You're bumping into something much more personal, and treating it like a simple information gap, "let me just show you how easy it is," almost always misses.

[KEY-INSIGHT: A 2026 scoping review of digital health technology adoption in older adults found a genuinely two-sided effect from family help: guidance from younger relatives can reduce a person's anxiety about a new technology, but when that help is rushed or delivered impatiently, it does the opposite, increasing anxiety and undermining the person's own sense that the technology is something they could actually use, discouraging them from continuing at all. || Source: Birati and Tzemah-Shahar, "Barriers to Digital Health Adoption in Older Adults: Scoping Review Informed by Innovation Resistance Theory," Journal of Medical Internet Research, 2026.]

That finding cuts against the most natural instinct in the room. When someone you love is struggling or resisting, the urge is to move faster, explain more, show them one more time, more clearly. The research says that exact instinct, applied with any hint of impatience, is what closes the door rather than opening it.

## What actually worked, eventually, with Hal

Linda stopped bringing it up. Not out of defeat, out of a real change in approach: she decided to let Hal find his own way in, if he ever did, rather than continuing to make her comfort with the technology into a referendum on his. Two months later, watching her write a birthday message for their granddaughter using the method from chapter thirteen, Hal asked, unprompted, whether it could help with the toast he was dreading giving at a friend's retirement dinner. That was the actual opening, not because Linda had finally found the right way to explain it, but because Hal came to it on his own terms, at a moment it solved a problem he already had, rather than a moment Linda had decided was a good time for a lesson.

She sat with him for that one, following the exact same rule chapter thirty-two lays out properly: he held the phone, he typed his own words, she only helped when he got stuck. It wasn't a triumphant conversion. He still doesn't use it for much. But he uses it for that one thing now, because that one time, it actually helped him with something he actually needed, on a day he'd chosen, not a day chosen for him.

## What to actually do, stated plainly

Stop bringing it up as a topic. Every unsolicited pitch reinforces that this is something you think they're missing, which is exactly the confirmation they're avoiding. Instead, use it naturally, visibly, in your own life, and let curiosity do the inviting instead of persuasion. When they do ask, and many eventually do, resist the urge to make it a full lesson. Answer the specific question, solve the specific problem in front of them, and stop there. Let the next question, if it comes, come on its own schedule.

And if the door never opens at all, that has to be an acceptable outcome, not a project left permanently unfinished. Chapter thirty-two's whole argument is about becoming the person your family calls. Sometimes what someone needs isn't to be taught, it's to have someone they can still call instead, indefinitely, no pressure attached to eventually learning it themselves. That's not a failure of this book's method. It's a legitimate, permanent answer for some people, and pretending otherwise does nobody any favors.
