# Walking Into the Doctor's Office Prepared

Linda's husband Hal had a follow-up appointment after a scan came back with a word neither of them recognized: a finding on his kidney, small, probably nothing, but the kind of "probably" that keeps you up at eleven at night reading forums you shouldn't be reading. The appointment was fifteen minutes long. Linda knew from the last three appointments how fast fifteen minutes goes once a doctor starts talking, and how much of it turns into a blur the second you're in the room and scared.

She sat down the night before with the printout from the scan, the parts she could actually read, and typed: "My husband's kidney scan mentioned this finding: [she pasted the exact phrase]. We have a follow-up appointment tomorrow. Help me understand, in plain English, what this might mean and what the range of possibilities is, and give me five specific questions to ask the doctor so we don't leave the appointment more confused than we walked in." She wasn't asking for a diagnosis. She was asking for a translation and a question list, the two things a fifteen-minute appointment rarely leaves room to build from scratch in the room itself.

The explanation helped her understand the term well enough to follow the conversation instead of just nodding through it. The five questions were sharper than what she'd have thought of on her own, standing in the room, watching Hal's face. One of them, "is this something that needs monitoring on a schedule, or a one-time follow-up," turned out to be exactly the question the doctor needed to answer for them to actually understand the plan. They asked all five. They left with real answers instead of a vague sense of relief that the appointment was over.

[KEY-INSIGHT: A Brown University study that recorded 189 real doctor's visits found patients accurately recalled only 49 percent of the decisions and recommendations made, without prompting, right after the appointment ended. Prompted, that rose to 85 percent. The gap between those two numbers is almost entirely the difference between walking in with questions already written down and walking in hoping you'll remember to ask. || Source: Laws et al., "Factors associated with patient recall of key information in ambulatory specialty care visits," PLOS ONE, February 2018.]

That's not a small gap. It's the difference between an appointment that actually changes what you understand and one that mostly just happened to you.

## Where the line is, said as plainly as this book says anything

This is the chapter where the limit matters more than anywhere else in this book, so it's worth saying it three separate times in three separate ways before moving on. An AI tool is not a doctor. It has not examined your husband. It does not have his full history, his other medications, the look on his face the doctor would notice in the room. What it can do is exactly what Linda used it for: turn confusing language into plain language, and turn "I don't know what to ask" into a list of specific, good questions. What it cannot do, and should never be asked to do, is tell you what's wrong or what to do about it. That's the doctor's job, every time, no exceptions, no matter how confident or specific the AI's answer sounds.

## Marisol's version: a symptom, not a scan

Marisol had been getting headaches most afternoons for three weeks, the kind that started behind one eye and made the light in the break room feel too bright. She almost didn't book an appointment at all. It's a headache, everyone gets headaches, and taking a half day off an hourly job to talk to a doctor about a headache felt, to her, like it might be an overreaction she couldn't afford, both in time and in the awkwardness of explaining herself.

What she typed wasn't a request for a diagnosis, and she knew better than to ask for one. It was: "I've had a headache most afternoons for three weeks, starts behind my right eye, worse by evening, better after I sleep. I have an appointment Thursday. What questions should I make sure I ask so the doctor has enough to actually figure out what's going on, and is this the kind of thing worth taking time off work for?" The answer didn't diagnose anything, and it said so directly, more than once. It did help her notice a pattern she hadn't put into words yet, that the headaches seemed to track with days she'd skipped lunch, and it gave her a specific way to describe the timing that made the appointment far more useful than "I've been getting headaches" would have.

She went. It turned out to be dehydration and low blood sugar from a work schedule that didn't leave time to eat, an unglamorous answer, and also the correct one, caught faster because she walked in with a pattern already described instead of a vague complaint.

## What never to type into a symptom checker

There's a specific trap worth naming directly, because it's an easy one to fall into with exactly this kind of tool: typing a symptom in and asking "what disease is this," expecting a diagnosis back. Even when an AI tool gives you a confident, specific-sounding answer to that question, treat it the way you'd treat a stranger's guess in a waiting room, mildly interesting, not a reason to act. The honest, useful question is never "what do I have." It's "what should I ask about, and how do I describe this clearly." That reframe alone keeps you on the right side of the line every time.

## After the appointment matters too

Preparation doesn't end when the appointment does, and it's worth naming the second half of chapter six's own research: recall fades fast once the visit is over, the same forgetting curve that made preparation matter in the first place. Linda started doing one more small thing on the drive home, or right in the parking lot before pulling out: talking through what she'd just heard into a voice memo, or typing a quick summary into the same chat window, "Here's what the doctor just told us: [her rough notes]. Help me organize this into a clear summary I can reference later and share with Hal's other doctors if needed." Ten minutes, captured while it was still fresh, turned a fading memory into something durable enough to actually reference weeks later, at the next appointment, when someone inevitably asked what the specialist had said and the honest answer would otherwise have been a shrug.

## Try this: the appointment-prep prompt

Before your next appointment, big or small, try this: "I have an appointment about [symptom or finding, described specifically]. Help me understand what I've described in plain English if there's anything confusing about it, and give me [4-5] specific questions to make sure I ask so I don't leave more confused than I came in." Bring the questions on paper or your phone. Ask all of them, even the one that feels like it might be a silly question. It usually isn't.

[TAKEAWAYS]

- An AI tool cannot diagnose you and should never be asked to. What it can do well is translate confusing medical language and help you build a sharp, specific list of questions before you walk in.
- Patients forget half of what they're told in an appointment without a written list of questions to anchor the conversation. Preparation, not memory, is what makes the fifteen minutes count.
- The honest question is "what should I ask about," never "what do I have." That single reframe keeps you on the right side of the line every time.
- This is not a diagnosis, does not replace a professional, and this chapter means that every time it says it, not just here.
