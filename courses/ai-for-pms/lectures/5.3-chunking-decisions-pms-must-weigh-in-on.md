@slide callout
kicker: Section 5 · Chunking, deeper

::: callout Ask an engineer this
"What's the chunk size?" ==Real content needs at least three answers,
not one==, and a question expecting a single number has already
assumed something that isn't true.
:::

@narrate
Section two taught you the failure mode: a chunk boundary cutting
through a list or a table, producing a fragment nobody could use. This
lecture is about the decision underneath that one, the one that gets
made once, early, and quietly shapes every retrieval from then on:
how big should a chunk actually be.

There's no universal right answer. There's a trade-off, and knowing
which side of it your content needs is a genuinely product decision,
not something to leave entirely to whoever's implementing it.

Most teams never have this conversation at all. Someone picks a chunk
size from a tutorial or a default library setting, it works well
enough on the first few test queries, and it ships. That's not a
decision, it's an accident that happened to look fine in a demo. The
gap between accident and decision is exactly what this lecture closes.

@slide table
kicker: The trade-off, both directions
## Smaller and larger chunks each cost you something different

| | Smaller chunks | Larger chunks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Precision | High, retrieves the exact relevant sentence | Lower, pulls in a whole section |
| Context | Risk of losing the surrounding explanation | Keeps the full thought intact |
| Cost per query | Lower, fewer tokens retrieved | Higher, more tokens retrieved |
| Failure mode | A sentence with nothing around it to explain it | An answer diluted by irrelevant neighbours |

@narrate
Read this as a genuine trade-off, not a right answer and a wrong one.
Neither column is the safe choice.

Small chunks retrieve precisely, and precisely is exactly the problem
when the precise sentence needs a sentence before it to make sense.
Large chunks solve that by keeping more together, and pay for it with
retrieval that's less targeted and a prompt that costs more tokens
every single call. There's no setting that avoids both costs at once.
Choosing where to sit on that line is choosing which failure you'd
rather have.

Which side should you favour? It depends on what your feature actually
does with the answer. A quick factual lookup, a price, a date, a policy number,
wants small and precise, because the whole answer is one fact and
padding it with surrounding paragraphs only adds noise. A feature
explaining a multi-step process wants larger chunks, because cutting
that process into precise but disconnected fragments defeats the
point of retrieving it at all.

@slide two-col
kicker: One corpus, different content
## Same document set, and one chunk size doesn't fit all of it

::: cols
:: card bad
### One rule for everything
- FAQ entries split at a fixed character count
- Policy clauses cut mid-sentence
- A pricing table chunked like prose
:: card good
### Matched to what it actually is
- FAQ entries chunked by question and answer, as a pair
- Policy chunked by clause, its natural unit
- The pricing table kept whole, or chunked by row with headers repeated
:::

@narrate
Here's the decision most teams skip entirely: your corpus probably
isn't one kind of document. It's several, and each one has a natural
unit that isn't a character count.

An FAQ's natural unit is a question and its answer, together, always.
A policy document's natural unit is a clause. A table's natural unit
is a row, and a row without its header means nothing. Apply one fixed
chunk size to all three and you'll get an FAQ answer with half the
question missing, a policy clause severed from the sentence that
qualifies it, and a table row nobody can interpret. Three different
content types, three different failures, one shared cause.

This is worth raising specifically because it's an easy thing for a
PM to actually catch that an engineer implementing quickly might not
think to ask about. You know what's in your knowledge base. You know
an FAQ looks nothing like a policy document. That knowledge is the
entire input this decision needs, and it's exactly the knowledge
sitting with the product side of the table, not the engineering side.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
Bigger doesn't mean safer. ==A chunk covering three different ideas
produces an embedding that's a blurry average of all three==, and
matches every one of them worse than a chunk about just one would.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because the instinct when chunking feels risky
is to make chunks bigger, and that instinct backfires in a specific,
technical way.

An embedding represents what a chunk is about. Cram three unrelated
ideas into one chunk and the embedding becomes a blur of all three,
matching none of them as sharply as a focused chunk would have. Bigger
doesn't buy you safety. It buys you a chunk that's vaguely about
everything and precisely about nothing, which is a worse retrieval
result than a well-scoped small chunk that happens to lack a little
surrounding context.

The instinct to go bigger usually comes from a real fear, that a
narrow chunk will miss something important. The fix for that fear
isn't size, it's the natural-unit thinking from the last slide. A
chunk scoped to one clause, one question, one row, is both focused and
complete. The safety people are reaching for when they oversize a
chunk was never about bytes. It was always about keeping one coherent
idea together, and a bigger chunk is a blunt way to get there.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Chunking strategy
The deliberate choice of chunk size and boundary, made per content
type, trading retrieval precision against surrounding context. Not a
single number applied to an entire corpus by default.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
List the distinct content types in your own knowledge base. For each
one, name its natural unit, the way an FAQ's is the question-answer
pair, before assuming one chunking rule covers all of them.
:::

@narrate
That's the definition worth carrying forward: a strategy, decided
per content type, not a number picked once and forgotten.

The exercise is the audit most teams have never done. List what's
actually in your corpus, not as one blob but as the distinct kinds of
document it contains, and name the natural unit for each. If you can't
name one, that content type is probably being chunked by a default
nobody chose on purpose.

kicker: Before you move on
## One thing worth doing now

List the distinct content types in your knowledge base, and name whether each is chunked by its natural unit or by an unchosen default

@narrate
One thing before you move on.

List the content types feeding your retrieval system, genuinely distinct
types, not just documents. An FAQ, a policy library, and a product spec
are three different chunking problems wearing the same file extension.
Check each one honestly: if the answer is "whatever the default did,"
that's not a decision, it's a gap this lecture just made visible.

Next lecture gives you the metrics to actually measure whether any of
this is working, instead of judging retrieval quality by how a handful
of demo queries happened to look.
