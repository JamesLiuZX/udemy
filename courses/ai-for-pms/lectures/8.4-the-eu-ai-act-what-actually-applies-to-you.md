@slide statement
kicker: Section 8 · The regulation everyone's heard of
## The Act doesn't ask what your feature is built with. It asks what your feature is used for.
lead: Most features clear this in one paragraph. The ones that don't need to know it now, not during legal review the week before launch.

@narrate
Ask most product people what the EU AI Act says and you'll hear
something vague about "regulating AI," which is roughly as useful as
saying a nutrition label "regulates food." The actual document is far
more specific, and far more checkable, than its reputation suggests.

It sorts every AI system into one of four risk tiers, by what the
system is used for, not by which model powers it or how it was built.
Two features running the identical underlying model can land in
completely different tiers, because the tiers are about the use case,
not the technology.

That's genuinely good news for most product work. A feature landing in
the lightest tier faces close to nothing. A feature landing in the
heaviest tier faces a real compliance programme. The entire job is
knowing honestly which one you're in, which is what this lecture
actually teaches.

This is also worth learning even if you never touch a single EU user.
Plenty of regulation being drafted elsewhere borrows the same shape:
sort by consequence, not by technology. Learn to read this one well and
the next one is faster to read, not a fresh start.

@slide table
kicker: Four tiers, sorted by use case
## Same underlying model, four completely different obligation levels

| Tier | What it covers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unacceptable | Banned outright | Social scoring, manipulative or exploitative systems |
| High-risk | Risk management, human oversight, documentation | Hiring decisions, credit scoring, education admission |
| Limited risk | Transparency obligations only | Disclose that a user is talking to an AI |
| Minimal risk | No specific obligations under the Act | Most internal tools, spam filters, recommendation widgets |

@narrate
Read this table from the bottom up, because that's where most real
product features actually land. Minimal risk covers an enormous amount
of ordinary product work: internal drafting tools, search ranking, most
copilots, anything that helps a person do their job without deciding
something consequential about them.

Limited risk adds exactly one obligation worth remembering: tell the
user they're talking to an AI. If your feature already does that, and
most chat-style features already should, you've likely cleared this
tier without changing anything.

High-risk is the tier worth actually stopping for, and it's defined by
consequence to a person, not by sophistication. A hiring tool that
screens candidates, a system that scores creditworthiness, a tool that
affects access to education: these carry real obligations regardless of
how simple the underlying prompt is. A basic classifier deciding who
gets an interview can be high-risk. A frontier model drafting marketing
copy usually isn't.

The unacceptable tier is short and worth knowing by name rather than by
detail, because almost no legitimate product feature lands there. It
exists for practices most teams were never going to build anyway:
manipulating people through subliminal techniques, scoring citizens for
trustworthiness the way a small number of governments already do. If a
feature idea makes you personally uneasy enough to wonder whether it
belongs here, that discomfort is worth listening to before the tier
confirms it.

Tiers can also drift as a feature grows. A recommendation widget that
only ever suggests products is minimal risk. The same widget,
repurposed eighteen months later to influence which candidates a
recruiter sees first, has quietly walked itself toward the high-risk
tier without a single line of the original code changing. Re-check the
tier when the use case changes, not only when the feature first
launches.

@slide two-col
kicker: The assumption that surprises non-EU teams
## "We're not based in the EU" is not the question the Act actually asks

::: cols
:: card bad
### What teams assume
- "Our company is US-based, so this doesn't apply to us"
- Never checked whether EU users can actually reach the feature
- Found out during a customer's EU-market compliance review
:: card good
### What the Act actually checks
- Whether the feature's output reaches users in the EU, not HQ location
- Checked deliberately, for this specific feature, not the company overall
- Documented alongside 8.1's register row for the regulatory category
:::

@narrate
Notice the parallel to 8.3's training-data mistake, because it's the
same shape of error: a comfortable assumption standing in for a check
nobody actually ran.

The Act is extraterritorial by design, the same way some privacy law
already is. What matters is whether your feature's output reaches
someone in the EU, not where your company is headquartered or where
your servers sit. A US-only company with EU customers, or even EU users
of a nominally domestic product, can find the Act applies to that
specific feature regardless of where the team sits.

Check this deliberately, feature by feature, rather than assuming a
single company-wide answer covers everything you ship. A feature with
no EU exposure today can gain some the moment sales closes a deal
nobody saw coming, which is exactly why this belongs as a dated, owned
row in 8.1's register, not a one-time answer everyone forgets to
revisit.

Checking reach in practice is more concrete than it sounds: look at
where your paying customers or registered users are actually located,
not where your marketing targets them. A feature built for a domestic
audience that quietly picks up EU-based users through an existing
customer's global rollout has EU reach the moment those users start
sending real requests, whether or not anyone updated a go-to-market
document to say so.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
This lecture teaches the mental model, not a permanent checklist.
==Guidance, enforcement, and interpretation are all still evolving==,
and specifics will keep changing after this lecture is recorded.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat plainly, because regulation of this kind is being
actively interpreted in real time, and a course can freeze a mental
model far more reliably than it can freeze a set of legal specifics.

What's stable is the shape: sort by use case, check reach rather than
headquarters, treat consequence to a real person as the signal that
pushes a feature toward the heavier tiers. What's not stable is exactly
which use cases sit where, and exactly what a specific obligation
requires in practice, both still being clarified as the Act's own
timeline unfolds.

Bring the specific question to counsel, the same instruction as 8.3:
not "are we compliant," but "does this specific feature, used this
specific way, for these specific users, fall into the high-risk tier."
That's a question they can actually answer.

Treat this the way 7.4 treated a model selection scorecard: a recheck,
not a one-time decision. A feature that cleared the minimal-risk tier
this year, under this year's guidance, is worth re-confirming on a
cadence, the same discipline as everything else this section has asked
of a register row.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition The four-tier check
Sort a feature by what it's used for, not what it's built with. Check
whether EU users can reach it, not where your company sits. Treat
consequence to a real person as the signal that pushes a tier up.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Pick your feature closest to a consequential decision about a person:
hiring, credit, access, eligibility. Check it against the high-risk
examples first, before assuming minimal risk.
:::

@narrate
That's the check worth running honestly, and running it in that order
matters: start from your most consequential feature, not your simplest
one, because that's the one with something real to find.

Most product portfolios have at most one or two features anywhere near
the high-risk tier, and most people can name which ones they'd be,
before ever opening the regulation, if they're honest about it. That
instinct is usually right. Confirm it properly instead of leaving it as
an unconfirmed hunch nobody wrote down.

Do this before a launch review asks you to, not during one. A tier
assessment produced under deadline pressure, the week before a launch,
tends to arrive at whatever answer keeps the date. The same assessment
done two months earlier has room to actually change the feature's
design if the honest answer turns out to be uncomfortable.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Sort your features into the four tiers, honestly, starting with the riskiest
2. Check whether EU users can reach any feature you'd call high-risk

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Sort honestly, starting with whichever feature makes a decision that
actually affects a person's access to something: a job, a loan, a place
in a programme. That's the sort that matters, not a company-wide label.

Then check EU reach specifically for anything that lands in the top two
tiers. If the answer is yes, or even genuinely uncertain, that's a row
for 8.1's register, owned, dated, and headed for an actual conversation
with counsel, not a guess.
