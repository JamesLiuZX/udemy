@slide statement
kicker: Section 5 · Measuring retrieval
## The demo had five queries and all five worked. That's not a measurement. That's five data points that happened to be flattering.
lead: You already know this trap from the golden set lecture. It's the same trap, aimed at retrieval instead of the final answer.

@narrate
Retrieval quality gets judged the same way most things get judged
before someone learns better: a few queries, typed in by whoever built
the feature, all of which happen to work. That's not evidence.
Section four already taught you why: a small, self-selected sample
flatters you by construction.

This lecture gives you three specific, plain-language questions to ask
about retrieval instead, and the scorecard to actually ask them with.

Notice, too, why measuring retrieval separately from the final answer
matters at all. If you only ever check whether the finished answer
looked right, a retrieval problem and a generation problem produce the
exact same symptom: a wrong response. You'd know something failed and
have no way to know which of the two stages actually did it. Scoring
retrieval on its own is what turns "the bot got it wrong" into "the
right chunk was never found" or "it was found and misread," two
completely different fixes.

@slide table
kicker: Three questions, three real metrics
## What to ask, and the technical name for it

| Plain question | Technical name | What it catches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did we find it at all? | Hit rate | A document that exists but never surfaces |
| How high did it rank? | Mean reciprocal rank | The right chunk, buried past the cutoff |
| How much of what we got was useful? | Precision | Noise diluting the context |

@narrate
Three questions, and notice they catch three completely different
failures.

Hit rate is the blunt one: for a question with a known right answer
somewhere in your corpus, did retrieval surface it anywhere in the
results, yes or no. Rank goes a step further, because finding the
right chunk at position nine when you only keep the top five is the
same as not finding it at all. And precision looks at the other
direction entirely: of what you did retrieve, how much was actually
relevant, versus padding that dilutes the context the model has to
work with.

You'll hear these called recall at k, mean reciprocal rank, and
precision at k in a real engineering conversation. Same three ideas,
formal names. Use them, and you'll sound like you've done this before,
because you have.

Notice the shape of this table matches the p95 lesson from Section
two almost exactly. Hit rate alone is an average dressed up as a
metric: it tells you retrieval usually works. But what does "usually"
actually mean? Rank and precision are the tail: the specific cases
where it technically found something and that something wasn't good
enough to trust.

@slide metrics
kicker: What a real first scorecard looks like
## Ten test queries, scored honestly

::: metrics
- 8 of 10 :: Queries where the known source was found at all :: warn
- 3rd :: Average rank when it was found, out of the top 5 kept :: warn
- 60% :: Of retrieved chunks that were actually relevant :: bad
:::

@narrate
Here's what an honest first scorecard tends to look like, and it's
usually less flattering than the demo was.

Eight of ten found the right source somewhere, which sounds fine until
you notice the average rank: third out of five kept, meaning it's
riding close to getting cut if anyone tightens that number. And only
sixty percent of what actually got retrieved was relevant, which means
nearly half the context handed to the model on a typical query was
noise it had to read past. None of these numbers show up in a five
query demo, because a demo doesn't keep score.

Read those two missing queries carefully too, not just the eight that
worked. The two the system never found at all are the most important
row on this whole scorecard, because a rank problem is something the
existing pipeline can improve. A pure miss usually points somewhere
upstream entirely, back to chunking from last lecture, or ingestion
never having included that document in the first place.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
Good retrieval metrics don't guarantee a good answer. ==The right chunk
can be retrieved and still get misread==, which is why this scorecard
and Section four's answer-quality rubric are both required, not either
one alone.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because a clean retrieval scorecard can create
false confidence about the feature as a whole.

Retrieval and generation are two separate stages with two separate
ways to fail. You can retrieve exactly the right chunk and still watch
the model summarise it wrong, miss a caveat inside it, or misread a
number. This scorecard only measures the retrieval half. It's a
necessary instrument, and on its own it's an incomplete one. Pair it
with the rubric from Section four, which measures the half this
scorecard can't see.

Think of the two together as a diagnostic order, not a duplicate
effort. Run this scorecard first, because if retrieval itself is
failing, no amount of rubric scoring on the final answer tells you
where to actually intervene. Once retrieval is solid, the rubric from
Section four is what tells you whether the model is doing its job with
the material it was actually given.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Retrieval quality scorecard
A small set of test queries with a known correct source, scored for
hit rate, rank, and precision, kept separate from whether the final
generated answer was actually good.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Write ten real questions with a known correct source document each.
Run them through retrieval and score all three metrics by hand before
building anything more automated.
:::

@narrate
That's the artifact for today, and notice it's deliberately small.
Ten questions, scored by hand, tells you more than an elaborate
dashboard built on data nobody's checked.

Do it the unglamorous way first. Pick ten real questions, know the
right source for each one yourself before you run anything, and score
what actually comes back. The manual pass is what makes the automated
version trustworthy later.

Resist the urge to skip straight to automating this. Ten scored by
hand, once, teaches you what "relevant" actually means for your own
content, the exact judgment call that would otherwise get made
invisibly by whoever writes the automated check later.
