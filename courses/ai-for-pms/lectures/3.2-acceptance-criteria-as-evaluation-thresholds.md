@slide statement
kicker: Section 3 · The fix
## One sentence, four specific parts, replaces every version of "the AI should respond helpfully and accurately."
lead: Not a longer sentence. A different shape entirely, and it's the single highest-leverage rewrite in this course.

@narrate
Last lecture ended on a real PRD line that couldn't fail a build if it
tried. Here's what replaces it.

An evaluation threshold has four parts, always: what you're testing it
against, what counts as good, what fraction has to clear that bar, and
what's never allowed to happen even once. Get those four parts written
down, specifically, and you have a sentence QA can run. Leave any one of
them out, and you're back to hoping.

This is the rewrite. Watch what happens to the sentence from last
lecture.

Notice, too, what this format quietly forces you to do before you can
even write it. You can't fill in "on the fifty-case golden set" without
that golden set already existing. You can't say "score four or five on
the rubric" without the rubric already being written down somewhere
two people have agreed to. The sentence isn't just a better way of
phrasing the requirement. It's a forcing function that makes the real
work happen before launch instead of after.

@slide two-col
kicker: The same requirement, rewritten
## From a hope to a sentence QA can actually run

::: cols
:: card bad
### What the PRD said
"The AI should respond helpfully and accurately to customer questions."
:: card good
### What it should have said
"On the 50-case support golden set, at least 90% of responses score 4
or 5 on the accuracy rubric, with zero responses scoring 1."
:::

@narrate
Same intent, completely different sentence. Read the second one again
and notice it has no adjectives doing the actual work. Every word in it
points at something a QA engineer could go and check.

Fifty cases, named and fixed. A rubric with defined score bands. Ninety
percent, not "most." And a hard floor of zero for the worst category,
a factual error stated as fact, no matter how well the other ninety
percent perform. That last clause is doing more work than it looks
like, because an average can hide exactly the failure a launch review
most needs to catch.

Try the disagreement test on both sentences. Two reasonable people can
argue for hours over whether a given answer was "helpful enough." Which
one would you rather defend in a launch review? Almost nobody argues
over whether a response scored a four against a written rubric, or
whether it was response forty-one out of fifty. The
second sentence doesn't just describe the bar more precisely, it
changes what kind of conversation happens when someone questions
whether the feature is ready.

@slide table
kicker: Where each part comes from
## Four clauses, four separate things this section teaches

| Clause | What it specifies | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "On the 50-case golden set" | What you're testing against | The golden set, next lecture |
| "score 4 or 5 on the accuracy rubric" | What counts as good | The rubric, lecture 3.4 |
| "at least 90% of responses" | The bar that counts as passing | The threshold itself |
| "zero responses scoring 1" | A hard floor, not just an average | The failure taxonomy, lecture 3.5 |

@narrate
Here's why this lecture comes before the other three, not after them.
That one sentence is built from four things you don't have yet, and
each one gets its own lecture because each one is a real decision, not
a formality.

The dataset decides what you're actually testing against. The rubric
decides what "good" means precisely enough for two people to agree on
a score. The threshold decides how much slack you're willing to
tolerate. And the failure floor catches the one thing an average will
always hide: a rare but severe failure sitting quietly inside an
otherwise healthy pass rate.

Miss any of the four and the sentence quietly degrades back toward the
one from last lecture. Skip the dataset and you're testing against
whatever cases happened to be lying around. Skip the rubric and "score
four or five" means whatever the person scoring it feels that day.
Skip the floor and a rare, severe failure hides inside a pass rate
everyone's happy with. The sentence only works when all four clauses
are load-bearing, not decorative.

## How high a threshold needs to be climbs with the archetype
figcap: A summariser and an autonomous action don't earn the same benefit of the doubt.

```figure
kind: histogram
threshold: 4
threshold_label: "Where the bar gets highest"
bins:
  - {label: "Summarise", value: 80}
  - {label: "Extract", value: 85}
  - {label: "Draft", value: 85}
  - {label: "Answer", value: 95}
  - {label: "Act", value: 99}
note: "Illustrative starting points. The discipline of setting one matters more than the exact number."
```

@narrate
Remember the seven archetypes from Section one. Here's where that chart
turns into an actual number in your PRD.

A summariser that's wrong four times in twenty is annoying and
recoverable, a human reads the source anyway. An agent that acts on the
user's behalf at that same failure rate is a different conversation
entirely, because there's no draft step catching it first. The
threshold climbs with how directly the output reaches the user and how
hard a wrong answer is to undo.

Don't copy these exact numbers into your own PRD. Copy the habit: name
the archetype first, then set the bar it actually earns, instead of
reaching for whatever threshold felt reasonable in the meeting.

This is also where the disqualification checklist from Section one
earns its keep again. If naming the archetype and its blast radius
tells you the honest threshold would need to sit above ninety-nine
percent just to be responsible, that's not a number to negotiate down.
That's the checklist telling you, again, that the feature as scoped
might not be the one to ship.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
Overcorrecting to "100%, no exceptions" isn't rigour. ==A probabilistic
system essentially never hits 100%==, and an impossible threshold just
moves the argument to launch day instead of resolving it before.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because the team that's just been burned by a
vague criterion often swings straight to an impossible one.

A hundred percent sounds like the safe answer in a room full of people
who don't want to be blamed later. It isn't. It's a threshold nothing
will ever clear, which means the real decision, ship or don't, still
gets made informally, on launch day, by whoever's most persuasive in
the room. A threshold's whole job is to make that decision in advance,
on paper, where it can be argued about calmly.

You'll know a threshold is doing its job when it's specific enough to
lose an argument against. If everyone in the room would still agree to
ship at ninety percent but the document says ninety-nine, the number
wasn't a decision, it was a performance of caution. Set it where you'd
actually hold the line, not where it sounds most responsible in the
meeting.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Evaluation threshold
A pass rate, on a named dataset, against a defined rubric, with an
explicit floor for the failures you won't tolerate at any rate. Four
parts, all of them decided before launch, not during the retro after.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Take the untestable line you found last lecture. Write its four parts,
even with placeholder numbers. A wrong first guess is still more
useful than no criterion at all.
:::

@narrate
That's the definition worth keeping for the rest of this section, and
notice it's a checklist as much as a definition: dataset, rubric,
threshold, floor.

The exercise carries forward directly from last lecture. Take that same
untestable line and force it into these four parts, even if some of
the numbers are placeholders you'll revise. A rough threshold you can
argue with beats a fluent sentence nobody can test.

Expect the first attempt to feel uncomfortably specific, maybe even
arbitrary. That discomfort is the point. A vague criterion never felt
uncomfortable because it never committed to anything a reviewer could
push back on. The moment your threshold makes someone in the room say
"wait, is ninety percent actually enough here," the document is finally
doing its job.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Rewrite one acceptance criterion as a four-part threshold
2. Set the failure floor before you set the pass rate

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Rewrite one real acceptance criterion using all four parts. Guess at
the numbers if you have to, the structure matters more than precision
on a first pass.

And do the floor first, before the headline percentage. Deciding what
you'll never tolerate is a clearer conversation than deciding what
counts as usually good enough, and it tends to make the second number
easier to agree on too.

Next lecture builds the first of those four parts properly: specifying
the golden dataset your threshold actually gets tested against.
