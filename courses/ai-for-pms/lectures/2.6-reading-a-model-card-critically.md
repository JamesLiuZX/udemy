@slide callout
kicker: Section 2 · Past the leaderboard

::: callout Read this before you trust a leaderboard
A model can top every public leaderboard and still lose to a smaller
one on ==the one task you actually care about==. A benchmark table
isn't lying to you. It's answering a question you didn't ask.
:::

@narrate
Vendors publish a table, one model beats another by a few points on a
named benchmark, and the natural reading is "this model is better." Read
it that way often enough and you'll eventually ship the wrong one.

A benchmark score is a real measurement of a real thing: how a model
performs on a specific, fixed set of questions someone else wrote. It
is not automatically a measurement of how it'll perform on your
specific task, with your specific data, in your specific product.
Sometimes those correlate closely. Sometimes they don't correlate at
all.

This lecture is the handful of questions that tell you which situation
you're actually in, before you commit to a model on the strength of a
percentage.

It matters more than it sounds like it should, because model choice is
usually one of the harder decisions to reverse. Swapping a vendor
after launch means re-running your evals, re-checking your prompts
still hold, and possibly explaining to a room why the number everyone
signed off on wasn't the number that mattered. Five minutes of reading
the fine print before that decision is a lot cheaper than the version
of this conversation that happens after.

@slide table
kicker: What the claim says, and what to actually ask
## Four common lines, read the way an engineer already reads them

| What the card says | What it actually means | Ask this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "92% on [benchmark]" | Score on a fixed public test set | Does that benchmark include anything like my task? |
| "Beats [competitor]" | One benchmark, one snapshot in time | Independent number, or the vendor's own run? |
| "128k context window" | The maximum it will accept | Does quality hold near that limit, or does it just fit? |
| "Knowledge cutoff: [date]" | Where training data stops | How stale is that already, for what I need current? |

@narrate
Four lines you'll see on almost every model announcement, and four
questions that turn each one from a headline into an actual answer.

A percentage on a named benchmark is real, but it's a score on
questions somebody else chose. If none of those resemble what your
feature actually does, the number is true and almost irrelevant. "Beats
a competitor" does a lot of work in one sentence: it matters enormously
whether that comparison came from an independent lab or the vendor
grading its own homework.

Context window and knowledge cutoff are two you already have a head
start on. A stated context window is a ceiling, not a promise, and
quality can slip well before it fills up. A cutoff date only gets older
from the day it's published. None of these four lines are dishonest.
They're just narrower than the headline makes them sound.

Notice the pattern: each claim is precisely true and narrower than it
reads at a glance. That's what happens whenever a complicated system
gets compressed into one line on a comparison page. Your job isn't to
catch anyone lying. It's to expand the line back into the question it
was actually answering.

## Most benchmark questions are a handful of task types
figcap: Public benchmarks are dominated by a few categories. If your feature isn't one of them, the leaderboard says less than it looks like.

```figure

  - {label: "Tasks like yours", value: 6}
note: "Illustrative split. The exact mix varies by benchmark, the pattern doesn't."
```

@narrate
Here's the shape of a typical general-purpose benchmark, and it's
worth actually seeing once.

Most public benchmarks are built from maths problems, coding
challenges, trivia questions, and logic puzzles, because those are
easy to grade automatically and cheap to scale to thousands of
questions. That's a reasonable way to build a benchmark. It's just not
a survey of everything a model might be asked to do.

If your feature summarises support tickets, or drafts marketing copy,
or extracts fields from invoices, look again at that chart. There's
usually a sliver, if anything, that resembles your actual task. A
model can be genuinely excellent at maths and code and still be
mediocre at the one thing you're about to ship, because the benchmark
never tested for it.

There's a second problem worth naming, quietly, alongside the first.
Public benchmarks are, by definition, public. Their questions and
answers sit somewhere on the internet, and internet text is exactly
what these models train on. A model can score well partly because it's
genuinely capable, and partly because it's seen something close to the
test before. You can't easily tell which from the outside, which is
one more reason a headline percentage deserves less weight than it
gets.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
Public benchmarks aren't worthless. ==A model that's weak everywhere
else probably is weak==, benchmarks just can't tell you it's good at
your specific task, only that it isn't obviously broken.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because the lesson here isn't "ignore
benchmarks."

They're a genuine floor. A model scoring poorly across the board is
telling you something real and probably worth heeding. What they can't
do is promise a ceiling on your specific task, because your task, in
almost every case, was never in the test set to begin with.

That's exactly the gap your own golden set from Section four is built
to close. The benchmark tells you a model probably isn't broken. Your
fifty test cases tell you whether it's actually good at the thing you're
shipping.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Reading past the leaderboard
Checking what a benchmark claim actually measures, and whether that
measurement resembles your task, before comparing headline
percentages between models.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Pull the model card for whatever your team currently uses. Find the
training cutoff and the evaluation methodology. If either is missing
or vague, that absence is itself the answer.
:::

@narrate
That's the skill in one sentence: read what's actually being measured
before you compare the numbers, not after.

The exercise takes ten minutes and is worth doing once for real. Open
the model card your team already relies on, and find those two things
specifically. A card that's thorough about both is a good sign about
the vendor. A card that's vague about both is worth noticing, too.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## One thing worth doing now

Pull one benchmark claim your team has relied on and check whether anything resembling your actual task was ever in that benchmark

@narrate
One thing before you move on.

Find one benchmark claim that influenced a real decision on your team,
and check what it actually measured. Independent or vendor-reported?
Recent, or from a superseded model generation? And above all, was
anything resembling your actual task in it at all? If you can't answer
that, neither could whoever made the original decision.
