@slide statement

## Any list of what these models can't do is wrong by the time it ships. That's not an excuse to skip this lecture, it's the actual lesson.
lead: The skill isn't memorising today's frontier. It's testing where it sits for your task, this month.

@narrate
I'm going to give you a list in this lecture: categories of things these
models are reliably good at, and categories they're reliably weak at.
Before I do, I need to tell you something that makes the list more
useful, not less. Some of what's in the "weak" column today will move to
the "reliable" column within a year, sometimes within months.

That's not a reason to skip this. It's the actual point. The specific
list changes. The categories, and the discipline of testing rather than
assuming, don't.

By the end of this you'll have both: today's honest snapshot, and a
five-minute method for re-checking it yourself whenever a new model
ships.

Most teams get this backwards. They either treat the model as capable of
anything, because the demo was impressive, or they treat it as capable of
nothing serious, because someone read a horror story once. Which one is
your team doing right now? Both are guesses. The frontier is a specific,
checkable line, not a feeling.

@slide two-col
kicker: The current shape, in categories
## Reliable versus still genuinely risky

::: cols
:: card good
### Reliably strong
- Rewriting, summarising, and extracting from text you provide
- Classifying against categories you define
- Common, well-specified code patterns
:: card bad
### Still genuinely risky
- Exact arithmetic and counting, especially in longer numbers
- Anything past its training cutoff, unless you supply it
- Long chains of steps with no way to check the middle
:::

@narrate
Here's the current shape, in categories built to outlast any specific
benchmark.

Reliably strong: working with text you actually give it. Rewriting,
summarising, pulling structured fields out of something unstructured,
sorting things into categories you defined. This is pattern completion
applied to material that's right there in the prompt, exactly where the
mechanism from lecture one is at its best.

Still genuinely risky: exact arithmetic, especially with longer numbers,
because it's predicting digits as text, not calculating. Anything past
its training cutoff, unless you hand it the answer yourself. And long
chains of reasoning where nothing checks the middle steps, because an
early small error compounds silently into a confidently wrong final
answer.

Notice the pattern connecting the whole right column: every one of these
is a case where the mechanism has to extend past what's directly visible
in the prompt, into genuine calculation, genuine recency, or genuine
multi-step verification. That's precisely where pattern completion is
weakest.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
Every one of those "risky" items is an active area of real improvement.
==Tool use and verification are closing several of these gaps already==,
which means this exact table needs re-checking, not memorising.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because pretending this list is permanent would
be the least defensible thing in this whole course.

Every item in the risky column is being actively worked on. Arithmetic
gets more reliable once a model can call an actual calculator instead of
predicting digits as text. Some long reasoning chains get sturdier once a
model can check its own intermediate steps rather than committing blind.
Neither problem is solved everywhere, in every product, today, but both
are visibly moving.

That's exactly why the next slide matters more than this one. A team that
memorised this table eighteen months ago and never checked again is
making decisions on a map that's already partly wrong, in the specific
direction of being too cautious about things that quietly got fixed.

@slide definition
kicker: The actual skill

::: definition Boundary probing
Before you trust a feature, deliberately testing it at the edge of its
category, on purpose, rather than assuming based on what you read a year
ago.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Pick the one item on the risky list closest to what your feature actually
does. Test it, today, on your own real cases. That result is worth more
than anything in this lecture.
:::

@narrate
Here's the skill that actually survives every model upgrade, and it's the
reason this section exists at all.

Boundary probing means you don't take last year's capability list on
faith, in either direction. You test, deliberately, at the edge of
whatever category your feature sits in, with your own real cases, before
you build a launch decision on an assumption.

The exercise is that test, run for real. Whichever risky item is closest
to your feature, try it today. Whatever you find is worth more than the
table two slides back, because that table has an expiry date and your
test result doesn't.

This is also the entire reason Section four exists as a full section
rather than one paragraph here. A boundary probe you run once, by hand,
tells you where the edge is today. A golden set tells you if it moves.

@slide metrics
kicker: A boundary probe, run for real
## Testing one risky item on a real feature

::: metrics
- 200 words :: Length of the ticket thread asked about ::
- 3 of 10 :: Times it counted the messages correctly :: bad
- 10 of 10 :: Times it sounded equally confident :: bad
:::

@narrate
Here's what a real probe actually looks like. A support tool asked to
count how many messages, out of a two hundred word thread, came from the
customer versus the agent.

Ten runs, exact same question. Three correct. And every single one of
the ten, right or wrong, delivered with identical confidence, because
that's the consequence from lecture one showing up again: no separate
signal for "I'm not sure about this count."

Counting is exactly the kind of task that looks trivial and sits
squarely in the risky column. Nobody would have guessed three out of ten
from the demo, because the demo used a five-message thread, and five is
short enough to get right by pattern alone almost every time. The probe
only found the gap because it pushed past that.
