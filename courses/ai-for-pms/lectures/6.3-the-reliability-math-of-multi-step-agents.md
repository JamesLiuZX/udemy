@slide quote
kicker: Section 6 · The number that matters
attrib: Why a demo feels reliable and a production agent doesn't

> "Ninety-five percent accurate per step sounds excellent. At ten steps, it is a coin flip."

@narrate
Two lectures ago, the honest caveat said a five-step agent makes five
separate chances to be wrong, not one. Here's exactly what that costs,
in a number you can compute yourself in ten seconds.

A demo runs an agent through the same two or three steps a dozen times
until it looks polished. Production runs it thousands of times, often
chaining five, eight, ten steps per task. Same per-step accuracy, wildly
different odds of the whole chain finishing correctly. That gap is not a
bug in your rollout. It's arithmetic.

You don't need a statistics background to follow it, either. Everything
in this lecture is one operation, multiplication, repeated once per step,
and you can do the whole thing on the calculator already open in another
tab.

@slide table
kicker: The maths, computed
## Multiply the per-step accuracy by itself, once per step

| Steps in the chain | 90% per step | 95% per step | 99% per step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 90% | 95% | 99% |
| 3 | 73% | 86% | 97% |
| 5 | 59% | 77% | 95% |
| 7 | 48% | 70% | 93% |
| 10 | 35% | 60% | 90% |

@narrate
Read the ninety-five percent column first, because it's the one that
catches people. Ninety-five percent per step is genuinely good.
Individually, you'd approve it without hesitation. Chained across ten
steps, the whole task now finishes correctly sixty percent of the time.
That's not a rounding error. That's a coin flip wearing a good report
card.

So what's actually driving that drop? Just multiplication. If each step
succeeds independently with probability p, and nothing catches a mistake
before the next step builds on it, the whole chain succeeds with
probability p multiplied by itself once per step. Five steps at ninety-five percent isn't ninety-five
percent minus a bit. It's zero point nine five to the fifth power,
seventy-seven percent, because every single step has to land for the
task to count as done.

@slide statement
kicker: The walkthrough
## Five multiplications, twenty points gone
lead: Each one alone barely moves the needle. Stacked, they don't.

@narrate
Walk the five steps out loud and it stops feeling abstract. Point nine
five, times point nine five, is point nine oh two five after two steps,
already under ninety-one percent. Times point nine five again is point
eight five seven after three. Again is point eight one five after four.
Again is point seven seven four after five, matching the table. Each
individual multiplication barely moves the number. Five of them in a row
move it twenty points.

Now picture that fifth step being the one that actually issues the
refund, or sends the email a customer sees. The four steps before it
were invisible to anyone watching. The compounding wasn't.

@slide metrics
kicker: Why the ninety-nine percent column matters this much
## Ten steps, same chain, one number moved

::: metrics
- 60% :: Whole-chain success, 95% per step, 10 steps :: bad
- 90% :: Whole-chain success, 99% per step, 10 steps :: good
:::

@narrate
Look at the ninety-nine percent column too, because it's the argument for
why that number matters so much more than it sounds like it should. The
jump from ninety-five to ninety-nine per step looks modest. Chained ten
deep, it's the difference between a coin flip and a system you can
actually trust with something that matters.

Compare the two columns at ten steps directly: sixty percent against
ninety percent. Four points of per-step accuracy, the gap between
ninety-five and ninety-nine, buys you thirty points of whole-chain
reliability once it's compounded ten times over. That's the entire
argument for why teams spend so much effort chasing the last few points
of model accuracy on anything that runs long chains. It isn't
perfectionism. It's the only lever in that column that actually moves
the number this much.

@slide two-col
kicker: Two ways to respond to this table
## One of these is much easier to actually do

::: cols
:: card bad
### Chase per-step accuracy
- Hard: often needs a better model or more prompt engineering
- Gains shrink fast past a point, diminishing returns
- Doesn't change how many chances there are to fail
:: card good
### Shorten the chain, or checkpoint it
- Fewer autonomous steps between checks, fewer chances
- A human or a rule verifies output at natural checkpoints
- Cuts the exponent, which moves the number far more per unit of effort
:::

@narrate
Both responses are legitimate. They are not equally efficient, and the
table is exactly why.

Pushing a step's accuracy from ninety-five to ninety-seven percent is
real engineering work for a small gain. Cutting a ten-step chain to five
steps, or inserting one checkpoint that catches an error before it
compounds into the next three steps, moves the whole-chain number by
tens of points, for a design decision rather than a model upgrade. When
you're scoping an agent, this is the lever worth reaching for first, and
it's the same instinct behind the checkpoint idea Section three's
degradation paths were built around.

A checkpoint doesn't have to mean a person reading every output. Often
it's a cheap rule: confirm the order number the agent pulled actually
exists before letting it decide anything about a refund. That single
check doesn't just catch its own error. It resets the compounding, because
every step after it now starts from verified ground instead of carrying
forward whatever the chain assumed five steps back.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
Steps are rarely perfectly independent. ==That can make the real number
better or worse than this table==, but the direction, more steps
compounds more risk, holds either way.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat plainly, because the maths above assumes something
that isn't exactly true.

Real steps aren't fully independent. Sometimes that helps: a checkpoint
catches an error before it compounds, so the real chain does better than
raw multiplication predicts. Sometimes it hurts: one bad piece of context
early in the chain, a wrong order history, a misread date, quietly
drags every step downstream with it, so the real chain does worse.
Neither correction changes the shape of the lesson. More autonomous steps
between checks means more compounding, in whichever direction your
specific chain happens to break.

Don't let that honesty become an excuse to wave the whole table away.
Nobody in the room disputing this number can tell you, on the spot,
whether their agent's steps happen to correlate for the better or the
worse. Treating the multiplied estimate as a reasonable starting point,
and checking it against real production data once you have some, is a
far stronger position than assuming independence doesn't apply to you.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition Compounded reliability
The whole-chain success rate of a multi-step agent, roughly the per-step
accuracy raised to the power of the number of unchecked steps. Falls
faster than intuition expects, because every step must succeed.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Count the steps in an agent you're scoping, and estimate a rough
per-step accuracy. Raise it to that power. If the result surprises you,
that's the number you were missing, not a reason to distrust the maths.
:::

@narrate
That's the definition worth carrying past today, and the exercise takes
less time than most people expect to be worth doing.

Count honestly. Every tool call, every branch where the model decides
something, not just the steps that show up on the architecture diagram.
Then do the multiplication. Most people who run this for the first time
on their own proposed agent are surprised by how far the real number sits
below the number they'd have guessed from the demo.

Bring the result into the next conversation about that agent, whatever
it is. A budget request, a launch review, a debate about scope. A
specific number, computed in front of the room, changes that
conversation in a way that a general worry about reliability never does.
It's the same shift Section four spent a whole section teaching: a
measured number beats a confident feeling every time someone tries to
argue with it.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Count the steps in an agent you're scoping, and compute its real number
2. Find the one checkpoint that would cut that chain roughly in half

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Run the actual computation, on a real chain, not a hypothetical one.
Write the number down even if it's uncomfortable, especially if it's
uncomfortable.

Then find one point in that chain where a checkpoint, a human glance or
a simple rule, would split it into two shorter chains instead of one long
one. Next lecture is the design patterns that make checkpoints like that
a normal part of the architecture, not an afterthought bolted on once
something has already gone wrong.
