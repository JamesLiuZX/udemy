@slide quote
kicker: Section 8 · The question you'll actually be asked
attrib: What a customer's security questionnaire, or your own legal team, asks in one line

> "Is our data safe with this vendor?"

@narrate
Someone in a review will eventually ask exactly that, as though it's
one question with one answer. Is it, though? It's actually three
separate questions, PII handling, data residency, and training-data
rights, each with its own owner, and conflating them is how a team
confidently answers the easy one and never notices the other two went
unanswered.

This lecture keeps the three apart on purpose, and gives you the
specific question to ask for each, not a generic reassurance that "we
take data privacy seriously."

Picture the meeting where this actually comes up: a customer's security
team sends a questionnaire, or your own legal team asks before a
launch. The question arrives as one line. The honest answer needs
three, and arriving with only one prepared is how a launch stalls a
week before it should ship.

@slide table
kicker: Three different questions
## Same meeting, three different documents hold the actual answer

| Question | What it actually asks | Where the answer lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PII handling | What personal data reaches the model, and where does it go after | Your own architecture, if you've mapped it |
| Data residency | Which country's servers and laws actually govern this data | The vendor's infrastructure documentation |
| Training-data rights | Can the vendor use your data to improve their models | The contract, usually as a specific setting |

@narrate
Read the last column, because it's the one that actually matters: three
different places, three different owners, and a team that's checked one
often assumes it's checked all three.

PII handling is the one you can answer yourself, because it's about
your own system, not the vendor's. Trace an actual request: what
personal information is in it, and everywhere it goes afterward, a
prompt, a log line, a support ticket someone reads later. Most teams
have mapped this less thoroughly than they assume.

Map it further than the obvious path, too. The request itself is rarely
the only place personal data sits. A retry queue, an analytics event
fired for the product team, an error report captured by a monitoring
tool when something crashes mid-request, all of these can carry
personal data along with them, invisibly, well outside the one flow
anyone actually reviewed.

@slide sidenote
kicker: The two you don't own
## Residency and training rights sit in someone else's document

::: split
:: main
Data residency and training-data rights both live in someone else's
document, not yours, which is exactly why they get skipped. Reading a
vendor's infrastructure page or contract terms feels like someone
else's job, right up until an auditor or a customer asks and nobody on
the team has actually opened either one.
:: aside Where residency actually bites
A homepage claiming "enterprise-grade security" tells you nothing about
which countries your prompts are processed in. The trust or
infrastructure documentation does, and it's usually one page you've
never had a reason to open until someone asks.
:::

@narrate
Residency matters most concretely the moment a customer asks whether
their data ever leaves a specific region, a question that's become
routine in enterprise procurement and impossible to answer honestly
without having actually read the vendor's infrastructure documentation,
not just their marketing page.

@slide two-col
kicker: The assumption that costs teams the most
## Training-data rights specifically: assumed versus checked

::: cols
:: card bad
### What most teams assume
- "They're an enterprise vendor, surely they don't train on our data"
- Nobody checked the actual account setting
- Found out otherwise from a customer's security questionnaire
:: card good
### What a checked answer looks like
- The actual setting, screenshotted, dated, named to an owner
- Changed explicitly if the default wasn't what you needed
- A specific sentence you can put in your own security questionnaire
:::

@narrate
Notice the bad column's assumption isn't unreasonable, it's just
unchecked, and those are different problems with very different
consequences.

Most vendors do offer a setting that stops your data from being used
for training. Plenty of accounts have it left on whatever the default
was, which is not always off, and nobody on the product side ever
opened the settings page to look. The gap isn't malice on the vendor's
side or carelessness on yours. It's a question that felt like someone
else's job until a customer's own security review asked it directly.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple compared to the other risks in
this section: open the setting, read what it actually says today, and
write the answer down with a date, the same discipline 8.1 asked for
every register row. That one afternoon closes a question that otherwise
resurfaces every single time a customer's procurement team asks it.

Notice, too, that a single vendor can answer this question two
different ways depending on which of their products you're actually
using. A consumer chat product and its enterprise API often sit under
completely different data terms, from the same company, and assuming
the friendlier consumer answer applies to the API you're actually
calling is its own specific version of this mistake.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
This lecture teaches the three questions, not the answer for your
jurisdiction. ==Residency and privacy law genuinely differ by country
and by sector==, and this is where actual counsel earns their fee.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat plainly, because a course can't responsibly hand
you specific legal answers for every jurisdiction a learner might be
sitting in.

What doesn't change by jurisdiction is the shape of the three
questions. Where does personal data go, whose laws govern where it's
processed, and who can train on it. Ask all three, everywhere you
operate, and you've done the part of this job that's actually yours.
Getting the specific answer right for your specific country and sector
is exactly what legal and compliance are for, and asking them a precise
question is far more useful to them than asking whether the feature is
"compliant" in general.

"We're compliant" is the same empty shape as "the model is powerful"
from Section two, or "legal will review it" from two lectures ago: a
sentence that sounds like an answer and closes the conversation before
anyone's actually checked anything. The specific, checkable version is
always three separate sentences, one per question, each with its own
evidence behind it.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition The three-question check
Where personal data goes after it reaches the model, whose laws govern
where it's processed, and who can train on it, asked and answered
separately, never bundled into one reassurance.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Trace one real request through your feature and write down every place
it touches: the model call, any logs, any third party. Note which
country each stop is in.
:::

@narrate
That's the check worth running before anyone else asks you to, and the
exercise is more valuable than it sounds, because most people tracing
their own feature for the first time find a stop they'd forgotten
about.

A support log, a monitoring dashboard, a debugging export someone
downloaded once and never deleted. None of these are exotic risks.
They're ordinary infrastructure that happens to be holding personal
data in a place nobody's data-residency answer accounted for.

Do this for the request pattern that carries the most personal data,
not the simplest one, because that's the trace most likely to surface a
stop nobody remembered. A password reset flow, or a ticket like 4471
with a billing email that differs from the account email, will teach
you more than a generic product query ever will.

## One thing worth doing now

Trace one real request through every place it touches, and check your actual training-data setting rather than the assumed one

@narrate
One thing before you move on.

Trace the request first. You already know your feature better than any
document does, so this is usually faster than it sounds. Then open the
actual setting for training-data rights and write down what it says
today, not what you assumed it said. If it's not what you want, change
it, and note the date you changed it.
