@slide statement
kicker: Section 3 · The rubric clause
## "Reviewed by a subject matter expert for quality." That sentence means the bar is whoever's free on Tuesday.
lead: Not because the expert is wrong. Because "quality" lives entirely in their head, and nobody else can read it.

@narrate
Here's the rubric equivalent of last lecture's line: reviewed by a
subject matter expert for quality. It sounds like a real process. It
passes review the same way "helpful and accurate" did, back in the
first lecture of this section.

The problem isn't the expert. The problem is that "quality" was never
written down anywhere two people could compare. Ask that same expert to
score the same output twice, a week apart, and there's no guarantee
they land on the same answer. Would they even agree with themselves?
Ask a different expert, and the bar quietly moves again. Whoever happens to be reviewing that Tuesday is,
functionally, the entire rubric.

This is the same failure as the untestable acceptance criterion from
earlier in this section, just wearing a reviewer's name instead of an
adjective. "Helpful and accurate" hid its vagueness in the wording.
"Reviewed by an expert" hides the same vagueness behind a person, which
is why it survives review even more easily. Nobody wants to question
whether the expert knows quality when they see it. The document just
never asked them to write it down.

@slide two-col
kicker: The same gap, closed
## What the PRD actually needs to commit to

::: cols
:: card bad
### What most PRDs say
"Reviewed by a subject matter expert for quality."
:: card good
### What it should say
"Scored against the six-question accuracy rubric (attached), calibrated
to at least 85% agreement between two independent reviewers before
use."
:::

@narrate
Same intention again, and only one version can actually be enforced by
someone who wasn't in the room when it was written.

The second version points at a document, not a person. Anyone can open
it, read the six questions, and understand exactly what "good" means
for this feature. And it doesn't just claim the rubric is good, it
names the test that proved it: two reviewers, agreeing at least
eighty-five percent of the time, on cases neither of them discussed
beforehand.

Notice what that number is actually doing in the sentence. It isn't
decoration. It's evidence that the rubric survived contact with a
second person who wasn't in the room when it was written. A rubric
that's never been read cold by anyone but its author hasn't earned the
word "rubric" yet. It's still a draft with good intentions.

@slide table
kicker: What the PRD has to answer
## Five questions, answered on paper, before launch

| Question | Why it can't wait |
| --- | --- |
| Does a written rubric exist? | Or is "good" still just a feeling someone has |
| Do scores map to pass or fail? | "Score 4 or 5" from last lecture has to mean something specific |
| Has it been calibrated for agreement? | A rubric nobody's tested is a guess with better formatting |
| Who resolves a disagreement? | The answer should be a process, not a job title |
| Where does it live, who can edit it? | An unversioned rubric drifts exactly like an unversioned dataset |

@narrate
Five questions again, and the same discipline as last lecture: none of
these ask you to actually write the six questions yet. Section four
walks through exactly how to do that, and how to run the calibration
check that proves it works.

The pass or fail question deserves a pause. Remember the threshold
sentence from two lectures back: score four or five on the accuracy
rubric. That only means something if the rubric has score bands
written down, and if everyone agrees which bands count as passing
before a single case gets scored. Decide that after you've seen the
results, and you haven't measured anything. You've just picked the
answer you already wanted.

The versioning question matters for a reason specific to rubrics, not
just datasets. A rubric that quietly changes between one release and
the next makes every pass-rate comparison meaningless, because you'd be
measuring against a different bar without anyone announcing it moved.
Treat an edit to the rubric the same way you'd treat an edit to the
threshold itself: worth a note, a date, and a reason.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
Appointing a senior reviewer as tiebreaker feels efficient. ==It quietly
reintroduces the exact problem the rubric was supposed to remove==:
"good" becomes whatever one person thinks, again, just with a fancier
job title attached.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because this is the mistake teams make right
after they've done everything else correctly.

Two reviewers disagree on a case, and the instinct is to escalate: let
the most senior person in the room make the call. That feels
efficient, and it's the same trap wearing new clothes. It replaces an
ambiguous question with an authority's opinion, which is exactly the
setup this whole lecture opened by warning about.

The right answer to a disagreement isn't a tiebreaker. It's rewording
the question those two reviewers actually disagreed on, the way Section
four's calibration loop does it, so the next hundred reviewers don't
hit the same fork. A senior reviewer can absolutely help find the
better wording. They shouldn't be the wording.

Put it in the PRD explicitly if you want it to actually happen under
deadline pressure. "Disagreements are resolved by rewording the
question, not by escalation" is one sentence, and it's the sentence
that stops a rushed launch from quietly reverting to whoever's most
senior in the room.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition The rubric commitment
The paragraph in a PRD that names where the rubric lives, how its
scores map to pass or fail, and that it's been calibrated for
agreement, without containing the rubric's full text itself.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Find where your team's current PRD defines "good." If the answer is a
person's name instead of a document, that name is your rubric, and
nobody else can read it.
:::

@narrate
That's the shape again: a paragraph, not the whole instrument, and
every clause in it something a reviewer could actually check.

Do the exercise literally. Search your PRD for the word "quality" or
"good", and see what defines it. If you land on a role instead of a
rubric, you've found this lecture's gap in your own document, not just
in the example.

It's worth doing this search even on PRDs you didn't write yourself.
The pattern is common enough that finding it isn't a criticism of
whoever drafted the document. It's just what happens by default,
everywhere, until someone deliberately closes the gap this lecture is
about.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Find where "good" is defined by a person instead of a document
2. Write who resolves disagreement, and confirm it isn't a job title

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Find the place in your PRD where quality gets decided, and check
whether it points at a rubric or a person. Most, on a first honest
look, point at a person.

Then write down, specifically, how disagreement gets resolved. If your
answer was a role rather than a process, that's the fix to make before
anyone starts scoring.

Next lecture takes the dataset and the rubric you've now committed to
and asks the harder question: what happens when the feature fails
anyway, and how much of that failure a human has to catch before it
reaches a user.
