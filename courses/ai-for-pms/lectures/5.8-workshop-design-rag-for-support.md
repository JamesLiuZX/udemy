## Twenty minutes, one support use case, a complete RAG design draft.
lead: Not a polished document. Six sections attempted, ready to argue about, the same bar as Section three's workshop.

@narrate
Pick the support use case this course keeps returning to, or your own
equivalent: agents answering policy questions by searching several
systems by hand. Open A06 from last lecture, and give this the same
twenty minutes Section three's PRD workshop did.

The bar is identical too. Not perfect. Complete. Every section has
something in it, even where that something is a number you'll
validate later.

Support is the right use case to practise on precisely because it's
the one this course has walked through from every angle already: the
archetype from Section one, the threshold from Section three, and now
the retrieval mechanics from this one. Designing it end to end here is
what turns six separate lectures into one document you actually know
how to produce. Skip the exercise, and those six lectures stay six
separate ideas in your head, ones you can discuss in a meeting but
have never actually tested against a page you'd hand to an engineer.

@slide table
kicker: How to spend the twenty minutes
## A pace that keeps you moving, section by section

| Minutes | Section | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-5 | Why RAG | Would a genuinely better prompt have actually failed |
| 5-8 | Pipeline stages | Only add re-ranking if you can justify the latency |
| 8-12 | Chunking strategy | Name the natural unit for each content type |
| 12-16 | Retrieval targets | A guessed number beats a blank one |
| 16-20 | Failure coverage & permissions | Name the two most likely of the six failures |

@narrate
Notice why RAG gets the same five minutes acceptance criteria got in
Section three's workshop, and for the identical reason: everything
after it gets easier once that argument is actually made, not
assumed.

For the support case specifically, that argument is usually
straightforward to write. Agents are searching policy documents by
hand today, the policies are private and change quarterly, and no
amount of prompting supplies information the model was never given.
If your own case doesn't have an argument that clean, that's worth
noticing now, in minute five, not in minute twenty.

The pipeline stages minutes go quickly for the same use case, because
support content rarely needs the full seven stages built out. Most
support corpora don't need query rewriting or a heavy re-ranking pass
on day one. Naming what you're deliberately leaving out is as much a
decision as naming what you're building, and it belongs in the
document either way.

Chunking strategy and retrieval targets share the middle eight
minutes, and neither has to be perfect on a first pass. Sorting each
content type into its own natural chunk, an FAQ pair, a policy clause,
a full ticket thread, usually goes faster than people expect with
Section five's examples still fresh. The number that actually eats the
full four minutes is the retrieval target, because it's the first
point in the exercise where you're committing to something a
colleague could check against a real scorecard later.

The last four minutes, failure coverage and permissions, go fastest if
you resist listing every failure from two lectures back. Pick the two
failures most likely to actually bite a support corpus: near-duplicate
confusion from old tickets nobody archived, and permission leakage
from restricted policy pages sitting in the same index as public ones.
Two named failures with real fixes beat a generic list copied straight
off the table.

@slide quote
"Retrieval over the support and policy corpus, chunked per content type, target hit rate 90% at k equals 5, precision 70%, permissions checked live, re-indexed weekly, with a documented fallback when nothing relevant is found."
attrib: The support RAG design doc's retrieval section, from last lecture

@narrate
Recognise this sentence. It's the exact worked example from last
lecture, and seeing it again here is deliberate: this is roughly what
your own retrieval targets and permissions sections should sound like
once you've filled them in for real.

If what you've written in the last few minutes doesn't have this
shape, a scope, a chunking approach, two numbers, a permission model,
a re-index cadence, that's the gap worth closing while you're still in
the section, not three sections later.

Notice, too, what this sentence deliberately doesn't include: no
mention of which vector database, no model name, no framework. None of
that belongs in a design doc a PM writes. This sentence specifies what
the system has to achieve. It leaves precisely how to engineering,
the same boundary Section three drew around the PRD template.

It took this section six lectures to earn a sentence that specific, so
don't expect your own version on the first attempt. A rougher draft
that still names a scope, a chunking approach, and two numbers is a
completely reasonable place to be at the sixteen minute mark, with
four minutes still left to sharpen it.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
The retrieval targets section will feel impossible to fill in
honestly. ==Write a guessed number and flag it for validation==, not a
blank waiting for data that doesn't exist yet.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because this is the section most people stall
on, and for a reasonable reason: you genuinely don't know your real
hit rate until you've run the scorecard from 5.4, and you haven't run
it yet.

Write the number anyway. Ninety percent hit rate, seventy percent
precision, whatever feels like a defensible starting bar for this
specific use case, and mark it "target, to be validated against the
scorecard." That's not a weaker answer than a real measured number.
It's the honest, correctly-labelled version of exactly where you are
right now, and it's infinitely more useful than a blank cell nobody
will ever come back to fill in.

This is the same move as the placeholder threshold from Section
three's workshop, applied one section later in the process. A guessed
number, clearly labelled as a guess, is a real decision that can be
revised. A blank field is a decision nobody made yet, quietly waiting
to become someone's problem during launch week instead of during this
twenty minutes.

A labelled guess also ages well. Six months from now, whoever reopens
this document can see exactly which numbers were real and which were
placeholders, instead of wondering whether ninety percent was ever
actually tested against anything.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition A complete RAG design draft
All six sections attempted, including retrieval targets written as
validated guesses rather than left blank, ready for a colleague to
push back on today.
:::

::: example What to do when you finish early
Reread only the "why RAG" section. If you'd still defend it after
writing the other five, the draft is ready to share. If you wouldn't,
that's the one section worth five more minutes.
:::

@narrate
That's today's bar, stated plainly: attempted, not perfected, and
every number labelled honestly as measured or guessed. Six sections in
twenty minutes averages under four minutes each, which is exactly why
a labelled guess beats stalling on any single one.

If you finish with time left, don't smooth every section evenly.
Return to why RAG, the same instruction as Section three's workshop,
because it's still the section the other five are quietly depending
on.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Finish the draft, with retrieval targets labelled as guesses where they are
2. Send it to whoever owns the support corpus and ask what they'd challenge

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Finish the six sections. Anywhere you guessed a number, say so
explicitly, rather than letting it look more certain than it is.

Then send it to whoever actually owns the support content, today, and
ask what they'd push back on first. They'll usually find the gap in
minutes that would have taken you a week to notice alone.

Expect their first question to be about permissions, not chunking.
Content owners live inside the access model every day, and they'll
notice a gap there faster than anyone else in the room, before you've
even finished explaining the rest of the document.

Next lecture closes out this section: what you've actually built, and
how directly it plugs into Section six, where retrieval meets action
and the whole conversation changes.
