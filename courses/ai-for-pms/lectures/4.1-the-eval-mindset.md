@slide statement
kicker: Section 4 · The eval mindset
## You already evaluate your AI feature. You just haven't named which of three ways you're using.
lead: Each one catches a different disaster, and is blind to the other two.

@narrate
Right now, somebody on your team is deciding whether that AI feature is good
enough. Maybe it's you.

Most of the time that decision happens one of three ways. Someone opens the
tool, tries a few inputs, and reacts. Or a test suite runs against a fixed
set of examples before every release. Or a dashboard somewhere tracks what
real users are getting, after it's already live.

Those are three different instruments, and they measure three different
things. The argument you keep having with engineering about whether a
feature is ready is usually two people trusting two different instruments
and not realising it.

By the end of this lecture you'll know which one you're using, and, more
importantly, which disaster it can't see. Get that wrong and you either ship
on vibes alone, or trust a number that quietly stopped being true.

@slide table
kicker: The three mechanisms
## Vibes, offline, and online: what's actually running

| Mechanism | What runs | When | Sees |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vibes | A person reads outputs and reacts | Whenever someone happens to look | Whatever they happened to open |
| Offline | A fixed golden set, scored on a rubric | Before every release | The cases you chose in advance |
| Online | Real traffic, scored or watched | Continuously, after launch | Whatever users actually send |

@narrate
Here's the same three things stated precisely, because the names get used
loosely.

Vibes is a person reading outputs and reacting, right now, to whatever they
happen to have open. No fixed cases, no fixed questions. You already met
this one in Section 0, and you already know its problem: five people, five
different numbers.

Offline is the golden set, run against a rubric, before every release. It's
the instrument this section teaches you to build, by hand and then in code.
Repeatable, cheap once it exists, and it never sees anything you didn't put
in it.

Online is production itself. Whatever users actually send, scored or simply
watched, after the feature is already live. It sees everything, including
the inputs you never thought to imagine, but only after someone real has
already been on the receiving end of one.

@slide sidenote
kicker: The blind spot in each one
## What each mechanism cannot see, by design

::: split
:: main
1. **Vibes** misses consistency: ask five people, get five scores
2. **Offline** misses drift: the world moves on after you freeze the set
3. **Online** misses prevention: it only speaks after a user already felt it
:: aside None of them is optional
Skip vibes and you lose the fast check that catches the obvious. Skip
offline and you ship blind. Skip online and drift becomes a launch
incident instead of a two-week warning.
:::

@narrate
Here's the promise from the first slide, paid off properly.

Vibes misses consistency. You saw this exact failure in Section 0: the same
summary, scored two, three, three, four, five, by five people who each had a
real reason. That's not five bad reviewers. That's what happens when there's
no shared instrument.

Offline misses drift, which is the picture you're about to see. It's
excellent at catching anything that looks like the fifty cases you built it
from, and structurally unable to catch anything that doesn't.

Online misses prevention. It only speaks after a real user already sent the
input and felt the result. It's the most honest signal you have, and it is
always, by construction, too late for that one case.

None of the three is optional. Each one is the only thing standing between
you and the specific disaster the other two can't see.

@slide two-col
kicker: The case for offline
## Once it exists, it's the best instrument you have

::: cols
:: card good
### What it gets you
- The same fifty cases, scored the same way, on every single build
- A pass rate that moves only when the thing you changed moves it
- A number two disagreeing people can go and check
:: card bad
### What it quietly assumes
- Tomorrow's traffic looks like the traffic you sampled it from
- Nobody ships a feature, a policy, or a new customer segment
- The world holds still while you do
:::

@narrate
For good reason, most teams stop here. On its own terms, offline eval is
genuinely excellent.

Run the same fifty cases through the same rubric before every release, and
you get a number nobody can argue you out of: ninety-one percent, say,
moving only when the thing you changed moves it. That's the column on the
left, and it's real.

Now look at the column on the right, the assumption nobody says out loud. A
golden set is a photograph. It's exactly correct for the moment you took it,
and it says nothing about the moment after that. Hold onto that ninety-one
percent. You're about to watch it stop being the whole story.

## What happens when you re-run it on live traffic
figcap: Fifty golden-set tickets scored in March, and ten live tickets pulled and scored this week, on the same six-question rubric from Section 0.

```figure

lo: 1
hi: 5
axis_label: "Score out of 5, same rubric"
series:
  - {label: "Offline: golden set", note: "frozen in March", values: [4, 5, 4, 4, 5, 4, 5, 4, 4, 5]}
  - {label: "Online: live sample", note: "pulled this week", values: [2, 4, 1, 3, 5, 2, 4, 1, 3, 2]}
```

@narrate
Same product. Same six questions from lecture 0.4. Two different weeks.

The top row is your golden set, and it looks the way a healthy feature
should: tight, mostly fours and fives. That's the ninety-one percent that
got this feature shipped.

The bottom row is ten live transcripts, pulled at random this week and
scored on that identical rubric. Wider spread, lower average, a couple of
ones, and a one on this scale means the agent reopens the original ticket
anyway. Say your product added a new refund policy in April. Tickets like
Ticket 4471 got a lot more common, and none of March's fifty cases saw one.

Nothing here means the rubric is broken, or that March's fifty cases were
badly chosen. It means production moved and the photograph didn't.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Golden-set drift
The gap between how your golden set scores and how live traffic scores on
the identical rubric. It has exactly one cause: production changed and your
fifty cases didn't.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Once a release, pull ten transcripts you did not choose and did not curate,
and score them on your existing rubric. Match your golden-set number and
relax. Miss it, and you just found out two weeks before your users told you.
:::

@narrate
Notice the word doing the work in that definition: identical. Drift isn't
the rubric disagreeing with itself. It's the same six questions, asked of a
world that stopped holding still.

The exercise underneath it costs you twenty minutes a release. Ten
transcripts, the rubric you already own, no new tooling. It's the cheapest
early-warning system in this entire course.

Skip it, and you still find out. You just find out from a support
escalation, with your name already attached to the launch decision.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
Online signal is real, but it is never early. By definition, it only exists
after a real user has already sent the input you needed to see. And at ten
or twenty samples a week, a bad run and a bad model look identical: you need
volume before the number means anything.
:::

@narrate
Say the cost plainly, because it's real.

Online eval is a smoke alarm, not a sprinkler. It tells you something is
burning. It does not put the fire out, and it never once sounds before the
fire starts. That's not a flaw you can engineer away, it's what "after
launch" means.

And on its own, a handful of weekly samples is noisy. Two failures out of
ten live tickets: is that a true twenty percent failure rate, or a true
five percent rate having an unlucky week? At that sample size, nobody in
the room can tell you which. You'll meet the fix for that later in this
section: how many samples turn a hunch into a number you can defend.

Three mechanisms, and this section builds all three properly.

You'll turn vibes into something scoreable. You'll build the offline
instrument yourself, first where you can see every moving part, then in
code. And you'll give online signal a proper home instead of a Slack message
someone sends when a customer complains.

Do all three properly, and "is it ready" stops being an argument and starts
being a number everyone in the room can check.
