@slide table
kicker: Section 4 · The last honest step
## What this section built, and the one thing none of it protects

| Built | What it locks down |
| --- | --- |
| A frozen, versioned golden set | The cases can't quietly change |
| A calibrated rubric | The question can't quietly change |
| A validated judge | The scorer can't quietly change |
| A dashboard sized to sample noise | The number can't quietly change |
| None of the above | The room, the moment someone decides what to do about it |

@narrate
Here's an uncomfortable fact to close this section on. Every technique so
far protects the number itself. Not one of them protects the moment a
human looks at that number and decides what to do about it.

You can do everything right, freeze the set, calibrate the rubric, validate
the judge, size the noise, and still, in the room, quietly talk yourself
into shipping something the number told you not to.

How does someone who clearly believes in the process end up doing that
anyway? This isn't a new instrument to build. It's the last discipline:
catching yourself in the specific moment it's about to happen, because
there are really only two ways it happens, and both are avoidable.

Nobody sets out to do this. It happens gradually, under deadline pressure,
from people who would tell you, honestly, that they believe in the
process. That's precisely why it needs a defence built in advance, rather
than good intentions in the room.

@slide two-col
kicker: The two ways it happens
## What fooling yourself actually looks like

::: cols
:: card bad
### In the moment
- A case fails, so you quietly drop it from the set
- The score comes in low, so the bar quietly moves to meet it
:: card good
### Decided in advance
- The fifty cases were frozen before this release existed
- The bar was written down before anyone saw a score
:::

@narrate
There are really only two moves, and you already have the defence for
both. You just haven't pointed it at yourself yet.

The first is editing the instrument after you don't like the reading. A
case fails, and someone notices it's "not really representative", and it
quietly disappears before the next run. You built a freeze-and-version
discipline two lectures ago specifically so this can't happen without a
paper trail.

The second is moving the goalposts. You said ninety percent. The number
comes back at eighty-seven. And in the room, eighty-seven starts sounding
close enough, just this once, given the deadline. Nobody voted on that. It
just happened, in real time, under pressure.

@slide quote
kicker: A line you will hear
attrib: Every launch review, eventually

> "Eighty-seven is basically ninety, right?"

@narrate
You will hear a version of this sentence. Probably from someone
reasonable, under real pressure, not trying to deceive anyone.

That's what makes it dangerous. It doesn't sound like cheating. It sounds
like judgement, flexibility, reading the room. And it's the exact moment
where every hour you spent building a rigorous instrument gets quietly
overruled by a feeling.

The fix isn't a stronger argument in that room. It's making sure that
sentence has nowhere to land, because the decision already got made
somewhere else, earlier, on paper.

Notice, too, that nobody in that room is lying. Everyone believes what
they're saying while they're saying it. That's what makes a felt-in-the-
moment threshold so much weaker than a written one: it has no memory of
what it used to believe an hour ago.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Pre-registration
Writing your pass threshold down, and getting it signed off, before you
run the eval that will be judged against it. Borrowed from clinical
trials, for exactly the same reason: it stops the finish line moving once
the result is known.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Before your next release's eval run, write the threshold in the PRD or the
ticket, get one other person to see it, and timestamp it. Now "basically
ninety" has to argue with a document, not just a feeling in the room.
:::

@narrate
Borrow a word from a field that solved this problem before you did.
Clinical trials pre-register their success criteria for exactly this
reason: once you've seen the result, everyone's judgement about what
would have counted as good quietly shifts to match it.

The fix costs almost nothing. Write the threshold down, in the document,
before the eval runs. Get one other person to see it. Nothing fancy, no
approval workflow, just a timestamp that exists before the score does.

Now the debate in the room isn't "is eighty-seven close enough." It's "we
wrote ninety, here's why, do we still believe that," which is a
completely different, much more honest conversation.

@slide sidenote
kicker: Catching it in the moment
## Signs you're about to fool yourself

::: split
:: main
1. Someone says a case "isn't really representative" after seeing it fail
2. The threshold gets discussed only once the score is already on screen
3. "Just this once" is the actual justification being offered
:: aside Any one of these is enough
You don't need all three. One is the moment to stop the meeting and ask
where the number came from, and when the bar was set.
:::

@narrate
Three signs, and one is already enough.

Someone calling a failing case unrepresentative, only after it failed, not
before. The threshold getting discussed for the first time with the score
already visible to everyone in the room. And "just this once," said out
loud, as the actual reason.

None of these make anyone a bad actor. They're just what pressure does to
judgement, reliably, in every organisation that ships things on deadlines.
Naming them in the moment is the entire skill. You don't need a policy.
You need to recognise the sentence.

The person best placed to say it out loud is often you, precisely because
you're the one who knows where the golden set came from and when the
threshold was actually written down. That's not an awkward thing to raise.
It's the job.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
Sometimes the disciplined answer is: don't ship. That will occasionally
cost a deadline. ==A threshold that always bends is not a threshold==, and
a launch gate that never says no was never actually a gate.
:::

@narrate
Say the cost plainly, because this is where the discipline actually gets
tested.

Pre-registering a threshold means sometimes it says no, on a date you
didn't want to hear no. That's not the system failing. Slipping a date
because the number said so is the entire point of building the number in
the first place.

A threshold that bends every time it's inconvenient was never a
threshold, it was decoration. The credibility of the ninety-four percent
you report next quarter depends entirely on everyone remembering that
eighty-seven, once, genuinely didn't ship.

That's a harder thing to say in a roadmap review than "we shipped on
time." It's also the sentence that makes every future number you report
worth believing without someone re-checking your work.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Write your next release's threshold down before you run the eval
2. Agree, in writing, what "not representative" would actually have to mean

@narrate
Two things before you move on, and they're both five-minute habits, not
projects.

Before your next eval run, write the threshold down somewhere with a
timestamp, and show one other person. That's the whole defence against the
goalposts moving.

And agree, in writing, what would actually justify removing a case from
your golden set, before you're standing in a room with a failing one on
screen. Write the exception before you need it, and it stops being an
exception invented on the spot.
