@slide statement
kicker: Section 10 · The first two weeks
## Most AI feature ideas should die in a sprint. Very few should die in production.
lead: A discovery sprint is a cheap, fast way to find out which kind of idea you're holding, before anyone commits real engineering time.

@narrate
An idea for an AI feature arrives constantly, from a customer request,
a competitor's launch, an executive who saw a demo. Most of them sound
plausible in the room. A discovery sprint is how you find out which
ones actually hold up, in days, using tools you already have, rather
than months of engineering time discovering it the expensive way.

The sprint isn't new material. It's five questions this course already
taught you to answer, asked in a specific order, against a real idea,
under a real deadline. That order matters as much as the questions
themselves, because each day's answer decides whether the next day is
worth spending.

None of the five days ask you to do anything you haven't already
practised in this course. That's deliberate. A sprint built from
unfamiliar techniques takes longer to run than the five days it's
supposed to fit inside. A sprint built entirely from tools you've
already used once, for real, runs at the speed the format actually
promises.

@slide diagram
kicker: Five days, five questions you already know
## Each day is an exit, not just a step

```mermaid
graph LR
  D1[Day 1: Archetype fit] --> D2[Day 2: Blast radius]
  D2 --> D3[Day 3: Golden set]
  D3 --> D4[Day 4: Rough cost]
  D4 --> D5[Day 5: Go / no-go]
```

@narrate
Read the sequence as a series of exits, not a fixed five-day
commitment. Day one is just 1.6's seven archetypes, asked of a real
idea: does AI even fit this problem. An idea that fails there, on an
archetype mismatch, doesn't get four more days spent confirming what
day one already answered. Most ideas that don't survive a sprint don't
survive past day two.

Day three is usually where a genuinely good-sounding idea meets its
first real resistance. It's 4.2's coverage test, asked under a
deadline: if nobody can sketch even a rough golden set, fifty cases you
could plausibly score, that's not a paperwork gap. It's
a sign nobody's agreed what "correct" means for this feature yet, and
building anything before that's settled is building on an undefined
target.

Push day three specifically on the hard cases, not the easy ones. An
idea that only produces confident golden-set cases for the obvious,
common inputs and goes quiet on the genuinely ambiguous ones is telling
you something real: the feature sounds clear in the room and gets
fuzzy the moment someone tries to write down what counts as correct.
That fuzziness on day three is exactly what would have shown up as a
disputed pass rate months later, just discovered far earlier and far
cheaper.

@slide table
kicker: The exits that follow
## Days four, two and five

| Day | Question | Reused from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Four | Rough cost, order of magnitude, not a full model | 7.2 |
| Two | Blast radius, answered in writing | 6.6, 8.1 |
| Five | Go, no, or a narrower version of the same idea | this course |

@narrate
Day four doesn't need 7.2's full model, built properly, with real
token counts. It needs the rough version: an honest order-of-magnitude
guess at cost per use, multiplied by a realistic volume estimate. That
back-of-envelope number has killed more bad ideas by Friday than a
polished cost model ever will, because a rough number that's clearly
too high doesn't need more precision to make its point.

Day two asks the blast radius question a full section later than 6.6
first taught it, on purpose. Why wait until day two instead of day one?
Because an idea that survives day one but carries a genuinely high
blast radius, hard to undo, expensive if wrong, isn't automatically
disqualified. It just needs day two's answer in writing before day
three's effort is worth spending, the same discipline 8.1's register
asks of any risk with real stakes.

Day five isn't a fresh judgement call, it's an honest summary of the
first four. Go, if all four questions cleared. No, if any one of them
didn't, stated plainly rather than softened. Or a narrower version, the
option most sprints under-use: the same idea, scoped down to the slice
that actually clears every question, instead of either the full
original pitch or nothing at all.

@slide two-col
kicker: What the sprint is actually for
## A sprint that only ever says yes isn't running the process

::: cols
:: card bad
### Measuring success by launches
- Every sprint ends in "let's build it"
- No idea has ever failed day one or two
- The sprint has quietly become a formality before a decision already made
:: card good
### Measuring success by speed of the right answer
- Some sprints end in a fast, confident no
- A narrower version survives more often than the original pitch
- Everyone trusts the yes more, because the no was clearly possible
:::

@narrate
Notice the good column doesn't celebrate saying no more often. It
celebrates a process capable of saying no at all, quickly, when the
idea actually deserves it.

A sprint that always ends in yes isn't evaluating anything, it's
performing evaluation on top of a decision that was already made before
day one started. That's expensive theatre, not discovery, and everyone
on the team usually knows it within a sprint or two, which quietly
kills trust in every future sprint's yes as well.

This connects directly to this lecture's opening claim: a sprint's
entire value depends on it being genuinely capable of stopping. Remove
that capability, deliberately or through quiet organisational pressure,
and you haven't shortened the process. You've renamed a five-day rubber
stamp and kept calling it discovery.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
A fast no is a successful sprint, not a failed one. ==The sprint's job
is finding out fast==, cheaply, before anyone's spent real engineering
time finding out the expensive way.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat plainly, because it's easy to experience a "no"
result as the sprint having wasted a week, and that framing is exactly
backwards.

Compare the cost of a week-long sprint against the cost of three months
of engineering discovering the same no, after a launch date's already
been promised to someone. The sprint that produces a confident no on
day two is one of the cheapest wins available to a team, and it should
be reported that way, not quietly filed away as a project that "didn't
work out."

Track this the same way 9.1 asked you to track a real metric: count
sprints run against sprints that ended in yes, and watch that ratio
over time. A ratio sitting at or near one hundred percent yes is the
single clearest sign a sprint has stopped doing its actual job,
whatever the individual sprints looked like from the inside.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition A discovery sprint
Five days, five questions this course already answered for you, run
against a real idea in sequence, with an explicit exit at any day the
idea fails to clear its question.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Take your team's most recent AI feature idea. Run it through day one
and day two only. See whether it would have survived to day three.
:::

@narrate
That's the process worth running on the next idea that lands on your
desk, and the two-day version in the example is worth trying even if
you never run the full five.

Most ideas that eventually get shelved after real engineering time was
spent would have failed day one or two of this sprint, cheaply, if
anyone had run it first. That's the entire case for doing this before
committing resources rather than after.

Notice, too, that a sprint's real output isn't the yes or no by itself.
It's the written reasoning behind it, the same discipline 7.4 asked of
a model choice: not just the decision, but the specific answer to each
day's question that produced it, so someone revisiting the idea in six
months isn't starting from zero.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Pick your team's next AI idea and run days one and two on it
2. Report a fast no exactly as honestly as you'd report a yes

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Pick a real idea, not a hypothetical one, and run the first two
questions honestly. Most people find this takes under an hour once the
tools are already familiar.

Then, whatever the sprint concludes, report it with the same confidence
either way. A well-run no deserves the same visible credit as a well-run
yes, because both are the process working correctly.

Neither of these requires scheduling a formal five-day sprint today.
The two-day version is the whole point: fast enough to run this week,
on the idea that's already sitting in your inbox.

Next lecture is managing stakeholder expectations, the skill that
decides whether people actually trust the sprint's answer once you've
got it.
