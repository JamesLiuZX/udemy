@slide statement
kicker: The shift
## Every hard problem you'll hit with AI comes from one wrong assumption.
lead: You're treating it like software that either works or doesn't.

@narrate
I want to give you one idea in this lecture.

If you take nothing else from this course, take this. It explains almost every
argument you're going to have with engineering, every feature that slips, and
every launch that gets rolled back.

Here it is. The software you've shipped your whole career is deterministic. Same
input, same output. Every time. You learned to manage that kind of software, and
you got good at it.

Then you were handed something that does not behave that way at all. And nobody
told you the rules had changed.

@slide two-col
kicker: Two kinds of thing
## The difference that changes your job

::: cols
:: card
### A function
- Same input, same output, forever
- One test proves it works
- Bugs are defects to be fixed
- "Does it work?" has an answer
:: card
### A distribution
- Same input, a *range* of outputs
- One test proves almost nothing
- Bad outputs are the tail, not a defect
- "Does it work?" is the wrong question
:::

@narrate
Think about a checkout button. You click it, an order gets created. Click it a
thousand times, you get a thousand orders. If it fails, that's a defect. Someone
fixes it, and it's fixed.

Now think about a feature that summarises a support ticket. You run it on the
same ticket ten times and you get ten summaries. Most are good. One is excellent.
One quietly drops the detail that mattered most.

Nothing is broken. That is the system working as designed. You are not looking at
a machine that produces an answer. You are looking at a machine that produces a
spread of answers, and you happen to be seeing one draw from it.

The moment you were handed that, your job changed. Nobody sent the memo.

@slide bullets
kicker: The failure pattern
## How this shows up on your roadmap

1. The demo is spectacular, so the feature gets committed to
2. QA checks a handful of examples and passes it
3. Real users arrive with inputs nobody imagined
4. The bad tail becomes visible, loudly and in public
5. Everyone argues about whether it is "ready" — with no way to settle it

@narrate
Watch how predictably this plays out.

Someone builds a demo. It's genuinely impressive, because the happy path for
these systems is very good. Leadership sees it and the feature gets committed to
a quarter.

Then it reaches QA. And QA does what QA has always correctly done: tries some
examples, sees sensible answers, and passes it.

Then it ships. And real people arrive with inputs nobody thought of, at a volume
nobody simulated. Now the tail is visible. Not often. Just often enough that
support notices, then Twitter notices.

And here's the part that should bother you most. When the team sits down to argue
about whether the feature is ready, nobody can settle it. One person saw three
great outputs. Another saw two terrible ones. Both are telling the truth. There
is no shared instrument, so the loudest person wins.

@slide statement
kicker: The root cause
## You cannot write a checkbox for a probability.
lead: "The summary is accurate" is not a criterion. It's a hope with a checkbox next to it.

@narrate
The root cause is your acceptance criteria.

You've been writing them the same way for years, and the format is sound for
deterministic software. The summary is accurate. The classification is correct.
The user gets a helpful answer.

Read those again and ask a specific question: accurate on which input?

Because the honest answer is that it's accurate on *some* inputs and not others,
and the criterion doesn't say which, or how many, or which failures you'd accept.
So it can't be tested. It can only be argued about.

A criterion you cannot measure is not a criterion. It's a wish with a checkbox
next to it.

@slide two-col
kicker: The replacement
## Same intent. Now it's measurable.

::: cols
:: card bad
### What you write today
- "Summaries are accurate"
- "The tone is appropriate"
- "It doesn't make things up"
:: card good
### What you'll write by Section 3
- "≥ 92% pass on the 50-case golden set"
- "≥ 4/5 median tone score, no case below 3"
- "Zero unsupported claims on the 20-case citation subset"
:::

@narrate
So here's the replacement, and it's the spine of this whole course.

You pick a set of real inputs. Fifty is usually enough, and I'll show you exactly
how to choose them. You decide, in advance and in writing, what a good output
looks like for each one. That set becomes your instrument.

Then your acceptance criterion becomes a number on that instrument. Not "accurate"
— ninety-two percent pass rate on those fifty cases. Not "doesn't make things up"
— zero unsupported claims on the twenty cases where we specifically check
citations.

Now look at what you just gained. The criterion is testable. It can be re-run
automatically every time someone changes a prompt. Two people who disagree can go
look at the same number. And when someone asks "is it ready", that question has an
answer instead of a debate.

@slide callout
kicker: What this costs you
## The trade you're making

::: callout Be honest about this
Eval-based criteria are ==more work up front== than writing "the summary is
accurate". That is the entire cost. You buy it back the first time a launch
decision takes ten minutes instead of three meetings.
:::

@narrate
I want to be straight with you about the cost, because most courses skip this bit.

This is more work up front. Curating fifty cases and agreeing what good looks like
takes real hours, and it happens early, when everyone would rather be building.
You will feel that friction, and someone on your team will push back on it.

Here's the argument that wins. That work is not extra. It's the same work you were
always going to do, moved earlier. You were going to argue about quality
eventually. The only question is whether you argue with evidence or with
anecdotes, and whether it happens before launch or after rollback.

The teams that ship AI features reliably are not smarter. They just built the
instrument first.
