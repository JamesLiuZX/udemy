@slide statement
kicker: Section 0 · The shift
## Every hard problem you will hit with AI comes from one wrong assumption.
lead: You are treating it like software that either works or it doesn't.

@narrate
I want to give you one idea in this lecture.

If you take nothing else from this course, take this. It explains almost every
argument you're going to have with engineering, every feature that slips, and
every launch that gets rolled back.

Here it is. The software you've shipped your whole career is deterministic. Same
input, same output. Every time. You learned to manage that kind of software, and
you got good at it.

Then you were handed something that does not behave that way at all. And nobody
told you the rules had changed.

@slide figure
kicker: The distinction
## One input. Two completely different pictures.
figcap: A function returns the same output every time. An AI feature returns a draw from a range, and the range is the product.

```figure
kind: sampling
```

@narrate
Here are the two pictures side by side. I want you to hold this image, because
almost everything else in the course is a consequence of it.

On the left, the software you've shipped your whole career. Same input goes in,
one output comes out, and it's the same output every single time. That single dot
is why you could test it once and move on.

On the right, an AI feature. Same input goes in, and what comes out is one of
those scattered points. Run it again and you get a different one. Nothing is
broken on the right hand side. That scatter *is* the product working as designed.

Notice what that does to the word "it" when someone says "does it work". On the
left, "it" is a dot. On the right, "it" is a cloud, and you happen to be looking
at one point in it.

@slide definition
kicker: Stating it precisely

::: definition Key idea
A **deterministic function** returns the same output for a given input, every
time. An AI feature returns a *sample* from a range of possible outputs. The
range is the product. Any single answer you look at is one draw from it.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Run the same prompt on the same input ten times. Count how many outputs you would
have shipped. That fraction, not the best one, is your feature.
:::

@narrate
Let me make the distinction precise, because the whole course depends on it.

A deterministic function gives you the same output for a given input, every
single time. That's the checkout button. Click it a thousand times, you get a
thousand identical orders.

An AI feature gives you a sample from a range. The range is the actual product.
Any one answer you happen to be looking at is a single draw from it.

Here's how to feel that in about two minutes. Take a prompt you already use, run
it on the same input ten times, and count how many of those outputs you would
genuinely have been happy to ship. That fraction is your feature. Not the best
one you saw in the demo. The fraction.

Most people are surprised, and not in a good way.

@slide two-col
kicker: What changes for you
## Two kinds of thing, two kinds of management

::: cols
:: card
### A function
- Same input, same output, forever
- One test proves it works
- A bad result is a defect
- "Does it work?" has an answer
:: card
### A distribution
- Same input, a range of outputs
- One test proves almost nothing
- A bad result is the tail, not a defect
- "Does it work?" is the wrong question
:::

@narrate
Look at what that does to the way you manage the thing.

On the left, one test proves it works, because behaviour is repeatable. A bad
result is a defect: someone fixes it, and it stays fixed. And the question "does
it work" has a real answer.

On the right, none of that holds. One test proves almost nothing, because you saw
one draw. A bad result usually isn't a defect at all, it's the tail of the range
doing exactly what it does. And "does it work" stops being a question anyone can
answer honestly.

That's the whole problem. You're being asked a question that no longer has an
answer, using tools built for the column on the left.

@slide sidenote
kicker: How it plays out
## The pattern, every time

::: split
:: main
1. The demo is excellent, so the feature gets committed to
2. QA checks a handful of examples and passes it
3. Real users arrive with inputs nobody imagined
4. The bad tail becomes visible, loudly and in public
5. Everyone argues about readiness with no way to settle it
:: aside Why step 2 is not QA's fault
Sampling a few examples is exactly the right method for deterministic software.
It is the standard practice, correctly applied to the wrong kind of system.
:::

@narrate
Watch how predictably this plays out.

Someone builds a demo. It's genuinely impressive, because the happy path for
these systems is very good. Leadership sees it, and the feature gets committed to
a quarter.

Then it reaches QA, and QA does what QA has always correctly done: tries some
examples, sees sensible answers, passes it. I want to be clear that this is not a
failure of diligence. Sampling a few cases is the right method for deterministic
software. It's standard practice, applied correctly, to the wrong kind of system.

Then it ships. Real people arrive with inputs nobody thought of, at a volume
nobody simulated. Now the tail is visible. Not often. Just often enough that
support notices, and then the internet notices.

And here's the part that should bother you most. When the team sits down to argue
about whether it's ready, nobody can settle it. One person saw three great
outputs, another saw two terrible ones, and both are telling the truth. There's no
shared instrument, so the loudest person wins.

@slide statement
kicker: The root cause
## You cannot write a checkbox for a probability.
lead: "The summary is accurate" is not a criterion. It is a hope with a checkbox next to it.

@narrate
The root cause is your acceptance criteria.

You've been writing them the same way for years, and the format is sound for
deterministic software. The summary is accurate. The classification is correct.
The user gets a helpful answer.

Read those again and ask one specific question: accurate on which input?

Because the honest answer is that it's accurate on some inputs and not others, and
the criterion doesn't say which, or how many, or which failures you'd accept. So
it can't be tested. It can only be argued about.

A criterion you cannot measure is not a criterion.

@slide two-col
kicker: The replacement
## Same intent, now measurable

::: cols
:: card bad
### What you write today
- "Summaries are accurate"
- "The tone is appropriate"
- "It doesn't make things up"
:: card good
### What you write by Section 3
- "92% pass on the 50-case golden set"
- "Median tone 4 of 5, no case below 3"
- "Zero unsupported claims on the citation subset"
:::

@narrate
So here's the replacement, and it's the spine of this whole course.

You pick a set of real inputs. Fifty is usually enough, and I'll show you exactly
how to choose them. You decide, in advance and in writing, what a good output
looks like for each one. That set becomes your instrument.

Then your acceptance criterion becomes a number on that instrument. Not
"accurate", but ninety-two percent pass rate on those fifty cases. Not "doesn't
make things up", but zero unsupported claims on the twenty cases where you
specifically check citations.

Now look at what you just gained. The criterion is testable. It re-runs
automatically every time someone changes a prompt. Two people who disagree can go
and look at the same number. And when someone asks whether it's ready, that
question has an answer instead of a debate.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
Eval-based criteria cost more up front than writing "the summary is accurate".
That is the whole cost. You buy it back the first time a launch decision takes
==ten minutes instead of three meetings==.
:::

@narrate
I want to be straight with you about the cost, because most courses skip this bit.

This is more work up front. Curating fifty cases and agreeing what good looks
like takes real hours, and it happens early, when everyone would rather be
building. You will feel that friction, and someone on your team will push back on
it.

Here's the argument that wins. That work is not extra. It's the same work you were
always going to do, moved earlier. You were going to argue about quality
eventually. The only question is whether you argue with evidence or with
anecdotes, and whether it happens before launch or after a rollback.

The teams that ship AI features reliably are not smarter than yours. They built
the instrument first.

@slide figure
kicker: What a threshold looks like
## Your criterion is a line on this picture
figcap: Quality of 50 outputs, scored against a rubric. The criterion is not "accurate", it is where you put the line and how much must sit to the right of it.

```figure
kind: histogram
threshold: 4
threshold_label: "Pass mark"
bins:
  - {label: "1 · unusable", value: 2}
  - {label: "2 · poor", value: 5}
  - {label: "3 · borderline", value: 9}
  - {label: "4 · good", value: 19}
  - {label: "5 · excellent", value: 15}
note: "34 of 50 at or above the line. Pass rate 68%, which is nowhere near shippable for this feature."
```

@narrate
This is what an acceptance criterion actually looks like once you stop pretending
the feature is a function.

Fifty real inputs, each output scored against a rubric. The bars show how the
quality is distributed. And your criterion is the line: where you put it, and how
much of the distribution has to sit to the right of it.

In this picture, thirty-four of fifty outputs are at or above the pass mark. That's
a sixty-eight percent pass rate, and for most features that's nowhere near
shippable.

But look at what you can now do that you couldn't do before. You can say exactly
how far off you are. You can re-run this after a prompt change and see the bars
move. You can argue about where the line belongs, which is a productive argument,
instead of arguing about whether it "feels ready", which is not.

The grey bars on the left are also doing something useful. They aren't a mystery
any more. They're a list of specific inputs you can go and read.
