@slide statement
kicker: Section 9 · The dashboard nobody reads
## A fifteen-panel dashboard answers every question and gets read by nobody. Here's the one that actually changes a decision.
lead: The dashboard your team operates with and the dashboard leadership needs are two different documents, built for two different jobs.

@narrate
A team operating a feature day to day genuinely needs fifteen panels:
latency by percentile, cost by model, error rate by endpoint, every
number 4.5 through 8.6 taught you to watch. That dashboard is doing its
job correctly by being comprehensive.

Hand that same dashboard to a leadership review and watch it fail
completely, not because the numbers are wrong, but because
comprehensive and decision-ready are different qualities. Leadership
isn't operating the feature. They're deciding whether to fund it,
expand it, or pull it back, and that decision needs three or four
numbers, not fifteen. Which three would you actually defend in the
room?

This isn't a hypothetical mismatch. It's usually the first thing that
goes wrong when a feature moves from a scrappy team project to
something the rest of the company is watching. The instinct is to show
more, not less, because more feels more honest. It's usually the
opposite: more panels just give leadership more places to find whatever
story they already believed walking in.

Three or four numbers force a discipline fifteen never will: you have
to decide, in advance, which ones actually matter enough to survive the
cut. That decision is worth making deliberately, not by whichever
panels happened to get built first.

@slide table
kicker: Two documents, two jobs
## Built for operating, and built for deciding

| | The team's dashboard | The leadership dashboard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Panels | As many as operating requires | Three to five, no more |
| Refresh | Real time or near it | As often as the decision could change |
| Metrics | Every diagnostic signal | 9.1's adoption, acceptance, deflection |
| Job | Catch a problem early | Support one clear decision |

@narrate
Read the metrics row against 9.1, because it's not a coincidence. The
three metrics that lecture built specifically for a probabilistic
feature are exactly the ones dense enough in meaning to belong on a
one-page leadership view. Everything else in your team's fifteen panels
exists to help you diagnose why one of those three moved, not to be
read by someone deciding whether to keep funding the feature.

Refresh cadence matters more than it looks like it should. A leadership
dashboard refreshing every minute doesn't help anyone make a monthly
decision, it just adds noise between the meetings that actually matter.
Match the refresh rate to the decision cycle, not to what your
infrastructure happens to be capable of.

These two documents can share a source of truth without sharing a
screen. The same instrumentation from 9.2 feeds both. The team's
version queries it in detail, sliced by endpoint and percentile. The
leadership version rolls the same underlying events up to three
numbers: one pipeline, two honest views.

Ask which document you're actually looking at before trusting either
one's silence. A quiet number on the team's dashboard might mean
nothing's wrong. The same quiet number missing entirely from the
leadership version might just mean it didn't make the cut, a different
and much less reassuring kind of quiet.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
A dashboard that only ever shows good news isn't informative any more.
==It's stopped reporting and started performing==, the same trap 8.1
warned about for a stale risk register.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because the pressure on a leadership dashboard
runs one direction: make it look good, quietly drop the panel that
doesn't.

A trustworthy leadership dashboard has to be allowed to show a number
that's currently bad, with a sentence next to it saying what's being
done about it. The version that only ever shows green gets believed
right up until the quarter it can't hide the real number any longer,
and every green quarter before that one loses its credibility
retroactively.

This is the same discipline as 9.1's acceptance-versus-engagement
pairing, aimed at an entire dashboard instead of one metric. A
flattering number is easy to produce by choosing what to measure and
when. A dashboard that's allowed to look bad occasionally is the only
kind actually reporting reality rather than managing an impression of
it.

The instinct to omit rarely feels like dishonesty. It feels like
editing: choosing the story worth fifteen minutes of a leadership
meeting. The real test is whether the bad number would still make the
cut if it moved the wrong way, not just when it's flattering. Naming it
is where credibility actually gets built: a team that surfaces its own
weak quarter, with a plan attached, earns more trust than one whose
numbers are suspiciously smooth every time.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition A leadership dashboard
Three to five numbers, refreshed on the decision's own cadence, honest
enough to show a bad one, built to support a specific choice rather
than to demonstrate general thoroughness.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Look at the last dashboard you presented to leadership. Count the
panels. If it's more than five, ask which three actually drove the
decision in the room.
:::

@narrate
That's the standard worth building toward, and the exercise usually
lands quickly, because most people already know which panel got looked
at and which ones were scrolled past.

Cut to those three or four honestly, even if it means retiring a chart
you're proud of. A dashboard's value was never the count of panels on
it. It was always whether the room left knowing what to decide.

Do this exercise even if you weren't the one who built the dashboard.
Most people sitting in a leadership review can tell you exactly which
number the room actually reacted to, and it's almost always one of
three or four, buried somewhere among a dozen others competing for the
same attention.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Cut your leadership dashboard down to 9.1's three metrics, plus one
2. Check whether it's ever shown a genuinely bad number

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Cut the dashboard first. Adoption, acceptance, deflection, and one cost
or margin number from Section seven, on one page.

Then check its history honestly. If every quarter has looked fine,
that's worth a second look before you trust the next one, not a reason
to feel reassured.

Both of these are edits, not rebuilds. The data already exists from
9.2's instrumentation. This is a smaller, more honest view of it, not a
new project.
