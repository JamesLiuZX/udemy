## The leaderboard doesn't know your feature's actual bar. Your own scorecard does.
lead: Three numbers, one page, and a rule for reading them in the right order.

@narrate
Section two taught you to read a benchmark table critically. Here's
what replaces it when an actual decision is on the table: your own
golden set's pass rate, your own cost model's number, and a latency
figure, side by side, for every model you're genuinely considering.

Skepticism about benchmarks was always meant to lead somewhere, not
just leave you doubting every number you're handed. This is where it
leads: build the table yourself, from your own evidence, instead of
distrusting someone else's and having nothing to replace it with.

A public benchmark tells you how a model performs on someone else's
task. Your scorecard tells you how it performs on yours, at what it
actually costs you, and how long a real user actually waits. That's
the document worth building before anyone commits to a model, not the
leaderboard screenshot from the announcement post.

This is the natural endpoint of three separate threads this course has
been building since Section two: reading a benchmark critically, so you
know what it isn't telling you; building a golden set, so you have
something better to score against; and the cost model from two lectures
ago, so price sits on the same page as quality instead of arriving as a
surprise afterward.

@slide table
kicker: A worked scorecard
## Three real candidates, scored on your golden set, not a leaderboard

| Model | Pass rate (your golden set) | Cost per call | p95 latency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flagship | 94% | 8 cents | 3.2s |
| Mid-tier | 89% | 2 cents | 1.4s |
| Budget | 71% | Half a cent | 0.6s |

@narrate
Read this table in a specific order, because reading it left to right,
model by model, is how teams talk themselves into the wrong row.

Read the pass rate column first, alone, against whatever floor Section
three's acceptance criteria actually set for this feature. If that
floor is eighty-five percent, the budget model is gone before its
price or its speed matter at all. Seventy-one percent isn't a cheaper,
slower version of an acceptable answer. It's a model that fails the
threshold, and no amount of savings on the other two columns changes
that.

Notice, too, that the pass rate in this table isn't a number you look
up. It's the same fifty-case golden set from Section four, the same
rubric, run three separate times, once per candidate model, under
identical conditions. Nothing else in this table means anything if that
first column was measured inconsistently between rows.

Identical conditions is doing more work in that sentence than it looks
like. Same prompt, same cases, same rubric, same judge, whether that
judge is a person or the calibrated LLM-as-judge from Section four.
Change any one of those between rows and the pass rate column stops
being a comparison and becomes three unrelated numbers that happen to
share a table.

@slide two-col
kicker: The order that actually matters
## Disqualify on quality first. Compare cost and speed only after

::: cols
:: card bad
### Comparing all three columns at once
- "Budget is eighty percent cheaper" sounds decisive on its own
- Buries a real quality failure under an attractive price
- The cheapest disqualified model still looks like a bargain
:: card good
### Quality floor first, then trade off
- Anything below the floor is removed before cost enters the picture
- Flagship versus mid-tier becomes the real remaining decision
- Five points of quality against four times the cost, argued honestly
:::

@narrate
Notice what the floor actually buys you: it turns a three-way
comparison into a two-way one, and a cleaner argument in the room where
this gets decided.

Once the budget model is out, the real question is this: do flagship's
five extra points of pass rate justify four times the cost and double
the latency over mid-tier? That's a genuine trade-off, worth an actual
conversation, and a completely different one from accidentally
comparing a disqualified model against two that cleared the bar.

There's no universal right answer to that trade-off, and this lecture
won't pretend there is one. A feature where a wrong answer is
expensive, the kind of blast radius Section six spent a whole lecture
naming, leans toward paying for the five points. A feature where
speed is the actual product experience, and both models already clear
the bar comfortably, leans the other way. The scorecard's job is
making that a visible choice, not making it for you.

Write the reasoning down alongside the choice, not just the choice
itself. "We picked flagship because the failure cost outweighs four
times the spend" is a decision someone can revisit intelligently in six
months. "We picked flagship" on its own is a decision nobody can argue
with or defend, because the actual reasoning behind it left the room
the moment the meeting ended.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
A model that wins your scorecard today may not win it next quarter.
==Providers update pricing and capability constantly==, so this is a
recheck, not a one-time decision.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because a scorecard built once and treated as
permanent quietly goes stale in exactly the way a corpus does if nobody
revisits it.

Pricing changes. New models ship. The mid-tier option that won this
quarter's comparison might be beaten outright by something released
next month, at a lower price, that didn't exist when you built this
table. Put a date on every scorecard, and a reminder to rerun it,
because the model you locked in eight months ago is not guaranteed to
still be the right row.

This is also the argument for keeping your golden set genuinely
reusable rather than a one-time artifact. Rerunning the scorecard
against a new model release should take an afternoon, not a fresh
round of case selection, because the fifty cases and the rubric already
exist. Treat the golden set as permanent infrastructure and the
recheck becomes cheap enough that people actually do it.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition Model selection scorecard
Every genuine candidate scored on your own golden set's pass rate, your
own cost model's per-call number, and measured latency, with anything
below your quality floor removed before cost or speed are compared.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Score two real candidate models against your own golden set. If you
only have benchmark numbers for one of them, that's the gap this
lecture exists to close.
:::

@narrate
That's the document worth building, and the definition's last clause
does the real work: below the floor, removed, before anything else gets
compared.

Try it on a real decision if one's in front of you. Most people
building this for the first time discover they've been comparing
benchmark numbers for one model against a gut feeling for another,
which isn't a comparison at all, just two different kinds of guess
sitting next to each other.

The fix is rarely dramatic. It's usually one afternoon spent running
the same fifty cases through the second model, so the comparison is
finally apples to apples instead of a benchmark screenshot against a
vague impression from using it once.

## One thing worth doing now

Download **A10, the model selection scorecard**, confirm your quality floor first, and score two real candidates against it

@narrate
One thing before you move on.

Download the template and confirm the floor first, in writing, before
any cost number goes in the sheet. Write it down even if it feels
obvious, because "obvious" thresholds have a way of quietly shifting
once a cheap, fast option is sitting right there in the same table
looking tempting. Then run at least two real candidates through it,
using your own golden set, not a leaderboard.

every row of this scorecard and the feature still loses money per
