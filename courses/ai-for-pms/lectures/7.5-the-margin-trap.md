@slide quote
kicker: Section 7 · The trap
attrib: What the engagement dashboard will never tell you

> "Your most engaged users can be your least profitable ones."

@narrate
Every engagement dashboard you've ever seen treats more usage as
unambiguously good. Traditional software costs almost nothing per extra
use, an extra page view or an extra saved document, so a feature that
gets used more is simply a feature that succeeded, full stop. AI
features don't, and that one difference changes what "success" means.
Every single use has a real, measurable cost, the one you built in 7.2,
and that cost scales up exactly when usage does.

That's the trap this lecture names. A feature can pass every row of
last lecture's scorecard, launch with a healthy margin, and still lose
money on its most active users the moment they use it enough. Nobody
put that number in the launch deck, because nobody was looking at cost
and revenue on the same page.

Picture the support-ticket summariser this course keeps returning to,
scaled up to the desk that clears the most tickets a month. That desk
looks like the retention win on every dashboard, right up until the
same feature's cost line quietly crosses its allocated revenue for
exactly that user. Most teams are still running the old, safe intuition
about what heavy usage means.

@slide table
kicker: The same feature, three usage tiers
## Watch the margin cross zero as usage goes up

| Tier | Conversations/month | Cost | Allocated revenue | Margin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light user | 2 | 8 cents | 5.00 | +4.92 |
| Heavy user | 40 | 1.68 | 5.00 | +3.32 |
| Power user | 150 | 6.30 | 5.00 | -1.30 |

@narrate
Read the margin column down, not the cost column, because the cost
column looks fine at every single row. Eight cents, a dollar sixty
eight, six dollars thirty, all individually small numbers that don't
sound alarming on their own.

The margin column tells a different story. Somewhere between forty and
a hundred and fifty conversations a month, this feature crosses from
genuinely profitable to actively losing money, on the exact users a
product team would normally celebrate as its best. That crossover
point is the actual finding. Everything else in this table is just
arithmetic leading up to it.

Find your own crossover point rather than borrowing this one. It moves
with your actual per-conversation cost and your actual allocated
revenue, and a feature with thinner margins per user or a higher cost
per conversation will cross into loss much earlier than a hundred and
fifty. The shape of the table matters more than these specific three
rows: cost climbing steadily while revenue holds flat, until one
crosses the other.

kicker: The metric conflict this creates
## Two dashboards, same user, opposite verdicts

::: cols
:: card bad
### The engagement dashboard
- Power user: 150 conversations, most active user in the segment
- Flagged as a retention success story
- Nobody on this dashboard sees a cost number at all
:: card good
### The margin model
- Same power user: 6.30 dollars in real cost against 5 of allocated revenue
- Flagged as a $1.30 monthly loss, growing with every extra conversation
- The number that actually determines whether this user is good for the business
:::

@narrate
Both dashboards are describing the identical user, honestly, from the
data each one was built to show. Neither is lying. They're just
answering different questions, and only one of them is the question a
margin review actually needs answered.

That's worth sitting with, because it means nobody has to be careless
or dishonest for this trap to spring. Every dashboard involved is doing
exactly what it was built to do. The failure is architectural, not
personal: a metrics system with no page where usage and cost sit next
to the same user.

This is precisely why the two numbers need to live on the same page,
the way last lecture's scorecard put quality, cost, and speed together
instead of leaving them in three separate documents nobody cross-
references. An engagement win that's quietly a margin loss stays
invisible for exactly as long as those two numbers never meet.

Who in your organisation actually owns that meeting point today?
Product usually owns the engagement dashboard. Finance usually owns the
margin conversation, if anyone does, and often only discovers it
quarterly, long after a cohort of power users has already formed. The
gap between those two owners is exactly where this trap survives
undetected.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
The fix is not discouraging usage. ==Design pricing or limits that scale
with cost==, the same way the underlying cost already scales with usage.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat clearly, because the tempting wrong conclusion
here is that heavy usage is the problem. It isn't. Heavy usage proved
the feature works. The problem is a flat-rate structure that was never
designed to track a cost that isn't flat.

The real fix looks like tiered plans, a usage-based add-on past a
threshold, or a generous-but-real cap with a clear upgrade path, not a
feature quietly punished for succeeding. Whichever fix you pick,
design it before the power-user tier exists in large numbers, because
retrofitting pricing onto users who are already used to unlimited is a
far harder conversation than shipping it with the launch.

Frame the cap generously enough that it never touches a genuinely
typical user, and specifically enough that the crossover point from
this lecture's table sits comfortably above it. A cap set from a guess
protects nothing. A cap set from your own measured crossover protects
the feature's economics without most users ever noticing it exists.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition The margin trap
The point where an AI feature's real, scaling cost per user exceeds
its allocated revenue per user, invisible to engagement metrics because
they track usage, not cost.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Take your heaviest real user's actual usage and run it through your own
cost model. If the honest number surprises you, that's this lecture's
finding, not a hypothetical one.
:::

@narrate
That's the definition worth carrying past today, and it's worth
carrying specifically because it's counterintuitive. More usage is
supposed to mean more success. For a feature with real marginal cost,
past a certain point, it can mean the opposite.

Run the exercise on someone real, not a hypothetical average. Averages
hide exactly this crossover, because they blend your light users and
your power users into one comfortable-looking number that no single
real person actually has.

A blended average margin that looks healthy can be sitting directly on
top of a profitable majority and a loss-making tail, and the average
alone will never tell you the tail exists. Segment by actual usage
before you trust any single margin number presented as the whole
picture.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Find your heaviest real user's usage and compute their actual margin
2. Check whether your pricing or limits scale with cost at all today

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Find a real power user, not an estimate, and run their actual usage
through your cost model. If nobody can produce that number quickly,
that's itself the finding: cost and engagement have never been checked
against each other for this feature.

Then look honestly at today's pricing or usage limits. If neither one
moves as usage climbs, that gap is worth raising in the next planning
conversation, in plain terms: the feature's cost scales with success,
and nothing in how it's priced currently reflects that.

This section's next lecture, latency budgets, will matter to you
either way, but this margin question is the one worth raising first,
before a power-user cohort makes it an expensive conversation instead
of a cheap one.
