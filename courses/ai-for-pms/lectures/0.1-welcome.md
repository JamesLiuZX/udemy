# You are accountable for an AI feature you cannot confidently judge.
lead: That is the real problem. This course fixes exactly that.

@narrate
If you're here, I'd guess something like this happened.

An AI feature landed on your roadmap. Maybe you asked for it, maybe it arrived
from above. And somewhere between the demo and the launch date you realised you
had no reliable way to tell whether the thing actually works, and that everyone in
the room was quietly waiting for you to say.

That's not a gap in your intelligence. It's a gap in the toolkit. The methods you
were trained on assume software that behaves the same way twice. This doesn't.

## What changes for you

::: cols
:: card bad
### Before
- "It seems to work well"
- Quality debates settled by seniority
- Cost discovered on the invoice
- Risk raised by legal, late
:: card good
### After
- "94% pass, three known failure modes"
- Quality settled by a shared number
- Cost modelled before a line is written
- Risk registered before kickoff
:::

@narrate
Here's the concrete change.

Right now, the honest version of your status update is "it seems to work well". By
the end of this course you'll be saying "ninety-four percent pass rate on our
golden set, three known failure modes, and here's the one I'd hold the launch
for."

You'll settle quality arguments with a number everyone can go and look at. You'll
know what the feature costs per user before anyone writes code, rather than when
the invoice arrives. And you'll have surfaced the legal and safety issues at
kickoff, not in the week you were hoping to ship.

kicker: How this course is built
## Three commitments

1. **You build, not just watch.** Eleven artifacts you will use at work
2. **No tool worship.** The frameworks outlive whichever model is winning
3. **The trade-offs stay in.** Including where this approach costs you

@narrate
Three commitments about how this course is built.

First, you build things. There are eleven downloadable artifacts, and they're real
work deliverables: a spec, an eval harness, a cost model, a risk register. You'll
finish with a portfolio, not notes.

Second, I've kept this deliberately light on specific tools. Tools churn every few
months. Judgement doesn't. What you learn here will still be true after the next
three model releases.

Third, I've left the trade-offs in. Where this approach is expensive, or slow, or
overkill, I say so. You're going to have to defend these ideas to a skeptical
engineer, and you can't do that from a sales pitch.
