## Twenty minutes, one real feature, five specific findings instead of one vague reassurance.
lead: Not a security audit. Everything this section built, run against something you actually ship, the same bar as every workshop this course has run.

@narrate
Pick the feature you're most confident about, not your simplest one.
Confidence untested is exactly what this workshop exists to check. Open
A11, and give this the same twenty minutes every workshop in this
course has asked for.

The bar is identical too. Not perfect. Complete. Five specific findings,
one per category, even where the honest finding is "measured, and it's
fine," which is a completely different, far more valuable sentence than
"probably fine, never checked."

Red-teaming your own feature feels adversarial, and it should. You are
looking for the thing that's wrong, not confirming the thing that's
right. That's a different mindset from most product work, and it's
worth naming before you start, so the exercise doesn't quietly turn
into a victory lap.

This is also the only lecture in this section that asks you to attack
your own feature rather than assess it. Assessment can be generous by
accident. An actual attempted injection either works or it doesn't, and
there's no comfortable middle ground to retreat into.

@slide table
kicker: How to spend the twenty minutes
## Five lectures, five pacing blocks

| Minutes | Covers | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-4 | Try to break it: one direct, one indirect injection attempt, 8.2 | Did you actually try it, not just imagine it |
| 4-8 | The three data questions: PII, residency, training rights, 8.3 | A real answer, not a comfortable assumption |
| 8-12 | Sort it into the EU AI Act's four tiers, honestly, 8.4 | Start from consequence to a person, not the tech |
| 12-16 | One fairness slice: can your golden set measure it, 8.5 | A real dimension, not a generic checklist |
| 16-20 | Detection and kill switch, tested or assumed, 8.6 | Tested beats assumed, every single time |

@narrate
Notice the first block asks you to actually try an injection, not
discuss whether your feature is vulnerable to one in the abstract.
Write one sentence that looks like ordinary content and see whether it
changes what your feature does. That's the entire test, and it takes
four minutes.

The second block moves through three separate questions fast, and
that's fine as long as all three genuinely get answered rather than
folded into one blended "we're careful with data" sentence. Trace one
real request for the first, open the actual vendor setting for the
third, and resist answering all three with the same comfortable
paragraph.

The middle block, the EU AI Act tier, moves fast for most features and
that's fine. Landing in minimal or limited risk in under four minutes
is a genuine finding, not a block you rushed through. The feature that
takes the full four minutes to sort is the one worth a second look.

Minutes twelve through sixteen ask a question you can only answer by
opening your actual golden set, not by reasoning about it. Count the
cases in your chosen segment. If the honest count is close to zero,
that's the finding, immediately, no further analysis needed.

The last block closes the loop back to 8.1's very first row: nobody
owns what happens when it breaks. By minute twenty, that row should
have an actual name next to it, or an honest blank you're committing to
fill by a specific date.

@slide quote
"An uploaded PDF's text can currently reach the system prompt unescaped."
attrib: 8.1's example of a finding specific enough to actually close

@narrate
Recognise this shape. It's 8.1's own example of what a closeable
finding sounds like, and it's roughly what each of your five findings
today should read like by minute twenty.

Notice what the sentence contains: the exact input path, named,
specific enough that an engineer reading it knows exactly what to fix
without asking a single follow-up question. Notice what it isn't:
"prompt injection is a risk we should think about," the vague version
this exact sentence was written to replace back in 8.1.

Hold every one of today's five findings to this standard. "PII handling
seems fine" is not a finding. "Support ticket logs retain the caller's
phone number for ninety days with no redaction" is. Only the second one
tells anyone what to actually go fix.

Compare it, too, against the register row it replaced, the one 8.1
opened with: "prompt injection is a risk we should think about," owned
by no one, reviewed whenever someone remembered. Twenty minutes and one
honest attempt turned that sentence into this one. That's the entire
distance this workshop is asking you to cover, five separate times.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
Finding nothing in a category isn't the goal to chase. ==A specific,
honest "not yet measured" beats a vague "should be fine"==, in every
single one of these five blocks.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because the instinct under time pressure is to
write the reassuring answer rather than the true one, and this workshop
is specifically testing your resistance to that instinct.

A clean pass in all five categories is a wonderful outcome, if it's
actually earned by checking. The same five clean boxes, filled in from
confidence rather than a check, are worse than useless, because they'll
be trusted exactly as much as if they were real.

If minute twenty arrives and a block is genuinely unfinished, write
"not yet measured" and move on. That sentence, honestly written, is
worth more to whoever reads this afterward than a guess dressed up as a
result.

This is worth saying twice in one section, because it's the exact same
caveat 8.1 opened with, aimed at yourself this time instead of a
hypothetical teammate. Under time pressure, in your own workshop, the
temptation to write the reassuring row is just as real as it was
reading about someone else's.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition A red-team pass
Five specific findings, one per category this section covered, each
either a real gap named precisely or a real check confirmed, never a
comfortable guess wearing the shape of either one.
:::

::: example What to do when you finish early
Reread only the injection block. If you didn't actually attempt a real
crafted input, the block isn't done, whatever else it says.
:::

@narrate
That's today's bar, held to the same standard as every workshop before
this one: attempted honestly, not performed convincingly.

If you finish with time left, don't polish evenly. Return to whichever
block you moved through fastest, because speed in this specific
exercise is usually a sign of an assumption sliding through unchecked,
not genuine confidence earned by a check.

Resist the urge to write a summary paragraph at the end declaring the
feature safe. That sentence belongs to nobody, and it undoes the entire
point of five specific findings by replacing them with one vague one.

Most people running this exercise for the first time find at least one
block harder than expected, usually the one they were most confident
about walking in. That's not a bad result. It's the entire reason a
red-team pass is worth twenty minutes instead of a five-second gut
check in a hallway.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Finish all five blocks, with at least one honestly attempted test
2. Turn every real gap into a dated, owned row in 8.1's register

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Finish all five blocks for real, including the parts that feel
unnecessary for a feature you're confident in. Confidence is exactly
what a red-team exercise is built to test, not assume.

Then take every gap you found, however small, and give it a name, an
owner, and a date in 8.1's register. A red-team pass that produces
findings nobody follows up on is theatre. One that produces five dated
rows is this section actually working.

None of this needs to happen today for every feature you own, only for
the one you actually opened A11 against this session. Repeat the pass
on your next feature before it ships, not after, and the twenty minutes
gets faster every time you run it.

built, and how directly it plugs into analytics next, where you'll
learn to measure whether a feature is actually succeeding, not just
