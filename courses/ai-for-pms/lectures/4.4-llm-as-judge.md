@slide statement

## An AI can score your fifty cases in nine seconds. That speed is exactly why you shouldn't trust it yet.
lead: It has to earn the same trust a human reviewer did, and it lies in specific, learnable ways.

@narrate
Everything in the last two lectures took real hours: reading transcripts,
arguing about wording, running a calibration loop by hand. Here's the
tempting shortcut. Hand your rubric and your fifty cases to an AI model, and
it scores all of them in under a minute, for pennies.

That speed is real, and for most teams it's eventually the right call. But
notice what you just did. You handed judgement to a system built from the
same technology you're trying to evaluate, and asked it to grade its own
kind of homework.

Would you trust a student grading their own exam without checking the
answer key first? That's the position you just put the model in. It's not
disqualifying. It's a reason to check, not to trust outright. An LLM
judge doesn't lie randomly. It lies in a small number of specific,
well-documented ways, the kind researchers have measured across thousands
of comparisons, and once you know what they are, they're straightforward to
catch.

This isn't a reason to skip straight back to scoring everything by hand
either. Section 4 has already cost you real hours. The judge is how those
hours stop scaling linearly with how much traffic you have.

@slide table
kicker: How it lies
## Two specific, well-documented failure modes

| Bias | What actually happens | Quick check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Length bias | Longer answers score higher, padding included | Compare scores against word count |
| Position bias | Whichever answer is shown first scores higher | Swap the order, rerun, compare |

@narrate
Two are worth knowing before you use this on anything real.

Length bias: judge models consistently rate longer answers higher, even
when the extra words add nothing. Politeness, hedging, restating the
question, all of it reads as thoroughness to a model pattern-matching on
what confident answers usually look like.

Position bias shows up when you ask a judge to compare two outputs side by
side. Swap which one appears first, and on a meaningful fraction of cases,
the verdict flips with it. The judge isn't re-weighing the arguments. It's
showing a mild preference for whichever it read first.

Neither is a reason to abandon the idea. Both have a five-minute check: does
the score track word count more than it tracks the rubric, and does the
verdict survive being run again with the order swapped.

A third is worth a mention even though it's harder to check yourself: judges
tend to favour answers formatted with headings and bullet points over plain
prose making the identical argument. If your rubric never mentioned
formatting, and the judge is rewarding it anyway, that preference is coming
from the judge, not from you.

@slide metrics
kicker: Length bias, in one comparison
## Same information, one padded, one not

::: metrics
- 41 words :: The concise, correct summary :: good
- 3 of 5 :: LLM judge's score for it :: bad
- 118 words :: The same facts, restated three times ::
- 5 of 5 :: LLM judge's score for that one :: bad
:::

@narrate
Here's the bias caught in the act, on the ticket-summary case you already
know.

Two summaries, same underlying facts: charge, refund, deadline, likely
cause. One is forty-one words, direct, nothing extra. The other is a
hundred and eighteen words, the same four facts, restated with a longer
opening and a longer close.

Scored against your six-question rubric by a human, they tie. Every fact
that needs to survive, survives, in both. Scored by an unchecked LLM judge,
the padded version wins outright: five out of five against three out of
five.

Nothing about the padded version is more correct. It just sounds more
thorough, and "sounds thorough" is precisely the pattern a length-biased
judge rewards. Ship on that judge's numbers alone and you'd be optimising
your feature to get wordier, not better.

@slide sidenote
kicker: Making it trustworthy
## Run the same loop as last lecture, human against machine

::: split
:: main
1. Score fifteen cases yourself, using your calibrated rubric
2. Have the LLM judge score the identical fifteen, blind to your scores
3. Compare. Below about 85% agreement, don't ship the judge yet
:: aside Nothing new to learn here
This is the exact calibration protocol from last lecture. The only change
is that one of your two reviewers is now a model instead of a colleague.
:::

@narrate
You already own the tool for this. It's the calibration loop from last
lecture, run once more with a different second reviewer.

Score fifteen real cases yourself, using the rubric you already calibrated.
Have the model score the same fifteen, without seeing your verdicts.
Compare, case by case, the same way you compared against your colleague.

Clear roughly eighty-five percent agreement, and you've earned the right to
let it score the other thirty-five, and every future release after that.
Fall short, and look at where it broke: if the disagreements cluster on
your longer outputs, you've found length bias, not a broken judge.

This is also the one honest way to know a judge is ready. Not a vendor's
benchmark. Your rubric, on your cases, checked against you.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
An LLM judge is cheap per case, but never free, and it drifts. The provider
updates the underlying model without asking you, and a judge that agreed
with you in March can quietly disagree by June. ==Re-run the validation
loop== every time the judge model changes, not just once.
:::

@narrate
Say the cost plainly.

Per case, an LLM judge is close to free next to a human afternoon. But
"validated once" is not the same claim as "validated". The model behind
your judge gets updated on a schedule you don't control and usually aren't
told about, and an update can shift how it scores without changing a single
word of your rubric.

That's the same drift idea from earlier in this section, aimed at a
different target. There, production traffic moved and your golden set
didn't. Here, the judge itself moves and your validation doesn't.

The fix costs almost nothing: rerun the fifteen-case check against your
judge every time you notice its provider ships an update, or on a calendar,
whichever comes first. Treat "validated" as a date-stamped claim, not a
permanent one.

Put the date on the number itself when you report it. "Ninety-four percent,
validated against a human rubric on the third of March" survives a hard
question in a review meeting. A bare "ninety-four percent" invites one.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition LLM-as-judge
Using a language model to score outputs against your rubric instead of a
human. Trustworthy exactly to the extent you've measured its agreement with
a calibrated human reviewer, on your cases, recently.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Take fifteen cases you already scored by hand. Ask a model to score the
same fifteen against your rubric, one case per prompt. Sort the
disagreements by word count before you look at anything else.
:::

@narrate
Notice the word doing the work again: recently. Not "we checked this once".
Trustworthy is a claim with a shelf life.

The exercise reuses cases you've already scored, which is deliberate.
You're not starting a new curation project, you're pointing a second
reviewer at work you've already done. One afternoon, fifteen cases, a
model, and a rubric it's never seen before.

Sort the disagreements by word count first, before you read a single one.
If they cluster at the long end, you've found your bias before it found
you, and you know exactly which slide to come back to.

If they don't cluster anywhere obvious, that's a genuinely good sign. It
means the disagreements are ordinary judgement calls rather than a pattern
you can name, which is exactly what you'd expect from two competent
reviewers who occasionally see things differently.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## One thing worth doing now

Run your calibrated rubric against an LLM judge on the same fifteen cases, and sort any disagreements by word count first

@narrate
One thing before you move on.

Take the fifteen cases from last lecture's calibration and add one more
reviewer: a model, scoring blind, against the same rubric. You already have
everything else you need. If the agreement isn't there yet, sort the
disagreements by word count before you do anything cleverer. It's the
single most common cause, and checking it first will save you an afternoon
of chasing the wrong explanation.

Next lecture, we stop scoring one batch of fifty and start watching the
number over time: pass rate, regression testing, and the dashboard that
tells you the moment a change makes things worse, instead of a week later.
