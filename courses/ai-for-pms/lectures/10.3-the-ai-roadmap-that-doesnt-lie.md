@slide statement
kicker: Section 10 · The document everyone believes
## A roadmap that promises a ship date for a capability nobody's tested yet isn't optimism. It's a guess wearing a calendar.
lead: A roadmap for a deterministic feature can honestly commit to a date before the work starts. An AI feature's roadmap usually can't, and most still pretend it can.

@narrate
A checkout redesign's scope is knowable in advance. Nobody needs to
discover whether it's technically possible to add a shipping field.
An AI feature is different in one specific way: whether ninety-five
percent accuracy is achievable for this task is a genuine unknown until
someone's actually built the eval and run it, and a roadmap that puts a
firm date on that unknown is committing to a number nobody has earned
yet.

That's the lie in the title, and it's rarely told on purpose. It's
told by a template that has one column for "when" and no column for
"validated," inherited from years of roadmaps for features that never
needed that second column at all.

Notice this isn't an argument against roadmaps, or against giving dates
at all. Plenty of AI roadmap items genuinely are knowable in advance,
once the discovery sprint's already run and the threshold's already
real. The problem is never the date itself. It's a date that arrived
before the evidence that should have produced it.

@slide two-col
kicker: Same feature, two roadmap philosophies
## Committing to a date first, and committing to evidence first

::: cols
:: card bad
### Date-first
- "Contract review AI, ships Q3, 95% accurate"
- The 95% was never measured, only hoped for
- Q3 arrives with a feature that clears 80%, and a hard conversation
:: card good
### Evidence-first
- "Contract review AI: discovery sprint by March, date set after"
- The threshold gets set from 10.1's sprint, not guessed at kickoff
- The eventual date is real, because it was never invented early
:::

@narrate
Notice the good column doesn't refuse to commit, it just commits to
the right thing at the right time. A discovery sprint by a specific
date is a real, checkable promise. A ship date and an accuracy number
for a capability nobody's measured is two guesses stacked on top of
each other, presented as one confident line on a slide.

Notice, too, that the evidence-first line isn't vaguer, it's just
honest about which part is known today. "Discovery sprint by March" is
exactly as specific a commitment as "ships Q3." The only difference is
which fact it's actually willing to promise, and the evidence-first
version never promises a number it hasn't earned yet.

The bad column's Q3 conversation is the expensive one, and it's
entirely avoidable. Nobody enjoys explaining why a promised ninety-five
percent arrived at eighty. Almost everybody accepts "we're running the
sprint in March, and we'll have a real number and a real date by April"
without complaint, because it's honest about what's actually known
right now.

@slide example
kicker: Picture the actual meeting
::: example The bad column's Q3
Someone built to a promised number that was never real, spent real
engineering time getting as close as they could, and now has to
explain a gap that was baked in from the day the roadmap line was
written, months before anyone could have known better.
:::

@narrate
Sit with that meeting specifically, because it's the one every
date-first roadmap is quietly scheduling months in advance without
naming it.

@slide table
kicker: What a roadmap line actually needs
## Four columns, not two

| Column | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| Feature | The idea, named plainly |
| Sprint status | Not started, in progress, or cleared, from 10.1 |
| Threshold | The real number from the sprint, not a guess |
| Date | Only set once the threshold exists |

@narrate
Read the order left to right, because it's the actual sequence, not
just a table layout. What happens if you fill the date column before
the threshold column has anything in it? Exactly the trap this lecture
opened with, just formatted differently: sprint status before
threshold, threshold before date, never the other way round.

This table is also where 9.1's metrics stop being abstract and become
the specific number a roadmap answers to. The threshold column isn't
"good performance," it's adoption, acceptance, or deflection, at a
number someone measured, the same discipline the whole back half of
this course has been building toward one section at a time.

Notice, too, that this table doesn't ask for more total information
than a normal roadmap line, just the same information in an order that
makes an unmeasured commitment visible instead of hidden. A line with
"in progress" in the sprint-status column and nothing in threshold is
honest and incomplete, which is a completely different, much safer
state than a line with a confident-looking date and an invented number.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
An evidence-first roadmap looks less impressive on a single slide.
==Leadership often wants the date more than they want the honesty==,
and that pressure is real, not imagined.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because pretending this trade-off doesn't exist
helps nobody walking into a real roadmap review next quarter.

A roadmap full of specific dates reads as more confident than one with
"date set after the sprint" next to half its rows, even when the first
one is substantially less true. This is 10.2's calibration principle
again, aimed at an entire quarter instead of one stakeholder
conversation: the honest version costs you something in the room today,
and pays you back the day a date would otherwise have quietly slipped.

Resist the urge to solve this by quietly filling the threshold column
with an optimistic guess just to make the row look finished. That's the
exact failure this lecture is naming, now hiding one column deeper. An
empty threshold cell is an honest state. A guessed one dressed up as
measured is the same hedge from 10.2, wearing a spreadsheet instead of
a sentence.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition A roadmap that doesn't lie
Every AI item carries a sprint status, a threshold sourced from real
evidence, and a date set only once that threshold exists, never a date
and a number both guessed at the same meeting.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Pull your current roadmap. Find one AI item with a date and an accuracy
number attached. Check honestly whether either one was ever actually
measured.
:::

@narrate
That's the standard worth holding the next roadmap review to, and the
exercise is worth running on the actual document your team uses, not a
hypothetical one.

Most roadmaps, checked honestly, have at least one line where the
answer is no, neither number was measured, it was estimated once in a
planning meeting and never revisited. That line is this lecture's
finding, and it's usually the line worth fixing before the next review,
not the one furthest away on the timeline.

Run this on the item that worries you most, not the safest one to
check. The feature everyone's already excited about, the one already
mentioned in an external announcement, is exactly the one most likely
to be carrying an invented number nobody's had the nerve to question
yet.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Add a threshold and sprint-status column to your roadmap template
2. Find one dated AI item with no real evidence behind it, and flag it

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Add the two columns first. It's a small template change that makes the
gap this lecture named visible to everyone who reads the roadmap after
you, not just to you.

Then find the one dated item that's honestly still a guess, and say so
plainly at the next review, before the date arrives and says it for
you.

Neither of these requires anyone's permission to start. A template
column and one honest flag are both things you can do this week, on the
roadmap you already have, before the next review makes the gap someone
else's problem to discover.
