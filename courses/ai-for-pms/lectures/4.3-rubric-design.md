@slide statement
kicker: Section 4 · The rubric
## A rubric nobody agrees on is vibes with paperwork.
lead: The test of a rubric is not whether it exists. It is whether two people score alike.

@narrate
You left the last lecture with properties written next to each golden example.
This one turns those properties into an instrument other people can operate.

Here is the trap waiting for you. Writing a rubric feels like the finish line.
You hold a document with six questions on it, everyone nods in the review, and
it looks like rigour. Then two colleagues score the same output with it and
land two points apart, and you are back in the meeting you were trying to
escape, except now you are arguing about the rubric as well as the output.

Remember the exercise in Section 0. Five people, one summary, a three point
spread, and every one of them could defend their number. A written rubric
shrank that spread only because its questions were good.

This lecture is about what makes them good, and how to prove it.


@slide two-col
kicker: The difference that decides everything
## Questions that collapse, questions that hold

::: cols
:: card bad
### Cannot be scored reliably
- "Is the summary accurate overall?"
- "Rate the tone from 1 to 10"
- "Is it clear and actionable?"
:: card good
### Scores the same in any hands
- "Does the 1 April deadline appear?"
- "Is every claim present in the ticket?"
- "Is the requested next step stated?"
:::

@narrate
Look at the left column and notice what the questions have in common. Every
one of them asks for a judgement about the whole output at once. Accurate
overall, by whose reading? Tone on a ten point scale, anchored to what? And
the third one quietly asks two questions wearing one question mark, so a reply
that is clear but not actionable has no honest answer.

The right column behaves differently. Each question names one observable
thing and asks whether it is there. You can point at the deadline on the
screen, or at its absence. Two people who disagree can look at the same line
of text, and the disagreement dissolves.

The pattern is worth memorising: when raters argue, the fix is almost never
to train the raters. It is to split, sharpen, or anchor the question until
the argument has nowhere left to live.


@slide definition
kicker: The standard for one question

::: definition Rubric question
A **binary** check on one **observable** property of the output. Pass or
fail, no scale. One property per question. Anyone who can read the input
can point at the evidence.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Rewrite "is the reply professional?" for the ticket case. You will probably
land on two checks: no blame aimed at the customer, and no invented policy.
Notice the vague word disappears entirely.
:::

@narrate
Here is the standard, and each word in it removes a way of disagreeing.

Binary kills the scale debate. On a five point scale, one rater's three is
another rater's four, forever. Pass or fail leaves no gap to camp in.

Observable kills the taste debate. If checking the question means re-reading
the input and pointing at a line, the evidence settles it. If it means
consulting your feelings, it will not.

One property per question kills the blended answer. The moment a question
contains "and", split it.

Try the rewrite on the slide. The word professional feels irreplaceable
until you ask what you would actually point at, and then it resolves into
concrete checks that never needed the word.

Six to eight of these per feature, straight from the properties you wrote in
4.2, and the writing half is done. The proving half comes next.

## Two raters, ten outputs, the same six questions
figcap: Each dot is one output, scored as questions passed out of six. Same outputs, same rubric. Rater B sits a point lower on most of them, and the gap is systematic, not random.

```figure
  - {label: "Rater B", note: "same ten outputs", values: [2, 3, 4, 4, 2, 5, 3, 4, 3, 5]}
```

@narrate
Before you trust a rubric, you run this experiment. Two people, ten outputs,
scored separately, no talking.

Here is a typical first result. Look at the two rows. Rater B is not scoring
randomly, and neither is Rater A. B simply lands about one question lower on
most outputs. That pattern has a specific meaning: somewhere in your six
questions, the two of them are reading one question differently. Usually it
is a phrase like "every claim", where one rater counts an implied claim and
the other does not.

A systematic gap like this is good news, strange as that sounds. Random
disagreement means the rubric is broadly broken. A consistent gap means one
or two questions carry the entire problem, and the comparison table will
point straight at them.

Which is exactly what the calibration protocol does next.


@slide sidenote
kicker: The repair loop
## The calibration protocol

::: split
:: main
1. Pick ten outputs, spanning good to bad
2. Two raters score them blind, separately
3. Compare answers question by question, not total by total
4. Where they differ, debate the **wording**, never the rater
5. Rewrite the guilty question, then re-score ten fresh outputs
:: aside The bar to clear
On each question, raters should match on at least 9 of 10 outputs. A question
that keeps missing that bar is broken. Retire it or split it.
:::

@narrate
The protocol is five steps, it fits in an hour, and the fourth step is the
one that makes it work.

Compare question by question, because totals hide the fault. Two raters can
agree the output scores four and still disagree about which four, and that
hidden disagreement will surface later on an output where it changes the
verdict.

When you find a mismatch, the rule is that nobody is wrong. Both raters
answered the words on the page honestly, so the words are the defect. Argue
about the wording until the ambiguity has a name, write the sharper version,
and re-score on fresh outputs, because re-scoring the ones you just debated
proves nothing.

One pass of this loop typically fixes most of the gap. Teams that skip it
ship a rubric that works only when its author is the one holding it, and the
author has other work to do.


@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
Calibration costs a real hour and a slightly awkward meeting, and agreement
numbers can flatter you: on a question nearly every output passes, two raters
==agree by luck==, not by shared understanding. Watch agreement where the
borderline cases live, and re-check it whenever the rubric changes hands.
:::

@narrate
Two honest warnings before you run this.

First, the cost is real. You are asking two busy people to score ten outputs
twice and then argue about wording. It will feel like bureaucracy in the
room. The purchase is that every eval score after this hour means the same
thing no matter who produced it, which is the property everything in the
next four lectures quietly depends on.

Second, do not let a high agreement number make you smug. If nine outputs in
ten pass a question, two raters will agree ninety percent of the time by
flipping no mental coin at all. The agreement that matters is on the
borderline outputs, the threes and fours, because those are the ones where a
verdict actually turns. When you sample your ten, over-weight the awkward
middle on purpose.
