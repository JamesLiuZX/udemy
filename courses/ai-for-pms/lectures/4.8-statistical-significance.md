@slide statement
kicker: Section 4 · How sure are you
## Four points of pass rate. Is that real, or did you just get unlucky?
lead: A few lectures ago I said this was coming. Here's the actual answer, not the rule of thumb.

@narrate
A few lectures ago, a pass rate moved four points, ninety-two to eighty-
eight, and I told you that alone tells you almost nothing. I also told you
a much bigger move, in a much smaller bucket, five out of five to two out
of five, deserved real attention. You might reasonably have asked: says
who, and by how much?

This lecture is the answer. Not a formula to memorise, a way of thinking
about sample size that tells you, roughly, when to trust a number and when
to shrug at it.

The short version: small samples lie to you constantly, in both
directions, and the only fix is knowing roughly how small is too small.

This is also the last piece of mechanism in this section. Everything after
this lecture is about judgement: reading results honestly, then closing
out. This is the last new idea you need to hold.

## What pure luck alone does to your number
figcap: A coin that is exactly fifty percent, flipped in batches of five and fifty, ten times each. Nothing about the coin changed between draws.

```figure

  - {label: "50 cases", note: "10 repeated draws", values: [44, 48, 52, 46, 50, 54, 48, 52, 46, 50]}
```

@narrate
Forget golden sets for a second. Here's a coin that lands heads exactly
half the time, always, no exceptions.

Flip it five times, call heads a pass, and write down the percentage. Do
that ten separate times. The top row is what you get: twenty percent one
round, eighty the next. Nothing about the coin changed. It is identical
every single time. What changed is that five flips is a small window, and
small windows swing wildly even when nothing underneath them is moving.

Now flip the same coin fifty times per round, ten rounds. The bottom row
barely moves, mid-forties to mid-fifties, hugging fifty the whole way.
Same coin. Same fifty percent. The only thing that changed is the sample
size, and look what it did to how much you can trust any single reading.

@slide table
kicker: A rule of thumb, not a proof
## How much swing is "probably just noise"

| Cases in the bucket | Swing that could easily be noise alone |
| --- | --- |
| 5 | Up to about 45 points, either direction |
| 15 | Up to about 25 points |
| 50 | Up to about 14 points |
| 100+ | Up to about 10 points |

@narrate
Here's the practical version, and it's deliberately a rule of thumb, not
something you'd hand a statistician as proof.

At five cases, a swing of up to roughly forty-five points in either
direction is well within what pure luck alone can produce. That's most of
the scale. At five cases, almost nothing is provably real yet.

At fifty, the size most of your buckets probably sit at, noise alone
rarely moves you more than about fourteen points. That's exactly why four
points, on the aggregate fifty, genuinely was closer to noise than signal.

At a hundred or more, you're down to about ten points of ordinary wobble,
which is where your bigger, common-path buckets will eventually sit if you
keep growing the golden set.

@slide callout
kicker: Back to the bucket that started this

::: callout Read this carefully
At five cases, a sixty-point swing sits right at the edge of what noise
alone can produce. That does not mean it's fine. It means five cases
cannot tell you either way, and ==the honest move is to collect more
adversarial cases==, not to declare a verdict this sample is too small to
support.
:::

@narrate
Let's go back to the number that started this whole section: adversarial,
five out of five before, two out of five after.

Sixty points of movement, at five cases. Look at the table: that's right
at the edge of what five cases can even measure. It's a real, honest,
uncomfortable answer, and it isn't the one anybody wants. You cannot yet
prove that drop is real, and you cannot dismiss it as noise either. Both
of those would be overclaiming what five data points can tell you.

What you can responsibly do is exactly what an earlier lecture already
told you to do when a bucket comes back thin: treat it as a warning, and
go collect more adversarial cases before you either panic or relax.
Fifteen or twenty would let you say something with real confidence. Five
can only make you suspicious.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Statistically significant, in plain terms
A swing large enough that it would be surprising to see from an unchanged
feature, given how many cases you measured it on. Small samples make
almost everything unsurprising. Large samples make small swings suspicious.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Look up your smallest bucket's case count in the table. That's the swing
you should currently ignore. Anything bigger deserves a look before anyone
argues about it.
:::

@narrate
Notice what "significant" actually means here. Not "big" or "important" on
its own. It's "bigger than luck alone would produce, at this sample size."
The exact same ten-point swing can be meaningless at five cases and
alarming at five hundred.

The exercise takes thirty seconds: find your smallest bucket, look up its
row, and now you have a number, not a feeling, for how much movement to
shrug off. Everything past that number is worth five minutes of somebody's
attention before a launch decision leans on it.

This cuts both ways, and the direction people forget is the reassuring
one. A bucket sitting dead flat release after release isn't necessarily
healthy. At small sample sizes, it can just as easily mean you haven't
gathered enough cases to see the problem move yet.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
Fifty cases was never a promise of certainty everywhere at once. It buys
real confidence on your biggest buckets and ==only a warning system on
your smallest one==. Making adversarial provably reliable might mean
twenty or thirty cases in that bucket alone, not five.
:::

@narrate
Say the cost plainly, because it complicates something from two lectures
ago.

Fifty cases sounded complete. It isn't, evenly. Your twenty common-path
cases give you real statistical footing. Your five adversarial cases give
you a smoke detector, not a verdict: useful for noticing something might
be wrong, not enough to prove it on their own.

Growing that bucket to something you can actually trust statistically
costs the same thing everything in this section has cost: real hours,
finding or writing more adversarial cases nobody was in a hurry to
collect. That's not a flaw in the four-bucket design. It's the honest
limit of building an instrument in an afternoon instead of a quarter.

Treat it as a roadmap, not a failure. Fifty cases got you a working
instrument this week. Twenty adversarial cases, added over the next few
releases as you encounter or write them, is what turns your weakest
smoke detector into something you can defend in the same room as the rest
of the dashboard.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Find your smallest bucket's row in the swing-to-ignore table
2. Flag any bucket under fifteen cases as "warning only" until it grows

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Find your smallest bucket, and note down the swing that's genuinely still
just noise at that size. Now you have a number to check any future alarm
against, instead of a gut feeling.

Then be honest in how you label small buckets. Anything under fifteen
cases is a smoke detector, not a verdict, until you've grown it. Say so on
the dashboard, so nobody in a launch review mistakes a warning for proof.
