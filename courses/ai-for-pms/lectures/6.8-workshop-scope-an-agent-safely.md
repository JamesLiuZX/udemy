@narrate
Pick something you've genuinely considered automating with an agent, or
the refund case this section keeps returning to if nothing else comes to
mind. Open A08, and give this the same twenty minutes every workshop in
this course has asked for.

The bar is identical too. Not perfect. Complete. Every question
answered with something, even where that something is a number you'll
validate later, the same move as Section three and Section five's
workshops before this one.

Automation is the right thing to practise scoping on precisely because
it's the branch of Section two's decision tree with the most ways to go
wrong quietly. Retrieval fails by returning a bad answer. An agent that's
scoped badly can act on one.

@slide table
kicker: How to spend the twenty minutes
## Seven lectures, five pacing blocks

| Minutes | Covers | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-4 | What it is, and 6.1's workflow test | Could a human specify every branch in advance |
| 4-8 | Tools touched, chain length, 6.3's maths | Compute the real number, don't guess it |
| 8-12 | Design pattern, single vs multi-agent | Add a second agent only with a specific reason |
| 12-17 | Blast radius sort, named approvers | Every action, not just the one that worried you |
| 17-20 | The disqualification checklist | Any single failed row stops the whole proposal |

@narrate
Notice minutes four through eight ask you to actually compute 6.3's
number, not estimate it from memory. Count the real steps in your
chain, pick a defensible per-step accuracy, and raise it to that power.
Most people find this the slowest four minutes and the most valuable.

Why does the blast radius block get the most time? Because sorting
every single action into last lecture's four rows, not just the one you
already suspected was risky, is usually where a workshop like this finds
the thing worth actually changing before anyone builds it.

The middle block, minutes eight through twelve, goes quickly for most
proposals, and that's fine. Plan-then-execute is usually the obvious
right call once you say it out loud, and most tasks turn out not to need
a second agent at all once 6.5's question, what specifically would one
agent fail to do, gets asked directly instead of assumed away.

The last three minutes are a single pass, not a new topic: run your own
proposal against all four of last lecture's disqualifying rows, out
loud, with the numbers you just computed rather than a fresh guess.

@slide quote
"Auto-approve refunds under $50, verified account, capped at 3 per day, escalating anything outside that to a named approver within the hour."
attrib: The bounded example from last lecture's checklist walkthrough

@narrate
Recognise this shape. It's last lecture's good column, and it's roughly
what your own worksheet's summary should sound like once minutes zero
through seventeen are actually filled in.

Notice everything this sentence specifies: the scope, the bound, the
cap, and exactly who gets the cases outside it. Notice too what it
doesn't specify: no tool name, no framework, no model. That's the same
boundary Section three drew around the PRD and Section five drew around
the RAG design doc. This document says what the system must do and
where a human stays involved. How to build it stays an engineering
decision.

Compare it against whatever you'd have written before this section
started. Most first attempts at describing an automation idea sound like
"let the agent handle refunds automatically," a sentence with nothing
specific enough in it to actually scope, let alone approve. Twenty
minutes from now, yours should read closer to this one.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
The reliability maths section will feel like it's arguing against your
own idea. ==Compute it anyway, and write down the real number==, not the
one that would feel better.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because minute four is where people quietly stop
being rigorous, exactly when rigour is the entire point of the exercise.

If your honest chain length and a defensible per-step accuracy produce a
whole-chain number that makes you wince, that's not the worksheet
failing. That's the worksheet doing its job four sections before launch
instead of during an incident review. Write the real number down, then
decide what to do about it: shorten the chain, add a checkpoint, or
accept that this one stays manual a while longer.

The same discipline applies to the blast radius block right after it.
It's tempting to sort a borderline action into the safer row because
that's the row that lets you keep moving. Sort it honestly instead. The
worksheet only protects you if the rows reflect the action as it
actually is, not as you'd prefer it to be scored.

:::

::: example What to do when you finish early
Reread only the blast radius section. If every row has a named approver
and nothing's hiding in "the team will review it," the draft is ready
to share.
:::

@narrate
That's today's bar, stated as plainly as it was in Section three and
Section five: attempted, not perfected, and the one number that
actually matters computed rather than guessed.

If you finish with time left, don't polish evenly. Return to the blast
radius section specifically, the same instruction as always, because
it's the section most likely to be quietly incomplete.

Resist the urge to smooth the reliability section instead, even though
it's the one with a satisfying number attached. A tidy number sitting
next to an unsorted blast radius section is a worksheet that looks more
finished than it is, and looking finished is exactly the trap this
course keeps warning you away from.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## One thing worth doing now

Finish the worksheet, reliability number actually computed, and send it to an engineer asking which row they'd push back on first

@narrate
One thing before you move on.

Finish all seven questions. Anywhere the honest answer is a guess, label
it a guess rather than letting it read as more certain than it is. Then
send it to whoever would actually build this, today, and ask which row
they'd challenge first. Expect it to be the blast radius section, not
the reliability maths. Engineers usually trust your arithmetic. They
want to know exactly who's approving what before they write a line of
code.

That question alone is often worth more than the twenty minutes you
spent writing the draft. It's the same finding the support-corpus owner
gave you back in Section five: the person closest to the system sees the
gap in minutes that would have taken you a week to notice working alone.

Next lecture closes this section: what you've actually built across ten
lectures, and how directly it plugs into Section seven, where every one
of these agents gets a real number attached to what it costs to run.
