@slide quote
kicker: Section 3 · Where the human stands
attrib: A line that closes almost every review of an AI feature

> "Don't worry, we'll have a human in the loop."

@narrate
You'll hear that exact sentence end a review, and it's almost never
specific enough to build against. In the loop when? Reviewing what?
Before the action or after it?

Those aren't details to fill in later. They're the entire decision.
Put the human in the wrong place and you either add friction to
something that never needed it, or you add a reassuring sentence to
the PRD that catches nothing in practice. "Human in the loop" isn't one
design. It's five, and the wrong one either burns your reviewers or
catches nothing.

Both mistakes are common, and they come from the same shortcut:
picking a pattern because it's the one your team has used before,
rather than asking what this specific feature actually needs. A pattern
that worked perfectly for a low-stakes summariser can be genuinely
dangerous applied to something that spends money, and a pattern built
for something irreversible can smother a harmless drafting tool in
approvals nobody needed.

@slide table
kicker: Five patterns, not one
## Where the human stands, and what it actually buys you

| Pattern | Human is involved | Right for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-approval | Before every single action | Irreversible, high blast radius |
| Draft-then-review | Before every output ships | Anything reaching a user directly |
| Sampled audit | After the fact, on a percentage | Reversible, high volume, low stakes each |
| Escalate on uncertainty | Only when the guardrail flags it | Once that guardrail is actually trustworthy |
| Monitor only | After the fact, in aggregate | Lowest-stakes, fully reversible tools |

@narrate
Read this table as a spectrum, from a human checking everything to a
human checking almost nothing, and notice the right column is doing
all the work.

Pre-approval belongs to the act archetype at its most dangerous: money
moving, an account changing, nothing recoverable if it's wrong.
Draft-then-review is the natural home for anything a user reads
directly, the human isn't approving an internal step, they're the last
check before publication. Sampled audit and monitor-only both trade
certainty for speed, appropriate once being wrong occasionally is
merely annoying, not costly. Escalate on uncertainty is the most
efficient pattern on this table, and the most dangerous one to pick
too early, because it's only as good as the guardrail deciding what
counts as uncertain.

Notice, too, that these patterns aren't a ladder you climb once and
leave. A feature often starts on draft-then-review while everyone's
still learning how it fails, and earns its way down to sampled audit
or escalation only once the pass rate and the failure taxonomy from
the last two lectures have actually proven it out. Starting anywhere
looser than that is borrowing trust the feature hasn't earned yet.

@slide two-col
kicker: Same feature, two different patterns
## The refund example again, and why the pattern isn't a detail

::: cols
:: card bad
### Escalate on uncertainty
- Auto-approves refunds the model scores as confident
- A wrong approval is money out the door, already gone
- Confidence and correctness are not the same thing
:: card good
### Pre-approval above a threshold
- Every refund over $500 waits for a human, no exceptions
- Wrong guesses get caught before money moves
- Slower, and the only version that matches the stakes
:::

@narrate
Remember auto-approving refunds over five hundred dollars, from the
disqualification checklist back in Section one. Here's the same
feature, and here's why the pattern you pick isn't a footnote.

Escalate on uncertainty sounds efficient, and it fails exactly where
this feature is most dangerous: a model can be confidently wrong, the
whole premise of hallucination from lecture one-point-five. A
confident, wrong approval sails straight past a guardrail built to
catch uncertainty, because the model wasn't uncertain. It was sure, and
it was sure about the wrong thing.

Pre-approval above a threshold doesn't have that gap. It doesn't ask
the model whether it's confident. It asks whether the dollar amount
crosses a line, a question with no ambiguity at all.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
A human in the loop isn't automatically a safe loop. ==Ask someone to
approve two hundred outputs a day and they start approving on
autopilot==, and the check becomes decorative long before anyone
notices.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because this is the failure mode that makes a
technically correct design fail in practice anyway.

Reviewer fatigue is real and it's fast. Give a human genuinely hard
decisions all day and judgment holds up reasonably well. Give them two
hundred near-identical approvals, most of them plainly fine, and the
ninety-eighth one gets the same half-second glance as the second. The
loop still exists on the org chart. It stopped doing anything
useful somewhere around case thirty.

Design against this directly: keep the volume any one reviewer sees
low enough to stay real, rotate reviewers, or narrow pre-approval to
genuinely high-stakes cases and route the rest to sampled audit
instead. A human check that's too broad to sustain is worse than an
honest, narrower one.

This is worth putting a number on in the PRD itself, the same way the
threshold and the failure floor got a number two lectures back. A
review queue with no stated volume limit will grow until it breaks,
quietly, and nobody will notice until an audit finds approvals that
took less time to make than to read.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Human-in-the-loop pattern
The deliberate choice of when a human is involved, before the action,
after it, or only on escalation, matched to the feature's blast
radius. Not a single default applied to every feature the same way.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Find where your PRD says "human in the loop." Name which of the five
patterns it actually means, and check that pattern against the
feature's real blast radius.
:::

@narrate
That's the definition worth holding onto: the pattern is the design,
not a sentence you add to sound responsible.

The exercise is specific. Wherever your PRD mentions a human check,
name the pattern precisely, and ask whether it matches how bad a wrong
answer would actually be. A mismatch in either direction is worth
fixing before launch, not after the reviewer burns out or the wrong
one slips through.

## Two things worth doing now

1. Name the human-in-the-loop pattern your feature actually uses
2. Check it against the blast radius, not against what felt easiest to build

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Name the pattern precisely, using this lecture's five options, not the
vague phrase your PRD currently has.

Then check it honestly against blast radius. If a wrong output is
expensive or irreversible and your pattern is anything short of
pre-approval, that mismatch is worth raising now, while it's a
sentence to edit and not an incident to explain.
