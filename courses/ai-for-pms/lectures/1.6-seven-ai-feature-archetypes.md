@slide quote
kicker: Section 1 · The seven archetypes
attrib: A defence you'll hear in your own planning meeting

> "It's fine, the summariser's been solid for months."

@narrate
You'll hear that exact line defending the next feature too. Here's the
question it never answers: is a summariser actually the same conversation
as an agent that books refunds on its own? Not even close, even when the
same model powers both.

By now you've got the mechanism, the frontier, and why hallucination
happens. Here's where that turns into something you can use in the room.

Almost every AI feature anyone builds falls into one of seven archetypes,
and the archetype tells you most of what you need to know about how hard
this is going to be to ship safely, before you've written a line of the
spec.

The mistake I see constantly is treating "should we use AI for this" as
one conversation. It's actually seven different conversations wearing the
same trenchcoat, and they do not carry the same risk. Watch for that
defence, in exactly that shape, in your own meetings.

@slide table
kicker: The seven shapes
## What each one actually looks like

| Archetype | A real example |
| --- | --- |
| Summarise | Condense a support thread into three lines |
| Extract | Pull the invoice number and amount from an email |
| Classify | Route a ticket to the right team |
| Draft | Write a first-pass reply for an agent to edit |
| Search | Rank documents by relevance to a query |
| Answer | Chat directly answers a question from your docs |
| Act | Book, cancel, or update something on the user's behalf |

@narrate
Here are the seven, roughly ordered by how directly they touch the user.

Summarise and extract both take real text you already have and reshape
it, condensing or pulling structured fields out. Classify sorts something
into a bucket you already defined, like routing a ticket.

Draft writes new words from a prompt, for a human to review before
anything ships. Search ranks existing documents so a human picks from
the results. Answer skips that step and states a conclusion directly. And
act is the one that actually does something in the world, booking,
cancelling, updating, on the user's behalf, with no draft step in
between.

Notice the drift as you go down that list: less and less human review
sitting between the model and the consequence.

A single product often contains several of these at once, and that's
normal, not a red flag. A support tool might summarise the thread,
extract the account number, draft a reply, and route the ticket, four
archetypes in one feature. Name each piece separately, because they don't
share a risk level just because they share a UI.


@narrate
Here's the pattern the last slide was building to. Same seven
archetypes, ordered by how much evaluation rigour they genuinely demand.

Summarise and extract sit low, because you're reshaping text that's
right there in the prompt, exactly where the mechanism from lecture one
is strongest. A spot check and a light golden set will catch most
problems.

Answer and act sit high, and for different reasons. Answer skips the
human review step search kept, so a wrong answer reaches the user
directly. Act compounds that: it's not just wrong, it's wrong and it
already happened. Those two are where you want the entire Section four
toolkit, not the light version.

Draft and search sit in the middle for a genuinely different reason than
either end. They're not risky because the model is unreliable on that
task, they're risky if the human review step gets skipped under
deadline pressure. The archetype is only as safe as the review step
someone swore they'd keep.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
This ranks the archetype, not your specific feature. ==A well-evaluated
agent can ship safely, and a careless summariser can still embarrass
you==. The chart predicts how much work you owe it, not the outcome.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because a chart like that invites the wrong
conclusion if you're not careful.

This is not a verdict on any specific feature. It's a prediction of
effort. A well-evaluated "act" feature, with the reliability maths from
Section six done properly, can absolutely ship safely. A "summarise"
feature that skipped Section four entirely can still embarrass you, just
with lower odds and probably a smaller blast radius.

The chart tells you how much you owe the problem before you trust it. It
doesn't do the owing for you.

Use it to argue about budget and timeline honestly, not to argue that a
feature is safe because of what category it happens to sit in. "It's
just a summariser" has talked more than one team out of eval work they
actually needed.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Archetype
The shape of what your feature actually does to the world: reshape text,
sort it, draft it, rank it, answer with it, or act on it. Not the
industry, not the UI, the shape of the consequence.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Name your own feature's archetype, out loud, in one word from that list.
If you can't, that's not a vocabulary problem. You don't yet know what
you're actually building.
:::

@narrate
Notice the definition is about consequence, not appearance. Two features
can look identical in the UI, a chat window, a text box, and sit at
completely different points on that chart, because one drafts for review
and the other acts directly.

The exercise is blunt on purpose. Say your feature's archetype in one
word. If nothing on that list fits cleanly, or more than one does at
once, that's worth sitting with before you write a single acceptance
criterion, because the spec you write depends entirely on which one
you're actually building.

Do this before Section three, not during it. Walking into a PRD already
knowing your archetype is the difference between writing the spec once
and rewriting it after someone asks a question you hadn't considered.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Name your feature's archetype in one word from the list
2. Check that word against how much eval work you've actually done

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Name your feature's archetype, one word, out loud or on paper. Then be
honest about whether the evaluation work you've actually done matches
where that archetype sits on the chart, not where you wish it sat.

If you're building several archetypes in one product, do this once per
piece. A single honest sentence, "we've built a search feature's worth of
rigour for something that actually answers," is worth more than the whole
rest of this lecture.
