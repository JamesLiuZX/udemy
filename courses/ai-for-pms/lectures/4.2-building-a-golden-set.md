@slide statement
kicker: Section 4 · The golden set
## When your eval says "safe to ship", it means safe on these fifty.
lead: The harness is plumbing. The examples are where the judgement lives.

@narrate
Everything your eval will ever tell you is a statement about the examples you
put in it.

When it reports that a prompt change is fine, what it has really checked is
that the change is fine on the fifty cases you chose, weeks earlier, probably
in an afternoon, possibly in a hurry. Every verdict inherits the strengths of
that afternoon's choices, and every blind spot too.

So this lecture is about choosing well. Not about tooling. The spreadsheet
arrives in lecture 4.6 and the code in 4.7, and both are the easy part. Fifty
rows of well-chosen inputs is the asset. It outlives the prompt, it usually
outlives the model, and it often outlives the engineer who set up the harness.

By the end you will know the four bands a strong set draws from, roughly how
many of each to take, and what to write down next to every example you keep.


@slide two-col
kicker: The habit vs the craft
## Two ways to fill fifty rows

::: cols
:: card bad
### How it usually happens
- Copy whatever inputs are lying around from the demo
- Grab one busy afternoon of recent traffic
- Keep the cases the feature already handles nicely
:: card good
### How you will do it
- Sample the routine middle of real traffic, on purpose
- Hunt the awkward variants and the rare-but-real edges
- Preserve every failure that has already embarrassed you
:::

@narrate
Be honest about how this usually goes, because the default is not neutral. It
is biased in the worst direction.

Sets built from whatever was handy skew towards the easy middle. The demo
inputs were picked to look good. A single afternoon of traffic over-represents
whatever that afternoon happened to contain. And keeping the cases that
already work feels harmless right up until you realise you have built an exam
the student has already passed.

The craft version has a different posture. You treat the set like a portfolio
with a job to do. Most of it comes from the ordinary middle of your traffic,
because that is what your users mostly send. But a deliberate slice is spent
on the inputs that hurt: the awkward, the rare, and above all the ones that
have already failed you in public.

## How this course splits the fifty
figcap: A starting split, not a law. Grey is the everyday work; the coloured bands are the ones that catch regressions, and the ones teams forget to collect.

```figure
  - {label: "Known failures", value: 7, note: "already burned you once", emphasis: true}
```

@narrate
Here is the split this course starts you with, and the reasoning behind each
band.

Half the set is routine, and that is deliberate. If the ordinary middle of
your traffic breaks, that is the worst possible regression, so it gets the
most coverage.

Ten are variants: still normal work, but with a complication. Two problems in
one ticket. A customer writing in a second language. An input three times the
usual length.

Then the coloured bands, which are the ones teams under-invest in. Eight edge
cases: rare, strange, but legitimate, like an empty message or a legal threat.
And seven known failures. Ticket four four seven one from Section 0 lives
here, because the summary that dropped its deadline must never pass an eval
of yours again.

Adjust the numbers for your feature. Keep the shape.


@slide table
kicker: Sourcing each band
## Where the fifty come from

| Band | Where you find them | What they catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine | A random pull from last month's real inputs | The everyday breaking quietly |
| Tricky variants | Filters on traffic: length, mixed intent, language | The "mostly works" complaints |
| Edge cases | Support escalations, plus an hour of imagination | The incident report |
| Known failures | Bug tickets and the complaints channel | The same fire twice |

@narrate
Sourcing is where the day of effort actually goes, so here is where to look.

For the routine band, resist choosing by hand. Pull at random from a real
month, because your intuition about what is typical is exactly the bias you
are trying to remove.

Variants respond well to simple filters. Sort by length and take something
from the long tail. Search for tickets that mention two products at once.

Edge cases come from the people who see them: your support team can name five
in one coffee chat. Then spend one honest hour trying to imagine what a
malicious or confused user sends on day one, because they will.

Known failures are the cheapest band and the most valuable. They are already
written down in bug reports and complaint threads. Collecting them costs an
hour, and it converts every past embarrassment into a permanent regression
test.


@slide definition
kicker: What you record for each one

::: definition Golden example
One real input, plus the **properties** any acceptable output must satisfy.
Not a stored perfect answer: the feature is a distribution, so many outputs
can pass, and the properties are what they share.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
For ticket #4471: the duplicate charge is stated, the 1 April deadline
survives, the email mismatch survives, nothing is invented. Four properties,
one minute.
:::

@narrate
Now the part almost everyone gets wrong on the first attempt: what you write
next to each input.

The instinct is to save a model answer, the output you wish the feature would
produce. Resist it. You learned in Section 0 why: this system can phrase a
correct answer twenty different ways, and comparing against one blessed
phrasing punishes nineteen of them.

What you record instead is the set of things that must be true. For the
double-charge ticket, an acceptable summary states the billing problem, keeps
the deadline, keeps the mismatched-email clue, and adds nothing the customer
never said. Any output with those four properties passes, however it is
worded.

Writing properties is slower than copying a nice output. It is also the step
where you discover what you actually mean by good, which is precisely the
argument the next lecture teaches you to run with other people.


@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
A golden set decays in two ways. Traffic drifts away from it, so it needs
feeding from production every month or two. And a team that stares at the same
fifty will quietly ==tune the prompt to the exam==. Version the set, refresh
it on a schedule, and treat a suspiciously perfect score as a question, not a
victory.
:::

@narrate
Two failure modes will come for your set, and you should plan for both on day
one.

The first is drift. The set is a photograph of your traffic, and traffic
moves. New customer segments arrive, new products launch, and the photograph
ages. The loop from last lecture is the answer: every failure production
surfaces becomes a candidate case, and the set gets a small refresh every
month or two rather than a rebuild every year.

The second is subtler. A team that optimises against the same fifty examples
for six months is no longer measuring the feature. It is teaching to the
test. You will know it is happening when the eval score climbs while the
complaints channel does not.

So version the set like code, note where every example came from, and when
the score reads one hundred percent, get curious rather than comfortable.

## What to do now

1. Download **A02**, the golden dataset spec and starter set, attached here
2. Draft your own fifty: pull the routine band from real traffic this week
3. Next lecture: turning "properties" into a rubric two humans score the same way

@narrate
Your homework writes itself this time.

The artifact attached to this lecture has two parts. The spec is the form to
fill in for each example: the input, its band, its source, and the properties
an acceptable output must satisfy. The starter set is fifty support-ticket
cases in exactly that format, which you can study, borrow the structure of,
and then replace with your own domain.

Start with the two cheap bands this week. The known failures are sitting in
your bug tracker, and the routine band is one random sample away.

Next lecture takes the properties you have started writing and turns them
into a proper rubric: the version two different humans can score the same way
on a bad Friday. That agreement problem is harder than it sounds, and it is
where evaluation is usually won or lost.
