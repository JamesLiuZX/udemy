## Five minutes, one judgement, one uncomfortable discovery.
lead: Pause when I ask you to. This only works if you commit to a number first.

@narrate
This one is hands-on, and it only works if you actually do it.

I'm going to show you a real AI output. You're going to score it. Then I'll show
you how other people scored the same thing, and we'll talk about why that result
is a problem you now own.

You need nothing but somewhere to write a single number.

@slide code
kicker: The input
## A support ticket, and what the AI wrote about it

```text
TICKET #4471
"Hi, I was charged twice for my annual plan on March 3rd.
 I've already cancelled the duplicate through my bank, but the
 second charge is still showing as pending on your side. I need
 this resolved before my next invoice on April 1st or it will
 overdraw the account. My account email is different from my
 billing email, which may be why."

AI SUMMARY
"Customer was double-charged for their annual plan and wants a
 refund. They have contacted their bank. Requesting resolution."
```

@narrate
Here's the ticket. A customer was charged twice for an annual plan. They've
already gone to their bank about it. There's a deadline, April the first, and a
consequence if it's missed: the account overdraws. And they've offered a probable
cause, which is that their account email and billing email don't match.

Here's what the AI wrote. Read both properly. I'll wait.

Now score that summary from one to five. One means useless. Five means you'd be
happy for a support agent to act on it without opening the original ticket.

Pause the video. Commit to a number. Say it out loud if you're somewhere you can.

@slide metrics
kicker: The reveal
## The same summary, scored by five experienced people

::: metrics
- 2 :: "Dropped the deadline entirely" :: bad
- 3 :: "Gets the gist, misses specifics"
- 3 :: "Acceptable for triage"
- 4 :: "Accurate as far as it goes" :: good
- 5 :: "Concise and correct" :: good
:::

@narrate
Here's what happened when five experienced people scored that exact summary.

Two, three, three, four, five.

Same text. Same question. A three-point spread on a five-point scale.

And every one of those people could defend their score. The person who gave it a
two noticed the April first deadline had vanished, and that deadline is the entire
reason the ticket is urgent. The person who gave it a five was reading it as a
triage summary, where "double charge, wants refund" is genuinely all you need.

They weren't disagreeing about the summary. They were disagreeing about what the
summary was *for*, and nobody had said.

@slide statement
kicker: The uncomfortable part
class: center
## If five people cannot agree, your quality bar is not real.
lead: It was never written down, so everyone brought their own.

@narrate
Now sit with the uncomfortable part.

Your number is somewhere in that spread. Not because you judged badly, but because
there was nothing to judge against.

This is exactly what happens in your organisation every week. Someone demos an AI
feature. People react. Some are impressed, some aren't. That reaction gets called
a quality bar, and decisions get made on it.

But it isn't a bar. It's five private opinions about five private definitions of
good, and whichever one belongs to the most senior person in the room becomes the
decision.

Notice what this means for the idea from the last lecture. You can't measure a
distribution with an instrument that gives a different reading depending on who's
holding it. Before you can measure the model, you have to fix the ruler.

@slide table
kicker: The fix
## Same output, scored against something explicit

| Dimension | What earns a pass | This summary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core issue captured | The billing problem is stated | Pass |
| Action required is clear | Agent knows what to do next | Pass |
| Deadlines preserved | Any date or time pressure survives | **Fail** |
| Consequence preserved | What breaks if ignored is kept | **Fail** |
| Diagnostic detail kept | Clues to root cause survive | **Fail** |
| Nothing invented | No claim absent from the ticket | Pass |

@narrate
Now watch what happens when we stop asking "is it good" and start asking six
specific questions.

Is the core billing issue captured? Yes. Is the required action clear? Yes. Are
deadlines preserved? No, April the first is gone. Is the consequence preserved?
No, the overdraft is gone. Is the diagnostic detail kept? No, the mismatched
email, which is very likely the actual cause, is gone. Did it invent anything? No.

Three out of six. And notice something important: the three it failed are the
three that would have let an agent resolve this without reopening the ticket.

Now score it again. Almost everyone lands in the same place, because they're
answering questions instead of having feelings. That three-point spread collapses
to about one. That collapse is the entire trick.

@slide definition
kicker: What you just built

::: definition Rubric
A fixed set of pass or fail questions, written **before** you look at the output,
that together define what *good* means for one specific job.
:::

::: example Why six
Fewer than four and you miss the failure that matters. More than eight and
reviewers stop reading carefully. Six is the number people actually complete.
:::

@narrate
Let's name what you just did, because it's more significant than it felt.

You took an unanswerable question, is this good, and swapped it for six questions
that each have an answer. The box on screen gives you the formal version. What I
want you to notice is the word *before*.

That word is doing almost all the work. Write your questions after you've seen the
output and you will, without meaning to, write questions it happens to pass. Every
team does this. It's why so many quality bars are quietly set by the thing they're
supposed to be judging.

On the number: fewer than four and you'll miss the failure that actually matters.
More than eight and your reviewers stop reading carefully and start ticking boxes.
Six is about the number people genuinely complete.

And a rubric is the atom of everything that follows. Run it across fifty tickets
instead of one and you have a golden set. Turn "three out of six" into a pass rate
across those fifty and you have an eval. Put a threshold on that pass rate and you
have an acceptance criterion you can put in a spec.

That's Sections 3 and 4, and you've just done the first version of it in five
minutes.

## Two things worth doing now

1. Download **A05, the rubric template**, attached to this lecture
2. Write six pass or fail questions for one AI output at your own work

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

The rubric template is attached to this lecture. Download it now, while you're
thinking about it.

Then do the five-minute version on something real. Take any AI output at your
work: a summary, a draft, a classification, a suggested reply. Write six pass-fail
questions for it. Not five, not ten. Six is enough to be useful and few enough
that you'll finish.

You'll find at least one thing you care about that the current output silently
drops. That finding is worth more than the rest of this section, and you got it in
five minutes.
