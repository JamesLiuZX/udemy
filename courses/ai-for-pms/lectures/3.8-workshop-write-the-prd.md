@slide callout
kicker: Section 3 · The workshop

::: callout Set a twenty-minute timer
Pick a feature you're genuinely close to, the messier and more real the
better. ==Not a perfect PRD. A complete one==, every section attempted,
ready to argue about instead of ready to avoid.
:::

@narrate
Everything up to this point has been building toward those twenty
minutes of actual writing. Open A01 from last lecture and follow along.

What does a complete draft actually mean here? Not a polished document.
Every section has something in it, even if some of those somethings are
rough. A complete rough draft is a document people can react to. A
blank section is just a gap nobody's found yet.

Choosing the right feature matters more than people expect going in.
Pick something hypothetical, or something so simple it barely has any
real risk, and the workshop feels easy and teaches you almost nothing,
because none of the hard sections have anything real to grip onto.
Pick the feature you're actually a little worried about, the one with
a genuine failure mode you haven't fully named yet, and every section
in this template will earn its place.

@slide table
kicker: How to spend the twenty minutes
## A pace that keeps you moving, section by section

| Minutes | Section | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-3 | Problem & archetype | Don't skip naming the archetype |
| 3-8 | Acceptance criteria | The four-part sentence, even rough |
| 8-12 | Dataset & rubric commitments | A placeholder beats a blank |
| 12-16 | Failure taxonomy & degradation | Three real failure types, minimum |
| 16-20 | Human-in-the-loop & ownership | Match the pattern to blast radius |

@narrate
Notice acceptance criteria gets the most time on this clock, five
minutes, almost a quarter of the whole workshop. That's not an
accident. Lecture 3.7 already told you why: get that sentence right and
everything below it gets easier to write, because you already know
what you're proving.

Everything after that section should move faster than you expect. Once
the threshold exists, the dataset and rubric sections are mostly
restating decisions you've already made, and the failure taxonomy is
just naming the ways this specific feature actually breaks. If a later
section is taking longer than the acceptance criteria did, that's a
sign the acceptance criteria wasn't specific enough yet.

Treat these numbers as a pace, not a deadline. Twenty minutes is
enough time to prove to yourself that a complete draft is possible in
one sitting, which is the actual point. If you're at minute twenty-five
and still finishing the last section, that's a fine outcome. Stopping
at minute twelve with three sections untouched, because the first two
felt too important to leave imperfect, is the outcome this pace is
built to prevent.

@slide quote
"On the 50-case support golden set, at least 90% of responses score 4 or 5 on the accuracy rubric, with zero responses scoring 1."
attrib: The support ticket summariser's completed acceptance criteria, from lecture 3.2

@narrate
Recognise this sentence. It's the exact rewrite from earlier in this
section, and that's deliberately not a new example. This is what a
real twenty-minute session actually produces when it works: one
specific sentence, four parts, nothing left to a reader's imagination.

If what you've written in the last few minutes doesn't look roughly
like this shape, a dataset, a rubric, a percentage, and a floor, that's
worth noticing now, while you're still in the section, rather than
three sections later when it's harder to fix.

It's worth being honest about what this sentence doesn't need to be:
elegant, or even final. It needs to be specific enough that changing
your mind about it later would be a visible edit, not a quiet drift.
Fifty cases can become seventy-five. Ninety percent can become
ninety-five. Both of those are real conversations a document like this
one makes possible. A vague sentence never gets edited, because there
was never anything precise enough in it to disagree with.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
One hard section will try to stop the whole draft. ==Write a dated TBD
and keep moving==, a complete rough draft beats one perfect section and
four blank ones.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because it's the thing that actually derails
most people's twenty minutes, and it's rarely the acceptance criteria.

It's usually the rubric, or the failure taxonomy, the sections that
feel like they need to be right before you can move on. They don't.
Write what you know, mark the rest "TBD, owner assigned by Friday," the
exact honest-gap move from the golden dataset lecture, and keep going.
A stalled section costs you the entire rest of the document. A named
gap costs you nothing but honesty.

Notice this is the same instinct fighting you here as the one that
built the untestable line all the way back in lecture one: the urge to
write something that sounds finished rather than something that's
actually true. A dated TBD looks less finished than a vague sentence
that quietly means nothing. It's the better kind of unfinished,
because everyone reading it knows exactly what's still missing.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition A complete first draft
Every section has content, even where that content is a dated
placeholder instead of a finished answer. Nothing was skipped in
silence.
:::

::: example What to do when you finish early
Reread only the acceptance criteria section. If you'd actually hold a
launch to it today, the draft is ready to show someone. If you
wouldn't, that's the one sentence worth another five minutes.
:::

@narrate
That's the bar for today, and it's lower than perfect on purpose. A
complete draft with three honest placeholders is something a
colleague can react to this afternoon. A perfect acceptance criteria
section sitting above four blank ones is something nobody can react
to at all, because there's nothing there yet to disagree with.

If you finish with time left, don't polish everything evenly. Go back
to the one sentence this whole section has been about, and ask
whether you'd actually defend it in a launch review.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Finish the draft, gaps marked honestly, not left blank
2. Send it to one person today and ask what they'd push back on

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Finish the draft you started. Every section touched, gaps marked with
a date and an owner instead of silence.

Then send it to one real person today, not next week, and ask them
specifically what they'd push back on. That answer will teach you more
about this section than another lecture would.

Expect them to push hardest on the acceptance criteria, the same
section that took the most of your twenty minutes. That's not a bad
sign. It means the document is finally specific enough for someone to
disagree with it, which is the entire difference this section set out
to make.
