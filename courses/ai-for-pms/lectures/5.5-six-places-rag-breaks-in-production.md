@slide statement
kicker: Section 5 · Production reality
## A RAG system that scored ninety-five percent on your test set can still embarrass you in week three. It happens in exactly six ways.
lead: Not six vague warnings. Six specific pipeline stages, each with its own failure and its own fix.

@narrate
Last lecture ended on a warning worth taking seriously: good scorecard
numbers don't guarantee a good system, because the scorecard was run
once, on a corpus that doesn't stay still. Here are the six specific
ways that gap actually shows up once real users and real time get
involved.

Each one maps to a stage from the pipeline two lectures back. Knowing
the name is what turns a vague "it's acting weird" into a specific bug
report someone can actually fix.

Notice, too, why this list matters even if you never write a line of
the pipeline yourself. When something goes wrong in production, the
room defaults to blaming the model, because the model is the visible
part. But is the model actually the problem? Five of these six
failures have nothing to do with the model at all. Naming the actual
failure is how you stop a real bug from turning into a vague,
unfixable complaint about the AI being unreliable.

@slide table
kicker: Six failures, six fixes
## What breaks, and where the fix lives

| Failure | What it looks like | Fix lives in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stale index | Confidently cites a policy that changed last month | Freshness pipeline, next lecture |
| Permission leakage | Retrieves a document this user shouldn't see | Access filtering, next lecture |
| Chunking damage | A retrieved fragment nobody could actually use | Chunking strategy, last lecture |
| Context overflow | Retrieved chunks plus history silently truncated | Trim history first, not chunks |
| Near-duplicate confusion | An old and current version both retrieved, blended | De-duplication before indexing |
| Silent failure | Nothing relevant exists, the model answers anyway | The degradation path, Section three |

@narrate
Read down that middle column, because every one of these looks
completely different from the outside and traces back to a specific,
nameable cause once you know what to look for.

Two of these, staleness and permissions, are big enough to earn their
own lecture next, so we'll only name them here. The other four are
worth sitting with now, because they're easy to misdiagnose as "the
model being unreliable" when the actual bug is sitting somewhere
earlier in the pipeline.

Context overflow deserves a second look, because the fix in that row
isn't obvious. When retrieved chunks and conversation history together
exceed the context window, something has to get cut, and most systems
default to trimming whichever arrived last, usually the retrieved
content. That's backwards. History is often padding by the time it's
five turns old. The retrieved chunks are the actual facts the answer
depends on. Trim history first, and only touch retrieval as a last
resort.

@slide callout
kicker: The one to worry about most

::: callout Silent failure doesn't announce itself
Nothing relevant exists in the index. ==The model answers anyway,
confidently==, and nothing about the output signals that retrieval
came back empty.
:::

@narrate
Silent failure is the row worth the most attention of the four,
because it's the one that produces the most convincing wrong answers.

A model asked a question with nothing relevant in its retrieved
context doesn't usually say so. It answers anyway, confidently, the
exact hallucination mechanism from Section one. That gap is precisely
what the degradation path from Section three exists to close.

@slide two-col
kicker: A failure you've already met
## Ticket 4471, renamed correctly

::: cols
:: card bad
### How it looked at the time
- The summary blended two different tickets together
- Blamed on the model "getting confused"
- Fixed by rewriting the system prompt, four times
:: card good
### What it actually was
- An old resolved ticket, still sitting in the index
- Retrieved alongside the real one, both relevant-looking
- Near-duplicate confusion, fixed by removing the stale copy
:::

@narrate
Remember Ticket 4471 from the context engineering lecture. That bug
now has a name, and it's worth hearing it named formally, because
you'll meet this exact shape again in your own system.

Near-duplicate confusion happens whenever two versions of similar
content both sit in the index, both look relevant to the same query,
and both get retrieved together. Neither one is individually wrong.
The model, reading both, produces something blended and false. It
isn't a reasoning failure. It's an index that was never cleaned of the
document it superseded.

Notice how this connects directly back to the honest caveat on
chunking, two lectures ago: an embedding covering more than one idea
matches worse than a focused one. Two near-duplicate chunks, retrieved
side by side, do something similar to the model's reasoning. It's now
holding two versions of the truth at once, with nothing in the prompt
telling it which one is current.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat

::: callout Be straight about this
Passing your eval once isn't permanent. ==Your corpus keeps changing
even when your code never does==, growing duplicates and going stale
in ways a launch-day scorecard can't see coming.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because a RAG system is unusual in exactly this
way: the code can stay frozen and the system can still degrade.

A normal feature breaks when someone changes the code. A RAG system
can break when someone on a completely different team uploads a
revised policy document and forgets to remove the old one, six months
after your launch review closed the file. Nobody touched the
pipeline. The corpus underneath it quietly grew a near-duplicate, and
your scorecard from launch day has no idea.

That's the argument for treating retrieval quality the way Section
four treats a golden set: something you re-check on a schedule, not
something you verify once and consider finished. A PRD that specifies
a threshold and never revisits it has quietly assumed the world holds
still. This section keeps proving that it doesn't.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely

::: definition Corpus drift
The slow accumulation of staleness, duplicates, and permission gaps in
a RAG system's underlying documents, happening independently of any
code change, and invisible to a scorecard run only once.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Search your own index for two documents covering the same topic. If
you find one, you've found a live case of near-duplicate confusion,
not a hypothetical one.
:::

@narrate
That's the concept worth carrying past this lecture: the pipeline can
be perfect and the system can still drift, because the documents
underneath it have a life of their own.

The exercise takes ten minutes. Search for the same topic twice in
your own knowledge base. If two documents come back covering it,
you've found this lecture's failure mode sitting live in your own
system, not a warning about someone else's.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Search your index for one topic covered by two different documents
2. Check who owns removing a document once it's been superseded

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Run the search. One topic, two documents, is often all it takes to
find your first real instance of near-duplicate confusion.

Then ask a question most launches never answer: who's actually
responsible for pulling a document out of the index once it's been
replaced. If the honest answer is nobody, that's the gap this whole
section keeps returning to, an unowned decision quietly becoming
someone's Tuesday.

Next lecture goes deep on the two failures we only named today:
permissions and freshness, the two realities that break more enterprise
RAG demos than anything else on this list.
