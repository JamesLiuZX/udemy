@slide quote
kicker: Section 10 · The trust you spend slowly
attrib: What confidence sounds like before anyone's checked it

> "It's pretty much ready."

@narrate
A stakeholder's expectations rarely come from your golden set. They
come from a keynote demo, a competitor's launch post, or a headline
about a frontier model, none of which mention the failure category your
own eval work already found. Your job isn't to dampen their enthusiasm.
It's to replace an expectation built on marketing with one built on
evidence.

That's a different skill from simply being the person who says "it's
not ready yet." Vague caution earns exactly as little trust as vague
hype, because neither one gives a stakeholder anything they can check
later against what actually happened.

@slide callout
kicker: Where the gap actually comes from
::: callout Assume good faith
They're reacting rationally to what they've actually seen: a polished
demo, a viral post, a press release. Nobody handed them your golden
set.
:::

@narrate
This gap isn't a communication failure on the stakeholder's part, and
treating it that way is the fastest way to lose the conversation before
it starts. Where would their expectation have come from, if not the
same marketing everyone else sees? Assume good faith about that, and
spend the conversation closing the gap, not arguing they were wrong to
have one at all.

Think of the stakeholder relationship the same way 9.5 asked you to
think about a leadership dashboard: something that has to survive
contact with a bad number eventually, not something optimised to look
good in this one meeting. A relationship that's only ever heard good
news breaks the first time it hears anything else.

@slide table
kicker: Translate the hedge
## What a stakeholder hears, and the specific claim to say instead

| Instead of | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "It's pretty much ready" | "94% on our golden set, fails on this named category" |
| "The model's really good now" | "Cleared 7.4's floor, here's the model we picked and why" |
| "It should be fine" | "Blast radius is bounded, here's the named approver" |

@narrate
Read the right column as a direct export of tools you've already
built, not new language to invent under pressure. A pass rate from 4.5,
a scorecard decision from 7.4, a blast radius sort from 6.6, each one
already exists before the stakeholder conversation even starts.

Notice what every right-column sentence has that the left column
lacks: something a stakeholder could check later. "Ninety-four percent,
fails on this category" is a claim reality can prove wrong, which is
exactly what makes it trustworthy now. "It's pretty much ready" can't be
checked against anything, so it never earns or loses credibility. It
just gets forgotten until something breaks.

Notice, too, that the right column is shorter to say than the left,
not longer, once you've actually got the number in hand. "Ninety-four
percent, fails on this category" takes about as long to say as "it's
pretty much ready." The specificity isn't extra work in the room. It's
extra work beforehand, done once, back in Section four, long before
this conversation ever happens.

@slide two-col
kicker: What you actually put in front of them
## The same demo, cherry-picked and representative

::: cols
:: card bad
### The happy-path demo
- Three cases, hand-picked, all clean successes
- The known failure category never comes up
- Trust breaks the first time a stakeholder hits it themselves
:: card good
### A representative demo
- One clean success, one boundary case from the golden set
- The known limitation named before anyone finds it alone
- Trust survives the first real failure, because it was already named
:::

@narrate
Notice the good column doesn't demo worse. It demos honestly, using a
boundary case straight out of 4.2's golden set instead of avoiding one.

The difference shows up later, not in the room. A stakeholder who's
never seen the feature fail treats the first failure as a broken
promise. A stakeholder who's already seen a named limitation, in a
controlled demo, on your terms, treats the same failure as an expected
edge case. Identical failure, completely different trust outcome,
decided entirely by what was shown weeks earlier.

Choosing the boundary case deliberately is harder than it sounds,
because the instinct under pressure, especially before an important
stakeholder meeting, pulls hard toward the safest possible showing.
Resist it specifically here. The whole value of naming a limitation
before someone finds it is lost if the limitation shown is a trivial
one nobody would have hit anyway. Pick the real one, the case that
actually worries you, not a token weakness chosen for how harmless it
looks.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
Sandbagging every estimate is its own failure, not a safe default.
==A number that's always beaten by a huge margin stops being
information==, the same way an inflated one does.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because the instinct after being burned once is
to swing hard toward maximum caution, and that instinct has its own
cost.

If every estimate you give is quietly padded three times over, a
stakeholder learns to mentally discount everything you say, which means
your actual signal, the case where something really is at risk, stops
landing too. The goal was never pessimism. It was calibration, the same
word 4.8 used for a golden set's own honesty: a number that's right
about as often as it claims to be, not padded in either direction.

Watch for the specific symptom of over-correction: a stakeholder starts
routinely double-checking numbers you've already checked, or quietly
building their own buffer on top of your buffer. That's not caution on
their part. It's the market price of a track record that's stopped
being informative, and it's exactly as expensive to earn back as trust
lost the other way.

Aim for a number that's wrong in both directions roughly as often as an
honest estimate should be, not one that's never once come in worse than
promised. A track record with zero misses in either direction isn't a
sign of skill. It's usually a sign of padding thick enough to hide the
real variance underneath it.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition A calibrated expectation
A specific, checkable claim, backed by a number this course already
taught you to produce, stated without padding it safe in either
direction.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Take your next stakeholder update. Find one hedge, "should be fine,"
"pretty solid," and replace it with the specific number behind it.
:::

@narrate
That's the standard worth holding the next update to, and the exercise
is worth running on a real sentence you were about to send, not a
hypothetical one.

Most hedges, looked at honestly, already have a real number sitting
behind them somewhere, a pass rate, a scorecard result, a blast radius
row. The hedge isn't hiding an absence of evidence. It's usually hiding
evidence nobody bothered to quote directly.

Do this exercise on the update you were about to send today, not a
hypothetical future one. The habit only forms by catching a real hedge
in the moment it's about to go out, not by reviewing old messages after
the fact when there's nothing left to fix.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Replace one hedge in your next update with the number behind it
2. Plan your next demo to include one named boundary case

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Find the hedge first. It's usually the sentence that felt safest to
write, which is exactly why it's worth checking for a real number
underneath.

Then look at your next planned demo and add one case you know will
show a limitation, on purpose, before someone else finds it by
accident.

Neither step requires new work. The number behind the hedge and the
boundary case in your golden set both already exist. This is entirely
about what you choose to say out loud, not what you have left to build.

discipline applies to a whole quarter's commitments, not just one
