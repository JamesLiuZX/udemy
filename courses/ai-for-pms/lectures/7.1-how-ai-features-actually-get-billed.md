@slide statement
kicker: Section 7 · The billing mechanics
## A ten-turn conversation doesn't cost ten times one turn. It costs closer to five times that.
lead: The reason is mechanical, not mysterious, and it's the single most common surprise in a first AI feature's bill.

@narrate
Every call to a model bills the tokens it actually processes, in and
out. That sounds simple, and the surprising part is what counts as "in"
once a conversation has more than one turn.

This is worth learning properly, not just accepting as a line item on
an invoice, because it's the number every finance conversation about
this feature eventually comes back to. Get the mechanism right here and
the rest of this section, cost models, caching, margins, all rest on
solid ground.

Most chat features resend the entire conversation history with every
new message, because the model has no memory between calls. It only
knows what's in the prompt you send this time. Turn ten doesn't bill
turn ten's tokens. It bills turn ten's tokens plus every turn before it,
again.

That single fact explains more surprising invoices than any other
mechanic in this section. A team ships a feature, tests it with short
three or four turn conversations, prices it from that, and launches.
Real users have longer conversations than test users do, almost always,
and the bill scales with conversation length in a way three-turn testing
never reveals.

@slide table
kicker: What actually gets billed
## History resent in full, every single turn

| Turn | New content | History resent | Billed this turn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 200 | 0 | 200 |
| 3 | 200 | 400 | 600 |
| 5 | 200 | 800 | 1,000 |
| 7 | 200 | 1,200 | 1,400 |
| 10 | 200 | 1,800 | 2,000 |

@narrate
Read the middle column, because it's the one nobody budgets for. Two
hundred tokens of genuinely new content arrives each turn. Everything
else in that row is the same conversation being billed again, in full,
because the model needed it in context to answer coherently.

Sum the whole conversation and the total billed across ten turns is
eleven thousand tokens, not the two thousand you'd get multiplying one
turn's cost by ten. That's not a bug in anyone's implementation. It's
the direct, mechanical consequence of 1.2's lesson: the model has no
memory, so the full context has to travel with every single call.

Notice the shape of that growth too, not just the total. It's linear,
turn by turn, which means a feature that seems perfectly affordable at
turn three can be paying five times as much per turn by turn ten. Nobody
decided to spend more. The conversation simply kept going, the way real
support and coaching conversations actually do.

@slide two-col
kicker: The other asymmetry
## Input and output are priced differently, and the gap is large

::: cols
:: card bad
### What people assume
- Tokens cost roughly the same whether they're in or out
- A short question costs about what a short answer costs
- Total cost scales with total tokens, input and output treated alike
:: card good
### What's actually true
- Output tokens are typically priced several times higher than input
- A short question with a long answer can cost more than a long question with a short one
- Generation length is usually the bigger lever on cost, not prompt length
:::

@narrate
This asymmetry exists because generating a token is more computationally
expensive than reading one, and providers price accordingly. The
practical consequence lands squarely on feature design.

A feature that asks a model to summarise in three bullet points costs
less than one that asks for a detailed paragraph, even if the input
prompt is identical both times. Before optimising a prompt to be
shorter, check which side of the conversation is actually driving the
bill. It's often the output length, not the input you spent an afternoon
trimming.

This is worth checking specifically because the two fixes look similar
from the outside and cost completely different amounts of engineering
effort. Trimming a system prompt by a hundred words is an afternoon's
work for a modest saving. Capping response length, or restructuring a
prompt so the model naturally answers more concisely, often moves the
bill further for less effort, precisely because output is the side
carrying the higher price.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest caveat
::: callout Be straight about this
Resending history isn't a mistake to eliminate. ==It's what makes the
model coherent across turns==, and the fix is caching it cheaply, not
refusing to send it.
:::

@narrate
Say the honest caveat, because the instinct after seeing that table is
to treat resent history as pure waste, and it isn't.

Without the full history in context, the model has no idea what "it"
referred to two turns ago, and the feature stops working. The real fix
isn't sending less history. It's paying less to send it, which is
exactly what prompt caching exists for, and that's next lecture but one.
For now, just carry the honest number: a long conversation costs more
per turn than the turn before it, by design, not by accident.

Some teams respond to this table by truncating history aggressively,
dropping everything past the last few turns to control cost. That
trades a real dollar saving for a real quality cost: the feature starts
forgetting things a user said honestly expected it to remember. Whether
that trade is worth it depends entirely on the feature, and it's a
decision worth making deliberately rather than discovering by accident
once the bill arrives.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it precisely
::: definition Billed tokens
Every token the model actually processes on a given call, input and
output combined, including resent history. Not the tokens a user typed
this turn. All of them, every time.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Estimate your longest real conversation's turn count and average turn
size. Multiply it out the way this lecture's table did. Compare that to
what you assumed the feature cost.
:::

@narrate
That's the definition worth keeping, and the word doing the real work
is combined. Input, output, and history, all counted together, every
call.

Run the estimate on your own feature's longest realistic conversation,
not the three-turn demo everyone actually tests with. The gap between
that number and what you assumed is usually the first real finding this
section produces.

It's worth doing this before the model selection conversation too, not
after. A cheaper model looks like the obvious fix for a high bill, but
if the actual driver is history resend or output length, switching
models barely touches it. Diagnosing which mechanic is actually
responsible comes first; the fix follows from that, not the other way
round.

@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## Two things worth doing now

1. Estimate the billed tokens for your longest realistic conversation, not the demo
2. Check whether your feature's cost driver is really input length or output length

@narrate
Two things before you move on.

Do the estimate honestly, using a conversation length a real user would
actually reach, not the tidy few-turn exchange in a product demo.

Then check which side of the bill is actually doing the damage. Most
people assume it's the prompt. Next lecture builds the full cost model
that answers this properly, with real numbers instead of an estimate.
