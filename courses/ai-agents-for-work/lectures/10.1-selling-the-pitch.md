kicker: What Marcus actually wanted to hear
::: example Not "I've been using AI"
A number, a safeguard, and one specific thing he was being asked to
approve. Enthusiasm convinces nobody who has ever been burned by a
vendor pitch, and you have almost certainly sat across from someone
who has.
:::

@narrate
Priya's conversation with Marcus took four minutes. Not because he was
easy to convince, but because she had already done the work Section 9
walked you through: a real number, honestly counted, and a clear answer
to what could go wrong. You have that same work sitting in your own
C09 tracker right now.

Remember who Marcus is, from the day he first appeared in this
course: a director who wants numbers, not enthusiasm, and who is
sceptical of "AI" as a word while being entirely persuadable by a
saved afternoon he can see. That is not one man's quirk, it is the
default setting of everyone who approves things for a living. They
have sat through vendor demos that went nowhere and pilots that
quietly died, and the word AI now arrives pre-discounted. Which is
good news for you, because the discount applies to claims, and you
are not bringing claims. You are bringing six weeks of a tracker.


@slide two-col
kicker: Two ways to open this conversation
## Enthusiasm versus evidence

::: cols
:: card bad
### "You should see what AI can do now"
Sounds like a sales pitch. Invites the exact skepticism it's trying to
avoid.
:: card good
### "Here's what I built, what it saved, and what nearly went wrong"
Sounds like a status update from someone who already thought this
through. Because it is.
:::

@narrate
The second version works because it leads with the thing a careful
manager is actually listening for: evidence that you have already
considered the downside, not just the upside.

Yes, "what nearly went wrong" means telling Marcus about the near
miss, and telling it on purpose. The instinct to hide Section 8's
story from the pitch is exactly backwards. Told properly, in one
breath, it is the strongest slide you have: a mistake happened, the
guardrail held, nothing reached a client, and the process got
stricter afterwards. That is not a confession, it is a
demonstration that your safety story has been tested by reality
rather than asserted on a slide. Managers do not expect zero
incidents, they expect contained ones, and you are one of the few
people who can prove containment rather than promise it. Tell the
story in three sentences, unprompted, and watch what it does to the
temperature of the room.


@slide callout
kicker: The question a careful manager always asks
::: callout "What happens when it gets something wrong?"
Priya had an answer ready, because Section 8 gave her one: it drafts,
it never sends, and she reads everything for two weeks before trusting
it unsupervised. A manager who hears that answer stops treating the
request as a risk and starts treating it as something already managed.
:::

@narrate
If you cannot answer this question about your own agents yet, that is
worth noticing before you pitch anyone. Go back to Section 1's job
description and Section 8's worksheet until you can, and only then book
the actual conversation.

And make the ask genuinely specific, because vague asks are where
these conversations go to die. "Support for using AI more" gives a
manager nothing to approve and everything to worry about. Priya's
ask was one sentence: permission to share four workflow templates
with the three others on her team, each person connecting their own
accounts, each running the same trial she ran. Small, bounded,
reversible, and costing Marcus nothing but a yes. A manager who
cannot approve that in the meeting was never going to approve
anything, and now you know that too, cheaply.


@slide bullets
kicker: The structure that actually works
## Four parts, in order

1. What you built and for what task, in one sentence
2. What it saved, from your C09 tracker, honestly counted
3. What safeguards are already in place, named specifically
4. The one specific thing you are asking for

@narrate
Practise the four parts aloud once before any real meeting, in
order, without notes. Not to memorise a script, but because the
order carries the persuasion: built, saved, safeguarded, asking. If
you find yourself starting with the ask, or worse with the
technology, the structure has collapsed into the vendor pitch your
listener has already survived a dozen times. Ninety seconds of
rehearsal keeps the spine straight under questions, and questions
are a good sign: a manager probing your safeguards is a manager
already imagining saying yes. The pitch that fails is rarely the one
that gets grilled. It is the one that gets a polite nod and no
follow-up, because nothing in it gave the listener anything solid
enough to push against.

Next lecture turns this structure into the actual one-pager you hand
over, so you are not reconstructing it from memory in the meeting, on
the spot, with everyone watching.
