@slide statement
kicker: The wrong belief worth correcting
## The temptation is to explain how it works. Nobody deciding whether to approve this needs that.

lead: A one-pager earns its name by leaving almost everything out, on purpose.

@narrate
You know how n8n works, what a system prompt is, what draft-only means
at the node level. None of that belongs in front of your manager. They
need three things: what it does, what it's worth, and what could go
wrong. Everything else is for you, not for them, however satisfying it
would be to explain.

Why does "how" actively hurt, rather than just pad? Because a
decision-maker cannot verify it, and unverifiable detail creates
work instead of confidence. Every node name on the page is a thing
Marcus cannot assess, which quietly turns into a question he has to
either ask or swallow, and a page that makes its reader feel
unqualified gets deferred, not approved. Deferral is how small good
ideas die: not rejected, just moved to a meeting that never quite
happens. The cut is not dumbing down. It is moving the conversation
onto the only ground where the reader is the expert: whether the
trade is worth it.


@slide two-col
kicker: What stays, what goes
## The cut that makes it actually one page

::: cols
:: card good
### Stays
The task, the number, the safeguard, the specific ask
:: card bad
### Goes
Node names, prompt wording, which model you chose, anything that
answers "how" instead of "what" and "so what"
:::

@narrate
This is a genuinely hard cut for anyone who just spent ten sections
learning how the machinery works, you included. The instinct to explain
it is exactly the instinct a one-pager has to resist, and the
consolation is real: everything you cut still exists, in your notes,
ready for the one meeting in ten where somebody actually asks how.


@slide callout
kicker: The exception worth keeping
::: callout One line about the safeguard is not machinery, it's the point
"Draft only, I review everything for two weeks before trusting it" is
not a technical detail. It answers the one question every decision-maker
actually asks. Keep that line. Cut almost everything else that sounds
technical.
:::

@narrate
That is the whole test. Does this sentence answer what it does, what it
saved, or what could go wrong? If it does not do one of those three
things, it belongs in your own notes, not on the page you actually hand
over to someone deciding whether to say yes.

Run two real sentences through it to feel the edge. "The agent uses
a Switch node to route on the model's verdict": answers how, cut it,
however proud of it you are. "If it misjudges an email, the worst
case is a flag I disagree with at review, nothing reaches a client":
answers what could go wrong, and stays, jargon-free, exactly as
written. Notice the second sentence is actually the more technical
achievement, wearing plain clothes. That is the register the whole
page wants.


@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## What to do now

1. Draft your one-pager, then read it back and cut anything that explains "how"
2. Time yourself reading it aloud. If it takes more than ninety seconds, cut further
3. Show it to someone who has never heard about your agents and ask what they understood

@narrate
The two checks in that list earn their place differently. The
ninety second read-aloud is a length gate that cannot be argued
with, because a busy reader gives you about that long before
deciding whether to keep reading. The naive-reader test catches the
subtler failure: words you no longer notice are jargon, because ten
sections of this course made them furniture. Whatever your test
reader stumbles on, the stumble is the edit list, delivered free by
the one reviewer who cannot be fooled by familiarity. Next lecture is
where you actually fill the page in, using C10 and your own numbers
from C09.
