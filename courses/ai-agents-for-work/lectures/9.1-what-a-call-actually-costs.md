@slide statement
kicker: The number that shouldn't be possible
## A month of Agent 1, running against Priya's real inbox, costs a few cents. Not a few dollars. Cents.

lead: This surprises almost everyone the first time they see the real number.

@narrate
Gemini's Flash model, the one every agent in this course was built
against, prices out at roughly thirty cents per million words of input
and two dollars fifty per million words of output. Priya's forty one
daily emails do not come close to using a million of anything in a
month.

Why so cheap, when the technology is supposedly the expensive kind?
Because you pay by the word, roughly: words the model reads, words it
writes, priced separately, and an office task barely registers on
either meter. An email is a couple of hundred words in; a triage
verdict is one line out. You are buying judgement by the thimble,
and a thimble of judgement, it turns out, costs a fraction of a
cent. The intuition that AI must be expensive comes from what it
costs to build these models. Renting one, per call, at office
volumes, is one of the cheapest inputs your job has ever had.

@narrate
That number is not a rounding trick. Small, short, structured tasks like
triage and extraction are exactly what these models are cheapest at.
The bill that actually adds up belongs to a different kind of workload
entirely: long documents, high volume, or a model far more capable than
the job needs.

Know what would actually move the number, so surprises cannot. Feed
the agent fifty page policy documents instead of emails and the
words-read meter starts mattering. Point it at a shared inbox with
ten times the volume and the arithmetic scales with it, still
modestly. Swap the small fast model for the largest one out of vague
ambition and the rate itself jumps, which is why Section 2.2's
surgeon-opening-the-post warning was also a budgeting rule. None of
those describe your four agents, which is exactly why the estimate
holds.

@slide callout
kicker: The bill you are not paying
::: callout Per call, not per seat
The obvious alternative is a subscription automation product, priced
per seat per month, used or not. Renting a model by the call means a
quiet fortnight costs pennies, a heavy season costs slightly more
pennies, and the meter is on your own dashboard.
:::

@narrate
The comparison is where the value actually lives, so make it out
loud when you present your numbers. Paying only for what ran, at
rates you can audit any morning, is an unglamorous superpower for
anyone who has ever had to justify a software line item, and it is
the difference between asking a manager for a budget and telling
them about a rounding error.


@slide callout
kicker: Why the pricing story changes fast
::: callout This exact model may not exist by the time you build this
Gemini's 2.5 generation of models has an announced retirement date
later this year. Prices for whatever replaces it will differ, and
probably will have dropped, since that has been the trend every quarter
this course has been in production. Check your provider's current
pricing page before you build, not this slide.
:::

@narrate
This is exactly why the course keeps tool-specific facts like this one
quarantined to lectures like this, instead of scattered through every
build. The judgement spine survives a price change. This one number does
not need to.

The re-check, when you need it, is a sixty second errand with two
stops. Your provider's pricing page tells you the current rate. Your
own usage dashboard, on the same account where you made the API key,
tells you what last month actually cost, which is the only number
that ever needs defending. Do that once a quarter, when the
freshness pass on your own materials comes round, and pricing news
between checks becomes something you can safely ignore. Headlines
about AI costs are written about training runs and enterprise
contracts, not about nine hundred emails a month. Your dashboard is
the only pricing journalism your agents will ever need.


@slide bullets
kicker: The number that actually matters more
## Cost was never the hard constraint

1. AI cost for all four of Priya's agents together: a handful of dollars a month
2. Her time reading every output during Trial: real, and worth counting honestly
3. The number worth optimising is hours saved, not cents spent

@narrate
Keep that ranking in mind for the rest of this section, because it
inverts where most people put their attention. The model bill is the
number everyone asks about and the one that matters least: rounding
error against any salary. Your checking time is the real cost of
running agents, which is why this course made you count it from the
first trial, and why the next lecture's tracker subtracts it before
claiming a single saved minute. Optimise the hours, tolerate the
cents. Next lecture turns that into a document you fill in with your
own real numbers.
