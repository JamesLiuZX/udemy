@slide statement
kicker: Priya's actual total, logged over six weeks
## Five and a half hours a week. Under five dollars a month. Every output still checked.

lead: Not a projection. A number counted the way Section 9.2 taught you to count it.

@narrate
Two hours from Agent 1, no longer hunting for the one email that
matters in a pile of forty. About fifty minutes from Agent 2, one clean
list per call instead of scrawled notes half-remembered by Friday.
Forty minutes from Agent 3, the weekly arithmetic gone. Half an hour
from Agent 4, chases that used to slip now sitting drafted and waiting.

Where did the figure come from? Six weeks of the C09 tracker, filled
in weekly, checking time subtracted every row, exactly as the last
lecture taught. That provenance is the difference between this slide
and every breathless automation statistic you have ever scrolled
past: you are looking at one person's counted week, not an
industry's press release, and by the time you present your own
version, it will have the same pedigree.

The six weeks were not flat, either, and the shape is worth knowing
in advance. Week one barely broke even: trials were running, every
output was being read, and the checking time ate most of the
saving. The number grew as trust climbed the ladder, as briefings
absorbed their tuning edits, and as checking thinned to sampling.
If your own week two looks unimpressive, that is not failure, that
is the curve, and it is precisely why the tracker runs for six
weeks before anyone presents anything.

## Four agents, one honest total

| Agent | Net hours saved, weekly |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Inbox triage | 2.0 |
| 2. Meeting notes | 0.8 |
| 3. Weekly report | 0.7 |
| 4. Follow-up chaser | 0.5 |
| **Total** | **~4.0** |

@narrate
Notice that table adds to four hours, not five and a half. The extra
ninety minutes in Priya's real total came from something the tracker
does not have a row for: fewer things falling through the cracks
altogether, and less time spent each week simply worrying about what she
might have missed. That number is real but softer, and the honest thing
to do is name it separately rather than fold it into the hard total.

Read the ranking in the table too, because it teaches you where to
start in your own job. Triage wins by a distance, and not because it
is the cleverest agent: it is the simplest. It wins on frequency.
Forty one emails a day, every day, each shaving a sliver of
attention, compounds past anything a weekly task can offer, however
painful the weekly task feels. When you pick your own first agent
after this course, resist the dramatic monthly chore and automate
the boring daily one. The maths is on the boring one's side, and
the daily agent also earns its trial evidence four times faster,
which Section 7 taught you to value.


@slide callout
kicker: Why the honest split matters more than the total
::: callout A number you can defend beats a number that sounds better
Four hours, cleanly counted, survives a skeptical question. Five and a
half hours, with ninety minutes of it fuzzy, invites exactly the kind
of pushback that makes a whole pitch look shaky. Report both, labelled
honestly, and let the number that can be questioned stand on its own.
:::

@narrate
This is the same instinct as Section 7's spot checks, aimed at a
spreadsheet instead of an agent's output: check your own numbers as
carefully as you would check theirs.

And when someone hears "four hours" and asks, is that all? Give the
answer Priya gives Marcus in the next section: four hours a week is
two hundred hours a year, five full working weeks, bought back for
the price of a sandwich a month, counted by someone who subtracts
her own checking time. Said that way, the modest number stops
sounding modest, and it still contains nothing anyone can take
apart. Big claims impress until the first question. Clean claims
survive the tenth.
