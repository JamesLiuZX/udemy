@slide statement
kicker: Who actually reads this
## Marcus does not want five rows of raw data. He wants three sentences and one number he can repeat in his own meeting.

lead: That is the translation this prompt has to make.

@narrate
Add an AI Agent node after your Sheets node, attach your Gemini model
the same way as the last two builds, and open the Prompt field. By the
third build this should take you about two minutes, and noticing that
it now does is the quiet payoff of doing the same steps three times.
This time the User Message is simple: hand over every row the Sheets
node read, as one block. You are not selecting which rows matter, you are
handing over all of them and letting the system message decide what to
do with each one.

@slide callout
kicker: The fair objection
::: callout Should a language model be doing arithmetic at all?
Over a small table, yes, provably, with a trial to back it. Over
hundreds of rows, no: let the sheet compute a summary row and hand the
agent the totals. The model writes. The sheet counts.
:::

@narrate
Fair question, and the honest answer has a boundary on it. Over a pipeline of five, ten,
forty rows, counting and summing sits comfortably inside what these
models do reliably, and the trial exists to prove that against hand
counts before anything rides on it. Over five hundred rows, stop:
that is spreadsheet work. At that scale the right design is to let
the sheet compute its own totals in a summary row and hand the agent
those, so the model writes and the sheet counts, each doing the job
it is actually good at. Priya's forty one accounts sit well inside
the comfortable zone. Know which zone your own sheet is in before
you copy this build onto it.


@slide example
kicker: The system message for this build
::: example Read it exactly as you would type it
"You are a weekly renewals report assistant. You are given a pipeline of
rows: Client, Premium, Renewal Date, Status. Write a short report with
three parts: how many renewals are open versus closed, total premium at
stake across open renewals, and any open renewal within 14 days of its
date, named specifically. Use only the numbers given. Never mark a
renewal closed unless the Status column says so. If you are unsure, say
so rather than guessing."
:::

@narrate
That last sentence is new, and it matters more here than anywhere else
so far. Agent 1 was told what to ignore. Agent 2 was told what not to
invent. This one is told to say "I'm not sure" out loud rather than
quietly picking an answer. A report that admits uncertainty is more
useful than one that hides it, because Priya can act on the first kind
and gets blindsided by the second.

@slide bullets
kicker: Choices that are yours to own
## Four deliberate settings inside that briefing

1. Columns named in the header row's exact words
2. A 14 day window, chosen, not defaulted
3. Risky renewals named, never just counted
4. Only the numbers given: the sheet is the authority

@narrate
The columns are named in the exact words of the header row, because
Section 5.2 made those headers an interface and the briefing is where
the other end of that contract lives. Fourteen days is not a magic
number, it is Priya's chasing window: far enough out that a reminder
still helps, near enough that it is worth interrupting a Monday for.
Yours might be seven or thirty, and the point is that somebody chose
it on purpose. "Named specifically" keeps the risk list concrete,
because a warning without a client name is a mood, not an action.
And "use only the numbers given" quietly blocks a failure you might
not expect: a model helpfully rounding, or adjusting a figure that
looks like a typo. The sheet is the authority. The agent repeats it
or questions it, never improves it.


@slide table
kicker: The translation, made visible
## From a row to a sentence Marcus can repeat

| What the sheet says | What the report says |
| --- | --- |
| 5 rows, Status: 4 Open, 1 Closed | "Four renewals open, one closed" |
| Premium column, summed over open rows | "$53,000 of premium at stake" |
| One open row with a date inside 14 days | "Dunmore Electrical renews on 19 August: open, inside the window, worth a call this week" |

@narrate
Read the right hand column aloud and notice it survives being said in
a corridor. That is the actual test of a director's report: can the
reader repeat it, thirty seconds later, to someone more senior,
without the sheet in front of them? Rows cannot be repeated in
corridors. Sentences with one number each can. Everything this prompt
does, the counting, the summing, the fourteen day window, exists to
manufacture that repeatability, and nothing in it exists for any
other reason. It is also why the report stays short on purpose: a
third paragraph would be the place a wrong number hides. Three parts,
three sentences each at most, every number traceable to a cell. If
Marcus wants more, the sheet is one click away, and the report's job
was to make him know which click to want. When you adapt this build,
keep the discipline: pick the two or three numbers your reader would
repeat upward, and refuse the rest of the sheet a seat in the report,
however interesting it looks on a quiet week.

@narrate
A word on where the draft should physically land, because "ready for
review" has to mean somewhere you will actually be on Monday. Point
the final step at your own eyes, whatever that means in your setup: a
document the workflow appends to, a message to yourself, a file in a
folder you open anyway. The one address it must not have yet is
Marcus's, or your Marcus's. The report earns its way into a
director's inbox at the end of a three-Monday trial, carried there by
Priya, not posted there by a schedule.

With the prompt written, your agent can now turn a table into a
report. Before testing it, read your briefing aloud once, the standing
ritual: if any sentence in it is one you could not say to a temp
taking over the Monday report, the model will stumble on it too. Next
lecture proves the build, against the exact numbers you would want
checked by hand first.
