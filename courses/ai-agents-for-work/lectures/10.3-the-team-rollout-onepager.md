@narrate
This is the document Priya actually handed Marcus. Open the C10
template. Six short sections: what this is, the numbers from C09, the
safeguards named plainly, what you are asking for, what you are
explicitly not asking for, and, if colleagues are involved, what
they commit to as well.

Read the six as one arc and you can hear the whole course in
miniature. What this is: the job description, compressed to a
sentence. The numbers: Section 9, counted clean. The safeguards:
Sections 6 and 8, in plain clothes. The ask and the not-ask: Section
9.4's limits, turned outward. The commitments: Section 7's trials,
made contractual. Nothing on the page is new, which is why filling
it in takes twenty minutes and not a weekend: you are transcribing
work you already did, into the order a decision-maker reads in.


@slide example
kicker: The section people most often skip, and shouldn't
::: example "What you are not asking for"
Priya's line: "I'm not asking to change how sign-off works. That
decision stays exactly where it is." One sentence, and it is doing more
work than the rest of the page combined.
:::

@narrate
Naming the boundary is what turns a request into something a careful
manager can approve quickly, because it closes off the worry they would
otherwise have to ask about themselves. You already know why this
matters: it is Section 9.4's never-automate list, restated for an
audience of one.

The section works because approvals are really priced on the
downside. Every manager reading a request is privately extending it:
if I say yes to this, what am I saying yes to next? The not-asking
line answers that unasked question in writing, which collapses the
imagined slope back into the actual request. Write yours for your
own job: not asking to change who approves invoices, not asking to
let anything send without review, whatever the escalation your
reader would silently fear. If you cannot think of one, you have
not imagined being your manager for long enough yet.


@slide bullets
kicker: If your team is involved
## The checklist that keeps a rollout from becoming a shortcut

1. Each person runs their own Trial, even on a template that already works for you
2. Each person fills in C05 for their own scope, not a copy of yours
3. Spot checks continue for everyone, indefinitely, not just during the first week

@narrate
That first line matters more than it looks, and the next lecture tells
you exactly why: what happened the first week someone other than Priya
actually used one of these templates. Resist the urge to soften it
for colleagues who seem capable. Capability was never the variable
the trial tests. Their world is, and nobody's capability has ever
made their world match yours.


@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## What to do now

1. Fill in C10 completely, using your real C09 numbers
2. Read it aloud in under ninety seconds
3. Send it, or bring it to the actual conversation, this week if you can

@narrate
The "this week" is not motivational garnish. Your C09 numbers are at
their most defensible right now, while the weeks they describe are
still recent enough to answer questions about, and your own command
of the material fades faster than you expect once the course ends.
A one-pager delivered this week is a status update from someone on
top of their work. The same page in three months is a proposal you
have to warm up again. You now have every artifact this course
builds toward: ten documents, on your desk. Next lecture tells you
what happened the first time someone else opened one.
