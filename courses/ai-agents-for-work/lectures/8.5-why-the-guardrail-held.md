@slide callout
kicker: The question worth asking about any near miss
::: callout What actually stopped this, not what should have stopped it earlier
Two different questions. This lecture is about the second one, and it
is the one you will actually need an answer to the next time something
of yours nearly goes wrong.
:::

@narrate
It is tempting to end the last lecture's story at "the scope should have
been narrower," and stop there. That is true, and it is also not the
most important thing the incident proves. The more important question is
what actually held when the first line of defence failed.

The two questions produce two different futures, which is why the
distinction earns a lecture. Reviews that only ask what should have
stopped it earlier end in resolutions: be more careful, read consent
screens, never skip the worksheet. All true, all fragile, because
they repair the person, and the person was never the reliable part.
Asking what held ends somewhere sturdier: it finds the design that
did not care whether anyone was careful that Thursday, and tells you
to build more of that, in places your future carelessness cannot
reach. Resolutions decay in a quarter. Wiring does
not. You have heard that principle twice now; this is what it looks
like the day it is load bearing.

kicker: Two guardrails, only one of which failed
## Scope versus action

::: cols
:: card bad
### The guardrail that failed
The credential's scope. It was wider than intended, and that is a real
failure worth fixing, covered in Section 8.3's worksheet.
:: card good
### The guardrail that held
The action level. Draft-only, decided in Section 6, before this
capability was ever imagined. It did not depend on the scope being
right.
:::

@narrate
This is the whole argument of Section 6, proven under real conditions
instead of just asserted in a lecture. A wrong scope is a mistake. A
wrong scope on an agent that can only draft is a mistake that stays
small, because the thing that actually causes harm, sending something,
was never possible in the first place.

Why did one guardrail's failure tell you nothing about the other's?
Because the two were independent, and the independence was partly
about when each decision got made. The scope was decided in a hurry,
mid-flow, on the busy Thursday, by exactly the version of Priya most
likely to wave something through. The action level was decided five
weeks earlier, on day one, calm, with the job description open,
before any temptation existed. Layers protect you when they cannot
all fail for the same reason, and "decided by different versions of
you" is one of the cheapest independences available: put the
irreversibility guard where your calmest self placed it, and the
hurried self who mis-scopes a credential cannot also have removed
it, because the hurried self was never asked.


@slide callout
kicker: The layered guardrail, named
::: callout Redundancy is not overcaution, it's the whole design
Scope limits what an agent can see. Action level limits what it can do
with what it sees. Approval gates, from Section 8.1, add a human step
even when action is allowed. Build more than one of these into
everything you make, because Section 8.4 proves you cannot always get
the first one right.
:::

@narrate
That sentence, "you cannot always get the first one right," is not
pessimism. It is the entire reason a single guardrail is never enough,
however carefully you think you set it.

Be honest about the price of layers, because there is one: each adds
friction, and friction is why people strip guardrails in the first
place. The craft is choosing layers whose cost you stop noticing.
Draft-only costs a click per output. The review read costs minutes
you were partly spending anyway. A scoped credential costs ten
minutes once. Stack those three and you have real depth for almost
nothing, which is why they are this course's standard kit. A gate
on every trivial action, by contrast, is a layer people quietly
route around by Friday, and a routed-around guardrail is worse than
none, because it still shows up on paper as protection.

kicker: What changes in how you build, starting now
## Three habits from this section, carried forward

1. Run C08 on every addition, including the ones that feel too small to bother
2. Never let action level be the guardrail you assume is fine because scope looks fine
3. Treat draft-only as the default for any new agent, and earn your way up from there, not down
4. After any near miss of your own, ask what held before you ask what should have stopped it

@narrate
Four agents, one real incident, and a scope error that never became a
disaster. That is the standard the rest of this course holds itself
to, and notice it is a standard about outcomes, not perfection: the
method did not prevent the mistake, the method made the mistake
survivable, and those are different promises. Only the second one is
honest, and it is the one worth building for. Next lecture closes
the section, and Section 9 turns to the number every manager
eventually asks for: what this actually costs, and what it actually
saves.
