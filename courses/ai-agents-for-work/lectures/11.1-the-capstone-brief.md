@slide statement
kicker: The brief, in one sentence
## Pick one task you do every week and would rather not. Run this course's method on it, start to finish.

lead: Not a new idea to learn. The one method, run once for real, on something that matters to you.

@narrate
You have watched this method work four times against Priya's week. This
is where you find out it works against yours. Pick something real: not
a toy example, the actual task that eats twenty minutes you would rather
spend elsewhere. It does not need to be dramatic. A weekly status update
you copy from three systems by hand, a folder of receipts you sort every
Friday, an inbox rule you have meant to write for months: any of those
qualifies.

Working on your own task changes the feel of the method in one way
worth naming, and it is an advantage. Following Priya, the course
had to hand you her ground truth: what her clients are like, which
email mattered, what the sheet was supposed to say. On your own
task, you are the ground truth. You know which Tuesday email is the
dangerous one and what correct looks like on your desk, which is
exactly the independent knowledge Section 7.3 said safe delegation
depends on. The capstone is not harder than the builds you copied.
It is the first one where the most qualified trial reviewer in the
world is already sitting in your chair.


@slide bullets
kicker: The six steps, in order
## Everything you have already practised

1. Write the job description (C05): the job, scope, action level, ceiling
2. Build it in n8n: trigger, judgement, routing if needed
3. Test it against pinned, realistic data before spending a real call
4. Run a proper trial (C06), reading everything, for a length you decide up front
5. Run the guardrails worksheet (C08) if it touches anything new or sensitive
6. Log the real cost and time saved (C09), honestly

@narrate
Every one of those six steps is something you have already done at
least once in this course. Nothing here is new. What's new is that
nobody is handing you Priya's story bible to work from this time.

As a plan, the six steps are smaller than they look on a slide. One
and two are an afternoon: half an hour of writing, then a build you
have now done four times. Three is an hour with pinned data. Four
runs in the background of your normal week, costing minutes a day.
Five and six are paperwork measured in coffee breaks. The two
places people actually stall are worth naming in advance: step one,
polishing a job description that was good enough forty minutes ago,
and step four, abandoning the trial early because the first outputs
looked fine, which Section 7.1 has already armed you against. Start
the afternoon version this week, while the last section's momentum
is still yours to spend. The perfect version does not exist, and
the shipped version teaches you things the perfect one never would
have.


@slide callout
kicker: If you don't have an idea yet
::: callout Borrow the shape of one of Priya's four
Something you triage, something you extract from a longer document,
something you summarise on a schedule, or something you chase. Almost
every recurring task fits one of those four shapes.
:::

@narrate
Do not spend a week searching for the perfect task. A modest, real task
done properly beats an ambitious one left half finished, and that
sentence has been true since Section 1. If two candidates come to mind,
pick whichever one you can describe in a single sentence right now.
That sentence is the first line of your job description.

The four shapes are also a map back into the course, and using it is
encouraged, not cheating. A triage-shaped task starts from Section 3
and the C01 template. An extraction task starts from Section 4 and
C02. A summary on a rhythm, Section 5 and C03. A chase, Section 6
and C04, draft-only wiring included. Import the nearest template,
strip what your task does not need, rewrite the briefing in your own
words, and you have skipped an hour of plumbing without skipping a
minute of the method, because the method was never the plumbing.


@slide bullets
kicker: What "done" looks like
## The finish line for this capstone

1. A filled-in C05 job description
2. A working n8n workflow, tested against pinned data
3. A logged trial, however short, with at least one output read critically
4. A one-sentence answer to "what did this actually cost, and what did it save"

@narrate
Notice what the finish line does not require: a perfect agent, a
finished trial, or anything at Release. It requires the method,
executed once, with receipts, because the method is the thing you
are actually taking from this course. The agent is just this
month's proof of it. Next lecture works through a full example from
start to finish, so you can see the whole method run once more
before you run it on your own.
