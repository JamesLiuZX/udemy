@slide definition
kicker: Same habit, new build

::: definition Pin it before you pay for it
Section 3.5's rule holds here unchanged: prove the wiring against a
realistic sample before you spend a real AI call finding out it was
wrong.
:::

@narrate
Open your form trigger, find "set mock data," and paste in one realistic
transcript. This build was tested against a short, ordinary renewal
call between Priya and Ravi Chandra: some scheduling small talk, and two
real commitments buried inside it.

What does mock data look like for a form? The same shape a real
submission would have: a value for each of your three fields, meeting
title, attendees, transcript. Pin that once and you can rewire and
retune everything downstream without ever re-filling the form, which
matters here more than it did for email, because you will be tweaking
the extraction prompt several times and pasting a full transcript in
by hand each round is exactly the sort of friction that makes people
stop testing.


@slide example
kicker: What a good test transcript contains
::: example Not just the easy case
Ravi agrees to send a stock valuation by 20 August. He agrees to send a
fire safety certificate by the same date. In between, there's a full
exchange about a football game that means nothing to this agent's job.
:::

@narrate
That football exchange is doing real work in your test. If your agent
extracts it as an action item, you have found a mistake cheaply, before
a real client call ever reached it. That is the entire point of testing
with something realistically messy instead of a clean, easy sample that
was never going to trip anything up.

When you write your own test transcript, plant one more trap beyond
the small talk: a commitment made and then walked back. Someone says
they will send the figures Thursday, then two lines later, actually,
hold off until the contract is signed. Real calls do this constantly,
and it is the single hardest case for extraction, because the
commitment genuinely was said out loud. An agent that reports it
anyway is obeying the letter of your ceiling and missing the
conversation. Whether yours handles it, and how, is exactly the kind
of thing you want to know on pinned data, for free, rather than
discover in the minutes of a real client call.


@slide callout
kicker: Reading the output like a sceptic, not a fan
::: callout A tidy answer is not the same as a correct one
When you run this against pinned data, read every line against the
transcript itself. Two items, right owner, right date, nothing invented,
nothing missed. That is a pass. A confident-looking list with one
invented date is a fail, however well formatted it reads.
:::

@narrate
This is the single most important habit in this entire course, and it
shows up first here because extraction makes it easiest to see: an AI
agent's output always looks equally confident, whether it is right or
wrong. Reading it critically is not optional, and it never stops being
your job.

Notice what tidiness does to your own judgement, because that is the
mechanism to defend against. A cleanly formatted list borrows the
authority of every correct list you have ever read: the brackets are
right, the dates are shaped like dates, and your eye wants to approve
it on grounds that have nothing to do with the call. The defence is
mechanical, not willpower. Put the transcript and the output side by
side and tick each line against the sentence that justifies it. No
sentence, no tick. Thirty seconds per item, and it is the same
side-by-side habit the spot check rubric in Section 7 turns into a
permanent tool.


@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## What to check before you trust this build

1. Run it against your pinned transcript and count the action items by hand first
2. Compare your count to the agent's output, line by line
3. Deliberately try a transcript with zero action items, and confirm it says so plainly

@narrate
The order of the first two steps is not decoration. Count the items
yourself before you look at the agent's answer, because whichever list
you read first becomes the anchor the other one gets judged against.
Read the agent first and its answer quietly replaces your memory of
the call; you will find yourself agreeing with items you could not
have produced. Count first, and your list does the anchoring instead.
And the zero-item transcript in step three is not a throwaway: it is
the escape-hatch test, proving the agent can hand back an honest
nothing. An extraction agent that cannot say "nothing here" will
eventually manufacture something, and you want to know which kind you
built while the stakes are a pinned sample.

With that proof in hand, next lecture connects this agent to a real form
and ships it.
