@slide definition
kicker: The second half of the toolbox

::: definition n8n moves the email. The model reads it.
Two different jobs, two different tools. You already chose the first.
This lecture chooses the second, on the free route.
:::

@narrate
When you watched the inbox triage agent work in Section 0, the box doing
the actual reading was Google's Gemini, on its free tier. That was not
an arbitrary choice for a demo. It is genuinely the easiest way into this
course, and Priya starts here too, before her volume forces an upgrade
later in Section 9.

What is a model, stripped of the mystique? A service on the internet
that takes text in and gives text back. Your workflow sends it an email
plus your plain English rules; it returns its reading. That is the
entire transaction, repeated every run. Two useful things follow. The
providers are largely interchangeable from n8n's point of view: the
model sits in its own box, so swapping Gemini for another provider
later is a two minute change, not a rebuild. And because the models
improve every few months on their own, your agents quietly get better
without you touching anything. You are renting judgement by the call,
from a market that keeps cutting the price of it.


@slide table
kicker: Where the free tiers actually sit
## Three providers, three free routes

| Provider | Free route | Real limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Google Gemini | No card needed, sign in with a Google account | Roughly 10 to 30 requests a minute, plenty for one inbox |
| OpenAI | New account credit, a few dollars | Expires after three months |
| Anthropic | New account credit, a few dollars | Expires after thirty days |

@narrate
Read that middle row again. Gemini's free tier is not a trial that runs
out, it is a genuinely free tier with a rate limit instead of a clock.
For one person automating their own inbox, that rate limit will not be
the thing that stops you. That is why this course builds every demo
against it.

Now the cost that is not on the table, because it is not a price. Free
tiers are free because the provider gets something: on some of them,
what you send can be used to improve the service, under terms that
change more often than this course re-renders. That is a fine trade for
a practice inbox and a terrible default for client correspondence. So
the working rule while you learn is the one Priya follows: free tier
plus practice data until you have read your provider's current data
terms with work content specifically in mind. When the agents go live
on real client email in Section 3, that reading is a ten minute task
you do once, and it belongs to you, not to this course, because the
terms will be whatever they are the week you look.


@slide two-col
kicker: What changes once volume grows
## Free tier versus paying per call

::: cols
:: card good
### Where free tiers hold up
- One person's inbox
- A weekly report, once a week
- Learning and testing, this whole course
:: card bad
### Where they stop being enough
- A shared team inbox, hundreds of emails a day
- An agent running many times an hour
- Anything a client's experience depends on staying fast
:::

@narrate
Section 9 puts real numbers against this, but the shape of the decision
is simple. Free tiers are rate limited, not crippled. You will feel the
ceiling as a slowdown, not a bill, and by the time you feel it you will
already know exactly how much of your week the agent is worth, because
you will have been running it for weeks by then.

It is worth knowing what the ceiling feels like from inside n8n, so it
does not read as breakage the first time. The model's box goes red, and
the message mentions limits or quota. Nothing is wrong. The provider is
saying not this minute, and a minute later the same run succeeds. If
you see it more than occasionally, that is your volume talking, and it
is the most pleasant upgrade prompt in software: it means the agent is
being used enough to matter. One more choice hides inside each
provider: models come in families, small and fast against large and
deep. Triage, extraction and sorting, which is most of office work,
belong to the small fast ones, and every build in this course names
the model tier it uses for exactly that reason. Reaching for the
biggest model for a sorting job is paying for a surgeon to open the
post.


@slide bullets
kicker: Connecting it in n8n
## What the screen actually asks for

1. Add a Chat Model node under any AI Agent, and choose your provider from the list
2. Paste in an API key: a long string from your provider's own dashboard, not a password you invent
3. n8n never sees anything beyond that key. The conversation happens directly between n8n and the model

@narrate
Getting the key itself is the one step this course cannot walk you
through on screen, because it means logging into an account only you can
create. It takes about two minutes on Google's side, and once it is
pasted into n8n, you will not need to touch it again for the rest of this
course.

Two small habits while you are on the provider's dashboard, both
thirty second investments. Name the key for what it does, something
like n8n dash agents, because six months from now a list of unnamed
keys is a list of things you are afraid to delete, and a named key can
be revoked surgically the day a laptop goes missing. And make one key
per purpose rather than reusing one everywhere: if this key ever needs
cutting, you want to cut the agents, not everything else you built on
the same account. Neither habit costs anything today. Both are the
kind of thing Section 8 will make you glad you did.


@slide callout
kicker: One habit worth starting now
::: callout Treat every API key like a password
Anyone with that string can run charges on your account. Never paste one
into a slide, a screen share, or a message to a colleague. n8n stores it
encrypted once you save it as a credential, the saved login a node uses
to reach an outside account, and that is the only place it should live.
:::
