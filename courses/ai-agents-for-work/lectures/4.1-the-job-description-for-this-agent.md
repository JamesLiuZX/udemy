@slide quote
kicker: The scene this agent fixes
> I know we agreed on something in that call. I just can't remember what, or who owns it.

attrib: Every manager, the day after a call, at least once a month

@narrate
Priya's calendar carries four or five client calls a week. Every one of
them ends with a small handful of things somebody agreed to do, and
without fail, some of them evaporate by Friday. Not from carelessness.
From nobody writing them down in a form anyone re-reads.

Take her weekly Brightwell call as the running example, because this
whole section builds against it. Twenty five minutes with the account
holder, and somewhere in the middle, three commitments made in
passing: she will resend a certificate, he will confirm a figure with
his finance person, and someone, this is the dangerous one, will
"look into" an open question before renewal. Which of those three is
most likely to surface again only when it has become a problem? The
unowned one, every time. Meeting notes exist to catch exactly that
item, and the reason most people's notes fail is that writing them
well takes twenty minutes nobody has after a call. Delegating that
twenty minutes, without losing the accuracy, is this agent's whole
job.


@slide example
kicker: This agent's job description
::: example Filled from the C05 template
**The job**: turns a call transcript into a short list of action items,
who owns each one, and when it's due if that was said out loud.
**Scope**: reads one transcript at a time, pasted in. Touches nothing
else.
**Action**: read only, output only. No email, no calendar, no message
sent anywhere.
**Ceiling**: never invents an owner, an action or a date that was not
actually said.
**Trial task**: run it against three real transcripts, check every
extracted item against your own memory of the call.
:::

@narrate
Read that ceiling line again, because it is the one doing the real work
in this build. An agent that occasionally invents a due date is worse
than no agent at all, because a wrong list looks exactly as confident as
a correct one.

Why does this agent need that ceiling when Agent 1 did not? Because of
how the two can fail. A triage agent's mistakes are visible by
construction: a wrong flag sits in front of you, asking to be
disagreed with. An extraction agent's natural failure is the plausible
gap-fill: the call never named a date, the list would look tidier with
one, so a tidy, reasonable, invented "by Friday" appears, and nothing
about it looks wrong. The ceiling turns that into a bright line you
can check against the transcript: if it was not said out loud, it
does not go on the list, and a blank cell is the correct output, not
a failure to fix. In two lectures, that exact sentence goes into the
briefing, and in the trial, "check every item against your own memory
of the call" is checking precisely this line, not the formatting.

@slide table
kicker: More freedom, more failure modes
## How each agent can be wrong

| | Agent 1: classify | Agent 2: extract |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wrong call | Flagged noise, or missed a real one | An item missed, or one invented |
| Wrong detail | Not possible: yes or no | Right action, wrong owner or date |
| Blended | Not possible: one email, one verdict | Two people's commitments merged |
| How it looks | A flag you disagree with | A tidy list you believe |

@narrate
That is a real shift in the kind of judgement you are asking for, not
just a different data source. Section 4.3 is where you write a prompt
for extraction instead of classification, and the two read differently
on the page.

The shift matters because the two jobs have different amounts of room
to be wrong in. A yes or no question can fail in exactly two ways.
Pull three items out of a half hour transcript and there are many
more: an item missed, an item duplicated, the right action pinned on
the wrong person, two people's commitments merged into one. More
freedom, more failure modes, which is why this agent's checking is
different in kind. You do not skim its output for plausibility,
because inventions are plausible by nature. You check each line
against the call you actually sat in, while your memory of it is
still warm. That is also why the trial is three transcripts rather
than a calendar week: the unit of evidence here is a call, not a day.

## Three pieces, then proof

1. The trigger: a simple form, not an inbox connection
2. The extraction prompt: pulling action items out of a mess
3. The proof: testing it against a real transcript, then shipping it

@narrate
Notice what the list does not contain this time: no routing lecture,
because a list of action items does not fork, and no credential hunt,
because of a choice the next lecture explains and defends. Agent 2 is
the fastest of the four builds, a single sitting end to end, and it is
deliberately sequenced second: same method as Agent 1, smaller
mechanical load, so the method is what sticks. Next lecture starts
