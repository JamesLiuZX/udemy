@slide quote
kicker: The sentence Priya says out loud to Marcus
> The final sign-off on a policy binding is mine. It stays mine. That's not a limitation, that's the point.

attrib: Said out loud, because it's what makes the rest of the pitch credible

@narrate
Every technique in this course names its cost, and this is the honest
edge of what agents earn you: hours back on the work around a decision,
never the decision itself where it genuinely matters. Priya's final
sign-off on binding a commercial policy is exactly that kind of
decision, and she has never once considered automating it.

Notice where she says it: out loud, to her director, as part of the
pitch, not buried in a footnote. That placement is doing persuasion
work worth studying. A person who names what they will not automate
is instantly more believable about everything they will, because
they have demonstrated the one thing a manager cannot verify from a
slide: judgement about limits. And in her trade the example is not
rhetorical. Binding is the moment cover legally exists; an error at
that step commits the firm itself. Some doors should have a human
hand on them because of what is behind the door, not because of any
doubt about the agent, and naming those doors unprompted is what
separates an operator from an enthusiast in the eyes of everyone
whose sign-off you will ever need.


@slide table
kicker: Three categories, not a long list
## What genuinely belongs on a never-automate list

| Category | Why | Example from this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Irreversible commitments | No draft-only version exists for a signature | Binding a policy, Priya's own line |
| Judgement with real stakes and no clear rule | The job description can't specify what "right" means in advance | Whether an unusual claim is legitimate |
| Anything where being wrong costs more than the task was ever worth | The savings never justify the risk | A legal opinion, a disciplinary decision |

@narrate
Notice this is a short list, on purpose. The temptation after four
successful builds is to reach for a longer one out of caution. A list
so long it excludes half your job is not a safety measure, it is an
excuse not to get the hours back you actually could.

The middle category is the subtle one, so give it a second look. An
unusual claim is not hard because the stakes are high, it is hard
because "legitimate" cannot be specified in advance: each one turns
on context nobody could enumerate into a briefing. There is a handy
tell for this category, and you met it in Section 1.4: if you sit
down to write question five of the job description, what correct
means in this job, and find you genuinely cannot, you are not
failing at paperwork. You are holding a task that belongs on this
list, and the template just told you so before any build wasted a
weekend proving it.


@slide two-col
kicker: The test that sorts one from the other
## Ask this before you build, not after

::: cols
:: card bad
### Belongs on the list
"If this goes wrong, there is no draft, no review, no undo. It is just
wrong, out in the world."
:: card good
### Doesn't
"If this goes wrong, a human catches it before it costs anything real."
Every one of your four agents passes this test. That is not an
accident, it is the design.
:::

@narrate
Run every agent you have ever built, or ever plan to, through that
question. It is the same test, asked one more way, that Section 1's
blast radius and Section 6's draft-only design were both built to
answer.

Notice what the test is not about: importance. That is where most
never-automate lists go wrong, banning tasks for being important,
which is really banning them for mattering. Plenty of important work
automates beautifully because its failures are catchable: the weekly
report matters to Marcus, and a wrong draft costs a correction, so
it automates. And occasionally a trivial-seeming task belongs on the
list because its failure path has no undo. The sorting question is
always about the failure's shape, never the task's dignity, and once
you see that, your list writes itself in ten minutes.


@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## What to do now

1. Write your own never-automate list, three items or fewer
2. For each one, name the specific irreversible step that puts it there
3. Keep this list next to your job description template. Check new ideas against it before you build them

@narrate
Treat the list as a living document with a high bar for edits in
both directions. Removing an item should take a genuine change in
the world, a real undo appearing where none existed. Adding one
should take a named irreversible step, not a bad week's nerves. And
keep it visible, because its audience was never only you: the next
section's whole pitch stands on Priya being the kind of operator who
can show her director, on one page, both what she automated and what
she refused to. With the costs counted and the limits named, next
lecture closes this section. Section 10 is where all of this becomes
something you can actually bring to your own team.
