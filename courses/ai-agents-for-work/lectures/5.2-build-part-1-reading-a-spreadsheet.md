@slide two-col
kicker: A trigger with no event to wait for
## Two triggers, two different jobs

::: cols
:: card bad
### What you've built so far
Something happens, then the agent runs. An email arrives. A form is
submitted.
:: card good
### What this agent needs
Nothing has to happen. Monday morning arrives, and the agent runs anyway.
:::

@narrate
Search "Schedule Trigger" and add it to your canvas. Set the interval
to weekly, on Monday, at whatever time you actually want the report
waiting for you. This is the first trigger in this course that runs on
a clock instead of an event, and it is worth noticing how little
configuration that actually takes: one dropdown, one time, and you are
done.

One honest self hosting caveat before you pick the time, because the
clock only ticks while n8n is running. Schedule the report for eight
and open the laptop at nine, and Monday's run simply did not happen:
there is no little machine in the cloud owed to you, that was the
trade from Section 2.1. So pick a time that matches your real Monday,
half past nine if that is when the machine is reliably open, and the
schedule becomes dependable by design rather than by luck. The day
your team relies on this report existing before anyone logs on is the
day the hosted option earns its monthly fee. Until then, schedule
around your actual habits, not your ideal ones.


@slide table
kicker: A different kind of when
## The three triggers you now know

| Trigger | Fires when | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Email (IMAP) | A message arrives | Work that reacts to the world |
| Form | A person submits | Work a human hands over deliberately |
| Schedule | A time comes round | Work with a rhythm nobody should have to remember |

@narrate
Why does the trigger menu matter enough for a table? Because choosing
the wrong "when" is the commonest design error in beginner workflows,
and it is invisible on the canvas. A report on an email trigger runs
at the mercy of whoever emails; a triage agent on a schedule reads
mail in stale batches. Match the trigger to the shape of the work:
reactive, handed over, or rhythmic. You now hold all three, and every
workflow you ever sketch starts by picking one of these three rows.

## Google Sheets, reading a range

::: split
:: main
Add a Google Sheets node after the trigger, operation "Get Row(s)."
Point Document and Sheet at Priya's renewal pipeline. That's the whole
configuration: no formula, no script, just "read this range."
:: aside The credential you'd need for real
A Google account connection, the same kind of two-minute setup as
Section 3's email credential. This build was proven against pinned data
standing in for that connection.
:::

@narrate
Look at the pinned output on the right of that panel. Five rows, four
columns: Client, Premium, Renewal Date, Status. That is the entire shape
of data your agent has to reason about, and it is exactly the shape you
will point it at once you connect your own sheet.

Those column headers deserve more respect than headers usually get,
because they are now an interface. The rows arrive on the wire with
each value filed under its header's name, and the next lecture's
prompt will refer to Premium and Status by those exact words. Which
means the sheet's header row has quietly become part of your agent:
rename a column to tidy things up, and the agent goes on reading a
field that no longer exists. If the sheet belongs to more than one
person, that is worth a team agreement now, headers do not change
without telling whoever runs the report. File that thought carefully.
Section 7 is built around the day a shared sheet changes shape in a
subtler way than a rename.

@slide callout
kicker: One setting worth double checking
::: callout Get Row(s), not Append or Update
Google Sheets nodes can write back to a sheet just as easily as they can
read one. Your job description says read only, so make sure the
operation dropdown says "Get Row(s)" and nothing else. That one setting
is your entire ceiling for this node.
:::

@narrate
It is easy to skim past a dropdown when you are moving fast, and this is
the one dropdown in this build actually worth slowing down for. Get it
wrong, and read only stops being true no matter what your job
description says on paper.

The general principle is worth extracting, because it recurs in every
tool you will ever connect: capability and permission live in
different places. The node is capable of writing, always was, and no
setting removes that capability from the software. What the dropdown
holds is your refusal to use it, which makes it the piece of paper
where this agent's action level actually lives. So give it the same
treatment as any load bearing setting: glance at it whenever you edit
this workflow for any reason, because future you, adding a column or
tweaking the range, is one careless dropdown away from an agent that
writes to the very sheet it reports on.

kicker: Before you move on
## What you should have now

1. A Schedule Trigger set to run weekly
2. A Google Sheets node reading your renewal pipeline, four columns wide
3. Real pinned rows flowing through, including one renewal close to its date

@narrate
That third item is not decoration either. Your pinned test rows need
at least one renewal sitting close to its date, because the risk list
is the report's whole reason to exist, and test data where nothing is
at risk proves only that the agent can say "all fine", which is the
one sentence nobody ever checks. Give the test something to find. The pinned set this course uses does
exactly that: five clients, four open, and one of them close enough
to its renewal date that a good report has no choice but to say so.

Next lecture is where those five rows stop being a table and become
three sentences you would actually want to read on a Monday morning.
