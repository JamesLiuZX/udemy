@slide statement
kicker: A Monday in Priya's trial of Agent 3
## The weekly report said Brightwell's renewal was closed. It was not.

lead: Nobody had told the agent that. The sheet had, by accident.

@narrate
Three weeks into trialling the weekly report agent, Priya was doing
exactly what the C06 checklist asks: reading every output before
trusting any of it. That Monday's report said Brightwell Manufacturing's
renewal,
eighteen thousand four hundred dollars, was closed. Priya knew it was
not. She had spoken to Ravi Chandra herself two days earlier.

Picture the moment properly, because the ordinariness is the point.
Monday, coffee, the drafted report open next to the sheet, her own
hand count on a sticky note, the habit of saying each line out loud
before agreeing with it. Renewals open: matches. Premium at stake:
matches. Brightwell closed, and the word stopped halfway out of her
mouth, because she could still hear Thursday's call. Now run the
counterfactual that makes this section earn its place. Without the
trial, that report goes to Marcus, Marcus repeats the good news
upward, someone stops watching the one renewal on the book that
most needed watching, and the error surfaces in mid September, at
the worst possible moment, wearing everyone's confidence. The gap
between those two Mondays is not luck. It is a checklist.


@slide two-col
kicker: What actually happened
## A row moved. Nobody told the agent.

::: cols
:: card bad
### What the agent saw
Row six said Brightwell, closed. It read that row exactly as
instructed, and reported exactly what it found.
:: card good
### What actually happened
A colleague had inserted a new row above Brightwell's while updating an
unrelated client. Brightwell's own row was still open. It had just moved
to row seven.
:::

@narrate
The agent did not hallucinate a status. It did not misread a word. It
read the correct cell, at the wrong position, because a document editing
another client's information shifted every row below it by one, and the
agent had no way of knowing that happened.

The deeper distinction is worth one slow beat: position and identity
are different things, and a spreadsheet only really tracks the first.
To a human, "the Brightwell row" means wherever Brightwell's facts
happen to live today, because humans read by identity. To the sheet,
row six is just row six, and an insertion quietly re-addresses
everything below it. The colleague did nothing wrong: inserting a row
is what sheets are for. The agent did nothing wrong: it read what was
at the address it was pointed at. The failure lived entirely in the
space between two correct behaviours, which is why nobody's checklist
of mistakes would ever have contained it.


@slide callout
kicker: The failure mode, named precisely
::: callout An agent that reads a structure correctly can still fail when the structure moves under it
This is not a prompt problem. Section 5's system message was exactly
right: never mark closed unless the Status column says so. The Status
column did say so, for the wrong client, because a human edit moved
Brightwell's data without moving Brightwell's identity along with it.
:::

@narrate
Sit with that distinction, because it is the one most people miss when
they think about AI failures. Every instruction in this course's system
prompts was followed correctly. The failure lived in the data's shape,
not in the agent's judgement.

And it is a family, not a one-off. The renamed header from Section
5.2 is this failure. A colleague re-sorting the sheet to find their
own client is this failure. The pin left on a node from Section 2.3
is its cousin: in every case the world moved, the agent kept reading
the old map, and no prompt engineering on earth fixes it, because
the model can only judge what arrives, never what should have
arrived instead. File the family under one name, shape failures, and
remember its signature: every box green, every instruction obeyed,
and the answer wrong anyway.


@slide bullets
kicker: What caught it, and why that matters
## The catch, traced back

1. Priya was still on Trial, reading every single output, per Section 7.1
2. She knew the real answer, because she had spoken to the client two days before
3. The checklist's own question, "what would this cost unreviewed," had already kept her paying attention

@narrate
Nothing exotic caught this. No clever monitoring, no second AI checking
the first one. A person, reading every output during trial, who happened
to know the truth well enough to notice a lie that looked exactly like
data. That is what the trust ladder buys you, and it is why Section 7.1
insisted a trial has to be real.

Count what did not catch it, too, because the list is instructive.
The agent raised nothing: from where it sat, Monday was entirely
normal. n8n raised nothing: every box green, every run successful.
The prompt's own safeguard, never mark closed unless the Status
column says so, was satisfied to the letter. The only alarm in the
entire system was a human who independently knew the truth, and that
is the design lesson hiding in the story: delegation stays safe
where you still hold ground truth of your own. Priya talks to her
clients, so a wrong status has something to collide with. Hand an
agent a domain where you retain no independent knowledge at all, and
you have not delegated the work. You have delegated the noticing.


@slide statement
class: center
kicker: What changed after
## Priya added one line to the prompt: confirm the client name matches the row before trusting its status.

lead: A small fix. The trial, not the model, is what found it.

@narrate
Look at how small the fix is, and what kind of fix it is. Not a
smarter model, not a longer prompt: one redundancy, two fields that
must agree before either is believed. Cross-checks like that are the
cheapest defence in this whole course, and you can retrofit one onto
any agent in an afternoon. Be honest about what it buys, though. The
name-status check narrows this failure; it does not retire the
family. Some future edit will move the sheet in a way no one
predicted, which is exactly why the checking never goes to zero, and
why the rung this incident happened on, Trial, is where you want to
be standing when your version of it arrives.

That is this section's whole argument in one sentence. Next lecture
turns the discipline that caught this into a rubric you can run even
after an agent has earned enough trust that you are no longer reading
every single line.
