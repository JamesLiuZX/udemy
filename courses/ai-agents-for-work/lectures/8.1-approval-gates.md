@slide statement
kicker: A distinction worth being precise about
## Draft-only means nothing leaves the workflow without you. An approval gate means something can leave, but only after you say yes.

lead: You have only built the first kind so far. Both matter, for different jobs.

@narrate
Keep both definitions where you can quote them, because the rest of
this section leans on the difference, and because the confusion
between them is how "we have a human in the loop" ends up meaning
four different things in the same meeting.

@narrate
All four agents in this course stop at a draft, a flag, or a report
waiting to be read. That was the right choice for everything you built,
and Section 6 made the case for it directly. Not every useful agent can
stay that cautious forever, and this lecture is about the next rung up:
one that can act, but never without someone actually saying so first.

Why teach this now, straight after the trials? Because of what a
passed trial does to your appetite. A month of clean drafts makes
the read-and-click step start to feel like ceremony, and the thought
arrives on schedule: it has been right every time, why am I still
the bottleneck? That thought is not wrong, it is premature until it
has a design behind it. The honest path upward from draft-only is
not removing the human. It is shrinking your involvement to one
deliberate yes at the one moment that matters, and building that
yes so it cannot happen by reflex, so that the ninety seconds you
used to spend per output becomes five, without the one decision
that mattered ever leaving your hands. That is an approval gate, and
done properly it keeps most of the safety at a fraction of the
attention.


@slide two-col
kicker: Two designs, two different jobs
## Draft-only versus approval-gated

::: cols
:: card good
### Draft-only, what you built
Every output sits and waits. You decide when, or whether, anything
happens next. Nothing runs on its own.
:: card bad
### Approval-gated, the next step up
The agent proposes an action and pauses. One explicit yes, from a named
person, and it proceeds automatically from there.
:::

@narrate
The difference sounds small and is not. Draft-only puts the entire
decision in your hands, every time, no exceptions. An approval gate
still trusts a "yes" once and then the action runs unattended, which
means the gate itself has to be genuinely hard to click through by
accident.

Mechanically, a gate is one more node: the workflow pauses, sends
you a message describing what it wants to do, and waits. n8n has
wait-for-approval steps built for exactly this, and the details of
wiring one are in its current docs rather than this video, for the
usual freshness reason. What matters more than the wiring is what
the pause message contains. "Proceed?" is not a gate, it is a
formality with a button. A real gate shows the payload: the exact
email text, the recipient, the amount, whatever is about to become
irreversible, in the message you approve from. You are not
approving the agent's intention. You are approving the specific
thing it built, having actually seen it.


@slide callout
kicker: Where an approval gate actually belongs
::: callout Not every low-risk action needs a human. Every irreversible one does
A calendar hold that is easy to cancel might not need a gate. A payment,
a sent contract, a message a client cannot unsee, always does. The test
is not how likely the mistake is. It is how expensive it is if it
happens, and whether any amount of noticing afterwards can take it
back.
:::

@narrate
None of the four agents you built needed this yet, and that was
deliberate. The moment you add one that does, this is the standard to
hold it to: a gate a busy afternoon cannot click through without
noticing.

Run the reversibility test on a few real actions to calibrate it. A
label applied to an email: reversible in one click, no gate. A row
appended to your own tracking sheet: reversible, no gate. An email
sent to a client: irreversible the moment it leaves, gate. A payment
instruction: irreversible and expensive, gate, and probably a gate
plus a second person. Notice the test never asks how good the agent
is, and that is its strength. Gate placement decided by trust
evaporates as trust grows, which is exactly when the stakes are
rising. Gate placement decided by reversibility stands still no
matter how good the agent gets, because sent is still sent.


@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## What to do now

1. Picture the next agent you would want to build, one rung more capable than these four
2. Decide, honestly, whether its riskiest action is reversible or not
3. If it is not reversible, name the exact point where a human has to say yes, and what that yes will show you

@narrate
Keep your answer to step three in writing, with the job description
it belongs to, because a gate named in advance is a design and a
gate improvised later is a patch, and Section 6.1's house-framing
argument applies to gates exactly as it did to locks. Next lecture
applies the same caution to something that feels smaller: adding one
new capability to an agent you already trust.
