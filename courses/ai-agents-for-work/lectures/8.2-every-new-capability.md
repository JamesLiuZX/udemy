@slide example
kicker: The sentence that starts most near misses
::: example It's just one small thing, I don't need to redo the whole checklist for it
Everyone thinks this, right before the checklist would have caught
something. You will think it too, probably about something that feels
exactly this reasonable.
:::

@narrate
You already know the method: job description, scope, ceiling, trial.
You have used it four times, once per agent, and it held every time.
The trap is not forgetting the method. It is deciding, quietly, that a
small change you are about to make does not really count as needing it.

Why is the sentence so persuasive? Because it prices the risk by the
effort, and the effort really is small: one node, two minutes, a
credential you already have lying around. Humans reason like that
everywhere, and permissions do not. A two minute change can double
what an agent can reach, and the doubling arrives with none of the
ceremony that made the original build feel serious. No blank canvas,
no new workflow, no sense of beginning. Just a plus button on
something that already works, on an afternoon when the change would
help immediately. That is precisely the moment this lecture is for,
and you will recognise it when it comes, because it will feel like
the opposite of a risk.


@slide two-col
kicker: What actually counts as "a new job description"
## Small in effort is not the same as small in reach

::: cols
:: card bad
### Feels small
"I'm just adding one tool so it can check something first."
:: card good
### Actually is
A new piece of access, with its own scope, its own credential, and its
own way of going wrong, bolted onto an agent whose trust was earned by
the version before it.
:::

@narrate
An agent's trust was earned by the specific thing it was built to do.
Add a capability, and you have quietly built a slightly different agent
that inherits the trust of the old one without having earned it yet.

Make it concrete with the addition this section keeps circling. The
chaser you built writes drafts and reads one sheet: its whole risk
story is about words waiting for review. Give it a mail-search tool
and it has changed category, from an agent that writes into a folder
you control to an agent that reads from an account full of things
that are none of its business. Same canvas, same name, same trusted
history in the execution log, and a genuinely different risk shape.
Section 7.4 already told you a prompt edit resets part of the trust.
A capability edit resets more, because it does not change how the
agent judges. It changes what the agent can touch.


@slide table
kicker: What common additions actually change
## Small clicks, and what each one really adds

| The addition | What it feels like | What it actually changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A search tool | One helpful check | A new read scope: everything the credential can see |
| A send node | Finishing the job | A new action level: irreversible output |
| A second sheet | More context | A new drift surface other hands can move |
| Memory | A smarter agent | A new failure mode: last week bleeding into this week |

@narrate
Scan the middle column and notice it is always generous, because the
feeling comes from what the addition does for you. The right column
is what it does to your risk, and the two have no relationship at
all. That mismatch is the entire reason the worksheet in the next
lecture exists: not to slow you down, but to make the right column
visible while the middle one is doing the talking.


@slide bullets
kicker: The rule this section is building toward
## Treat every addition like day one

1. Write what the new piece can reach, the same way you did in Section 1.3
2. Check the credential's actual permission level, not what you assumed you granted
3. Re-run a short trial on the new piece specifically, not the whole agent again

@narrate
Three steps, and none of them take long. That is the entire point:
the method is not slow enough to justify skipping, even under deadline
pressure, even for something that looks perfectly safe on the surface.

Put honest times on them. Writing what the new piece can reach is
five minutes, and it is the five minutes in which "the mailbox"
becomes "the Renewals label", which is the whole game. Checking the
credential's real permission level is two minutes on a consent
screen you were going to click through anyway. And trialling the
new piece specifically means a week of glancing at what the addition
actually did on each run, what a search tool searched, what it
found, whether that matches what you meant, without re-trialling
the parts that already earned their trust. Perhaps fifteen minutes
of writing and a week of glances, against an addition that changed
what your agent can touch. Nobody's deadline is tight enough for
that trade to go the other way.
