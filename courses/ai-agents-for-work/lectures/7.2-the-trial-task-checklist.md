@narrate
Open the C06 template. It is short on purpose: a list you can actually
fill in during a coffee break, not a form that becomes its own chore and
gets abandoned by week two. Fill it in for the first agent you built,
Agent 1, before you move on, so the habit is real rather than
theoretical the first time you need it.

Why does this one need paper when the habit already exists in your
head? Because trials are rare events. You will run one this month and
perhaps not another for a quarter, and skills exercised quarterly do
not stay sharp in memory. Paper also travels: when Dev picks up
Priya's templates in Section 10, the checklist is what lets him run
his own trial properly without Priya standing over him. The videos
taught you. The artifact is what survives you, exactly as promised
back in Section 0.


@slide bullets
kicker: What the checklist actually asks
## Three sections, in order

1. Before you run it once: job description complete, test data realistic, you know the expected answer
2. Every run during Trial: read everything, compare against your own answer, log invented facts
3. Deciding to move off Trial: a set number of runs, every failure explained, a second pair of eyes

@narrate
Notice section three asks for a number, not a feeling. Fill it in before
you start, the same way a real experiment sets its sample size in
advance. Three to five runs is a reasonable default for a weekly agent,
more for anything that touches a client directly.

Each section is aimed at a specific way trials rot. Section one stops
the trial that starts before there is anything to measure against:
no expected answer written down means every output gets graded on
plausibility, which extraction taught you to distrust. Section two
stops the comfortable sample, the human instinct to check the outputs
that look interesting and wave through the ones that look fine. And
section three's second pair of eyes is smaller than it sounds: not an
approval board, just one colleague reading your trial log for ten
minutes, because the person who built the agent is the one person
guaranteed to read its results generously. Priya's second pair is
whoever the job description's sign off line names. Yours can be
anyone who did not build the thing.


@slide example
kicker: The one question that overrides everything else
::: example If this went wrong right now, unreviewed, what would it cost?
That question sits at the bottom of C06 for a reason. A clean checklist
does not override a bad answer to this question. If the honest cost is
high, the agent stays on Trial regardless of how many runs have passed.
:::

@narrate
This is the same instinct behind Agent 4's draft-only build in Section
6, applied to a moment in time instead of a piece of wiring. Both exist
because "it has worked so far" is never, on its own, a reason to stop
checking.

Watch the question produce different answers across your own four
agents, because that is what it is for. Triage gone wrong unreviewed
costs a flag you disagree with at tomorrow's coffee: low, which is
why one clean week can genuinely end that trial. The chaser gone
wrong unreviewed is words in front of a client wearing your name:
high, which is why its trial reads every single draft and why the
wiring beneath it never sends. Same question, honestly answered
twice, two different amounts of caution. The checklist is not there
to make you careful everywhere. It is there to spend your care where
the cost lives.
