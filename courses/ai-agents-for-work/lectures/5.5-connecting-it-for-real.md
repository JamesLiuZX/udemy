@slide statement
kicker: The last step, third time round
## Unpin the test data. Connect your real sheet. Let Monday's schedule do the rest.

lead: One new thing this time: nobody has to remember to run it.

@narrate
Open your Google Sheets node, unpin the test data, and connect your
real credential. Point Document and Sheet at your actual pipeline. The
schedule you set in Section 5.2 means this now runs itself, every
Monday, whether or not anyone thinks to trigger it. That is the whole
point of building it this way: the twenty minutes it saves you happens
whether or not you remember it exists.

Expect the real sheet to be scruffier than your pinned rows, because
real sheets always are: a stray empty row, a note typed under the
table, a client name with trailing spaces, a second tab someone added
last spring. None of that appeared in your test, which is exactly why
the first live run gets read with the same answer-key discipline as
the pinned one. Count your real pipeline by hand once, run the agent,
compare. Where the live sheet trips it, the fix is usually to tidy
the sheet rather than complicate the briefing: this agent earns its
keep for years by reading one clean table, and clean is cheap to
maintain once you know an agent is reading.


@slide table
kicker: A short checklist before you rely on it
## Agent 3's trial, at a glance

| | This agent |
| --- | --- |
| First run | Manual, watched, sheet open beside it |
| Trial length | Three Mondays, every number against your hand count |
| One boring check | The schedule's time zone is yours, not the server's |
| After the trial | You still read it first; one random line re-counted monthly |

@narrate
That third point is an easy, boring mistake with a real cost: a report
that lands at 2am your time because nobody checked the time zone setting
is a report nobody reads before their actual Monday meeting.

The first point has a principle inside it worth keeping for every
scheduled thing you ever build: never let a schedule's first firing
happen unobserved. The manual run is you watching the machine do the
whole job once, end to end, on live data, while you are standing
right there to read the result. After that, the clock takes over with
your blessing. Skip it, and the first real run happens alone on
Monday morning, and whatever it got wrong waits patiently in a draft
until someone notices, which on a bad week is Thursday. Watch the
first one land, on purpose, with the sheet open beside it, and every
Monday after that inherits the confidence of the one you saw.

## The same step, third time round

::: cols

### What doesn't travel with it
Your sheet credential and your model key. Whoever imports this connects

@narrate
By now this step should feel routine, and that is exactly the point.
Four different agents, one export habit, no new thing to learn. One
wrinkle specific to this build: the export names the columns your
briefing expects, so a colleague importing C03 onto their own sheet
inherits your header contract without knowing it. When you hand this
template to anyone, hand it with one sentence attached: your sheet
needs these four headers, or the briefing needs your words instead.
That sentence is the difference between a template that transplants
and one that mysteriously underperforms in its new home.

@narrate
And after the three Mondays? The checking does not end, it thins, the
way Section 1.2's ladder said it would. Priya's version at Review:
every Monday she still reads the report before anyone else does, and
one Monday in a few she re-counts one line of it against the sheet,
chosen at random. Five minutes a month, permanently, in exchange for
twenty minutes a week. That is the ratio this whole course is selling,
and it only stays honest if the five minutes actually happen. C03 is
also the template most worth re-aiming at other sheets in your life:
same build, different columns, as 5.1 said. When you adapt it, the
trial comes with it. New sheet, new three Mondays.

Three agents down, one to go. Notice how each has stepped further from
your hands: Agent 1 waited for the world, Agent 2 waited for you, this
one waits for nobody. The last agent takes the next step, towards
writing words a client might one day read, and that is exactly why it
is the most carefully constrained of the four. Section 6 explains.
