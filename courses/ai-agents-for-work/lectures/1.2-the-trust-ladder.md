@slide quote
kicker: The question you'll be asked
> How long until I can just let it run?

attrib: Every new user, within the first week

@narrate
Every person who builds their first agent asks some version of this
question within the first week. They want a date they can circle:
Friday, it runs on its own. The honest answer is that trust is not a
date, it is a ladder with three rungs, and you climb it by watching what
actually happens, not by waiting out a calendar.

Why does the date instinct feel so natural? Because that is how every
other tool you have adopted worked. You used the new spreadsheet
software for a week, nothing exploded, done: trusted forever. That
worked because the spreadsheet does the same thing every time. An agent
judges, and the only way to trust judgement is the way you trust a
person's: by keeping score. Two numbers, kept honestly, replace the
circled date. How many outputs have I actually checked? And how many
times did one surprise me? When the second number has been zero for
long enough to mean something, you climb. When it stops being zero, you
climb back down. That is the entire mechanism.


@slide bullets
kicker: The three rungs
## Trial, review, release

1. **Trial**: it runs, you check every single output, nothing goes out unseen
2. **Review**: it runs, you spot check a sample, most output goes out on its own
3. **Release**: it runs unsupervised, on a schedule of checks you set and keep

@narrate
Trial is the slow, deliberate rung. You are not testing whether the
agent is clever. You are testing whether you understand its failure
modes well enough to recognise one when it happens. Section 7 gives you
the actual checklist for running a trial properly.

Review is where most of the time saving shows up, because you are
sampling instead of reading everything. Release is the rung people
picture when they imagine "having an agent." It only works because of
the two rungs underneath it, not instead of them.

How long does each rung take? There is no course wide number, and that
is deliberate. Every agent Priya onboards gets a trial sized to two
things: how often the task runs, and how bad its worst plausible
mistake would be. A triage agent that runs forty times a day earns its
evidence quickly. A weekly report that runs once a Monday needs several
Mondays before you have seen anything at all. Later sections name the
trial each of her four agents actually got, and they are not the same
number. If you take one rule of thumb from this lecture, take this one:
count runs observed, not days elapsed. A quiet fortnight in which the
agent barely ran has taught you almost nothing, whatever the calendar
says.

@narrate
Look at the last bar. It does not touch zero, and that is not a
drafting error. Section 7's spot check habit is not a training-wheels
phase you eventually remove. It is the seatbelt. You wear it less
often once you trust the driver, but you do not take it out of the car.

Why can the checking never retire completely, when it does for the
spreadsheet? Because the world the agent reads keeps moving. Your
clients change tone, your company renames a product, a colleague
restructures the shared sheet, and an agent that was genuinely right
for three months meets input it has never seen. Nothing broke. The
ground moved. The middle bar has a practical rule inside it too: when
you sample one output in five, sample blind. Pick by rolling a die, not
by picking the outputs that look interesting, because the ones that
look fine at a glance are exactly where drift hides.


@slide callout
kicker: Where people actually get hurt
::: callout Skipping straight to release
The costly mistakes in this course all trace back to one shortcut: an
agent that never really finished trial, because a good first result got
mistaken for a passed test. Section 7's failure was caught at review,
precisely because review still existed. Skip it, and the same failure
meets a client instead of a spot check.
:::

@narrate
Notice the exact shape of that mistake, because it does not look like
carelessness from the inside. The agent's first result is good. Relief
does the rest: relief feels like evidence, and ten right calls in the
first week feel like proof. But ten right calls tell you about the ten
kinds of input you happened to get that week, not about the kind that
arrives at quarter end, or the day a colleague reorganises the sheet.
The fix costs one sentence, written before the trial starts: what I
must see before this runs without me. Priya's habit of saying her
checks out loud is doing exactly this job. Decide the bar while you are
calm, so a good Monday cannot quietly lower it for you.

Hold onto that. Next lecture answers the question underneath the ladder:
what, specifically, is an agent even allowed to touch while it climbs.
