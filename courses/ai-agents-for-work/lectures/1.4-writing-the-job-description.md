@slide statement
kicker: The document that ties this section together
## Three lectures, one page.

lead: Onboarding, the trust ladder, blast radius: they all live on this one document now.

@narrate
Everything in this section so far has been a habit or a principle.
This lecture turns it into a document, because a principle you cannot
point to is a principle you will skip under deadline pressure. The job
description is the artifact that makes the four onboarding habits from
1.1 impossible to shortcut, because you cannot fill it in without
answering them.

Be honest about what deadline pressure does to principles. In a calm
week you would never grant broad access; in the week the report is due
and the tool is nearly working, "connect everything and tidy it later"
feels reasonable, and later never comes. The document exists for that
week. It takes about half an hour the first time and closer to ten
minutes once you have done one, and it is the difference between your
judgement being a thing you exercised once, in writing, and a thing you
have to re-argue with yourself every time a shortcut looks tempting.


@slide bullets
kicker: Seven questions, in order
## What the template actually asks

1. The job, in one sentence, the way you'd describe it to another manager
2. What it can see: the exact folder, sheet or account, named specifically
3. What it can do: read only, draft only, or act
4. The ceiling: a number or condition it may never cross alone
5. What "urgent" or "correct" means in this specific job
6. The trial task, and what passing it looks like
7. Who signs off before it climbs a rung

@narrate
Notice question two asks for the exact folder, not "my email." Vague
scope is where blast radius quietly grows past what anyone intended.
Question four is the one people most often leave blank, and it is the
one that matters most: a ceiling only works if you wrote it down before
you needed it, not after.

Question one has a test built into it. If your one sentence needs the
word "and", you are describing two agents, and the course's advice is
always the same: build the first half, let the second half wait. "Flags
urgent email and drafts replies" is two jobs with two different risk
levels wearing one sentence. Question five is where you stop being a
form filler and start being the expert. Nobody else in your company can
write what urgent means on your desk. That definition, in plain
English, is the single highest value paragraph you will produce in this
entire course, because it is the paragraph the agent actually reasons
from every time it runs.


@slide example
kicker: Worked example
::: example Priya's first draft, for the inbox triage agent
**The job**: flags any email that needs a same-day reply, in one sentence
a manager would recognise.
**Scope**: reads the Renewals inbox only. Writes nothing.
**Action**: read only. No drafts yet, that comes with Agent 4.
**Ceiling**: never marks anything as resolved. Flagging only.
**Urgent means**: a client names a deadline, or mentions a payment,
refund or lapsed cover.
**Trial task**: one week, every flag reviewed each morning.
**Sign off**: Priya, before the trial task, then Marcus at Review.
:::

@narrate
Read that back and notice how ordinary it sounds. That is the point. A
good job description is not clever, it is specific enough that someone
else in your office could read it and know exactly what the agent is
and is not allowed to do, without asking you.

Three of Priya's answers are worth slowing down on. Read only, for a
triage agent, is not timidity: the job is to point, and a pointing
agent that cannot write anything has a blast radius close to zero,
which is exactly what you want from a first hire's first week. The
ceiling, never marks anything as resolved, is aimed at a specific
failure: an agent that closes a query wrongly makes work disappear,
and disappeared work is the silent kind of damage from lecture 1.3.
Flagging wrongly just costs a glance. And the sign off line introduces
Marcus Webb, Priya's director. He does not appear at the trial, because
the trial is Priya checking her own delegation. He appears at Review,
because that is the rung where the agent's output starts reaching work
other people depend on, and that is the moment a boss is entitled to a
say. Matching the sign off to the rung is what makes this a real
document instead of a form.

## The template is this section, folded into a page

| Question | Taught in | The failure it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The job, in one sentence | 1.1 | A wish too vague to delegate or check |
| Scope, named exactly | 1.3 | Access quietly wider than the job |
| Action level | 1.3 | An agent that acts when it should draft |
| The ceiling | 1.3 | A bad day scaling while you are in a meeting |
| What urgent means here | 1.1 | Your judgement never reaching the agent |
| The trial task | 1.2 | A good first week mistaken for proof |
| Who signs off | 1.2 | Trust climbing with nobody accountable |

@narrate
Nothing on this page is new, and that is its strength. If any row feels
unfamiliar, the middle column says which lecture to rewatch before you
fill it in. The right column is the honest reason each question earns
its place: every one of them is a specific, boring failure that has
already cost someone real money, folded down into one line you answer
in a minute.


@slide two-col
kicker: The one sentence test
## A job a manager would recognise

::: cols
:: card bad
### Too vague to delegate
- "Handle my email"
- "Keep the sheet up to date"
- "Chase people when needed"
:: card good
### Specific enough to check
- "Flag any email needing a same day reply"
- "Add one row per new renewal query, never edit existing rows"
- "Draft a chase for any renewal quiet for 5 working days"
:::

@narrate
Here is a test that works for every row on the left: could you tell, on
Friday, whether the job was done well? "Handle my email" cannot be
checked, so it cannot be trialled, so it can never earn trust. Each
version on the right can be marked right or wrong against reality, one
output at a time. That is not a writing style. It is the property that
makes the whole trust ladder possible, and when a trial feels
unrunnable, the fault is almost always here, in the sentence, not in
the agent.


@slide bullets
kicker: Before you move on
## What to do now

1. Open the C05 template and fill in all seven sections for the agent you imported in Section 0
2. Do not skip question four. If you cannot name a ceiling, that is worth noticing, not working around

@narrate
What if you genuinely cannot name a ceiling? Treat that as information.
It usually means the task involves a judgement you have not yet put
into words, and an agent is about to exercise that judgement without
you. Shrink the job until a ceiling becomes nameable: narrower folder,
draft instead of send, flag instead of file. If it still will not
shrink, you may be holding one of the tasks Section 9 says should stay
human, and finding that out now, on paper, costs nothing.

Keep this filled-in page open. Section 3 builds your first agent from
scratch against exactly this document, so the scope and ceiling you
just wrote are not theoretical. They are what you build to.
