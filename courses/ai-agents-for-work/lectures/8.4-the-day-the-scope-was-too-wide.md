@slide statement
kicker: A week after Section 6 shipped
## Priya wanted the chaser to stop pestering people who had already replied. That one improvement is what let it read something it never should have.

lead: Not a reckless change. A reasonable one, made without the worksheet.

@narrate
Picture yourself a week after finishing Section 6, pleased with an agent
that already works. That is exactly the moment this story happens in,
because it is exactly the moment you would be least on guard too. The
follow-up chaser worked exactly as built. Priya, being sensible,
noticed it would sometimes draft a chase for a client who had already
replied that morning. Her fix seemed obvious: give it a tool to search
her mail first and check.

Was the idea wrong? No, and that matters: Section 6.4 told you this
exact improvement was coming, and it is a good one. The redundant
drafts were a real annoyance, the fix was a known tool one plus
button away, and the whole change was two minutes on a Thursday
afternoon between other work. Every condition from lecture 8.2 was
present: small effort, immediate benefit, an agent with weeks of
earned trust, and no sense of beginning anything. So the worksheet
stayed closed. Not decided against, just never quite risen to the
level of a decision, which is how the worksheet is usually skipped.
Nobody chooses recklessness. They choose Thursday.


@slide two-col
kicker: What Priya meant to grant
## The gap between intention and access

::: cols
:: card good
### What she meant to allow
Search inside her Renewals label only, to check for a recent reply from
one specific client before drafting a chase.
:: card bad
### What she actually connected
A Gmail credential authorised for her whole mailbox, because that was
the default option on the consent screen and she did not stop to look
for a narrower one.
:::

@narrate
This is the exact gap Section 8.2 named: a small-feeling addition,
skipped past the worksheet, carrying access wider than the job it was
meant to do.

Reconstruct the consent-screen moment, because you have stood in it.
The tool needs a credential, the screen appears mid-flow, and it
offers what such screens offer: the standard grant, phrased
reasonably, with the narrower option a link or a dropdown away if
you go looking. Priya was not reading a contract that afternoon, she
was finishing a two minute change, and clicking agree on a screen is
the most practised reflex in modern office life. She connected her
own personal Gmail credential, authorised for everything, and the
workflow lit up green and worked. Note that last part. Nothing
about over-broad access fails. It succeeds, silently, holding more
keys than its job needs, and waits.


@slide statement
class: center
kicker: What happened next
## Chasing Brightwell's overdue signature, the search reached into a Legal-labelled thread about an unrelated dispute.

lead: The draft it produced referenced a line from that thread. Content it should never have been able to see.

@narrate
The agent did exactly what a search tool does: it looked across
everything the credential could reach, found a thread that happened to
mention Brightwell in passing, in an entirely different, confidential
context, and pulled a line from it into the draft it was writing.

Understand the mechanism, because nothing in it is exotic. A search
tool ranks by relevance, and relevance has no concept of propriety:
a Legal-labelled thread that mentions Brightwell is, to a search
index, simply a strong match for the query "Brightwell". The label
that told every human at Calloway and Finch "not for general eyes"
told the tool nothing, because labels only bind things that read
them. And the model, handed a search result by its own tool, treated
it the way it treats everything that arrives: as material. Recall
lecture 1.3's line, an agent's input is also its instructions, and
here is its gentler sibling: an agent's input is also its source
material, whatever that input turns out to be. Nothing
malfunctioned. Wide access plus honest machinery produced exactly
what it produces, which is the point of never granting wide access.


@slide callout
kicker: What actually stopped this from becoming a real problem
::: callout The chaser was still draft-only, exactly as built in Section 6
Nothing was sent. The draft sat in Priya's Gmail drafts folder, waiting
for the review her job description had always required. She opened it,
read one sentence that made no sense in context, and understood
immediately that something had gone wrong before it went anywhere.
:::

@narrate
Read that again: the tool's scope was wrong, and the agent still could
not cause harm, because the one guardrail that mattered most was never
the tool's permission level. It was the fact that nothing this agent
produces leaves without a human reading it first. Ask yourself, right
now, whether that same sentence would be true of every agent you have
built so far.

And run the counterfactual honestly, because in a compliance-heavy
business it is not small. Had the chaser been allowed to send, a
client contact would have received an email, under Priya's name,
quoting from a privileged thread about a dispute he had no part in.
At minimum, an excruciating apology and a question nobody wants
from a client: what else can your systems see about me? In an
industry that runs on confidentiality, plausibly worse. The
distance between that paragraph and "she deleted a draft" is two
words in a dropdown, chosen five weeks earlier, on a calm day,
before anyone was tempted.


@slide table
kicker: The whole incident, on one slide
## Trace it: each moment, and the guardrail on duty

| Moment | What happened | The guardrail on duty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thursday, 2 minutes | Search tool added, worksheet skipped | None: this is the gap |
| Consent screen | Whole-mailbox credential connected | Scope: failed here |
| The next chase run | Search reached a Legal thread | Labels: bind humans, not tools |
| Drafting | The stray line entered the draft | None: input is source material |
| The drafts folder | Nothing sent; the draft sat and waited | Action level: held |
| Priya's review | One senseless sentence spotted | The human read: held |

@narrate
Walk down the right hand column with your own agents in mind, and ask
where your equivalents are. The first four rows are where things went
wrong, and notice how quietly: no error, no red box, no alert you
could have subscribed to. The last two rows are the entire defence,
and you built both of them in Section 6 without knowing this story.
When you design your next agent, this table is the exercise worth
repeating on paper: walk the worst Thursday you can imagine, row by
row, and make sure at least two rows near the bottom hold without
help from the rows above.


@slide bullets
kicker: What Priya actually did about it
## The fix, in order

1. Deleted the draft immediately, and did not send anything it referenced
2. Removed the search tool from the workflow entirely
3. Re-added it only after scoping the credential to the Renewals label specifically
4. Ran C08 properly this time, before reconnecting anything

@narrate
Notice what she did not have to do: apologise to a client, explain a
leaked detail, or rebuild trust with anyone outside her own desk. That
is what "blast radius, not disaster" actually looks like when a
guardrail does its job, and it is exactly what you are protecting
yourself for, every time you follow this method instead of skipping it.

Notice also the shape of the repair: remove first, understand, then
re-add properly. She did not patch the live problem in place, she
took the capability out entirely, which cost her nothing but a few
redundant drafts for a week, and brought it back only behind a
label-scoped credential with C08 filled in. That is Section 6.4's
promise kept: the reply-checking tool, added properly, is a genuinely
good addition, and the chaser runs with it today. The idea was never
the mistake. The Thursday was.
