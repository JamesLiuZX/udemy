## The batch that lost one claim to pre-flight still had three more checks left to clear.
lead: Sam pulled up the same batch the claims check had just flagged, and ran the rights pass next.

::: split
:: main
Section 4.1 caught a licence tier gap, the specific paid-use grant a
licence actually covers, on an avatar. The same gap can hide behind a
face, a voice, a song, or a logo.
:: aside Same question, four places
Not four new rules. One question, asked of everything in the frame that
isn't your own product.
:::

@narrate
Pre-flight doesn't run as one big audit at the end. It's a pass, then
another pass, on the same batch, and rights comes right after claims.
Section 4.1 already taught Sam one version of this problem: an avatar's
licence tier quietly gating paid use. That was never the whole picture.
It was the first, most obvious piece of a question that applies to
everything in a frame that isn't your own product.

Four things in a typical ad can carry a rights problem: a face, a voice,
a piece of music, and a brand that isn't yours. You've handled the first
two already. This lecture gathers all four, and adds the two you
haven't.

Why gather them into one lecture instead of leaving each where it first
came up? A checklist scattered across three sections is one nobody
actually runs in full, and remembering under deadline pressure is
exactly the failure mode this section exists to prevent. One place,
four questions, run together, every time.

Notice, too, that all four share the same shape. Each one asks whether
something in your frame belongs to someone else, and whether you have
permission to use it. Once you see that pattern, you stop needing a
separate category for "avatar rights" versus "music rights" versus
"brand rights." It's one question, asked of four things.

@slide table
kicker: The four things to check
## Faces and voices you know. Music and brands are new.

| Element | The question | Where this lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A face or voice | Licensed avatar tier, or documented consent for a real person | Section 4.1, already covered |
| Music | Is this track licensed for commercial, paid social use specifically | New in this lecture |
| A third-party brand or logo | Does showing it imply an endorsement nobody agreed to | New in this lecture |
| An AI-generated "actor" | Same licence-tier question as an avatar, dressed differently | Section 4.1's logic, same check |

[SCREENSHOT-NEEDED: an ad platform's music library licence terms, the
specific line distinguishing personal from paid-social commercial use]

@narrate
Notice the last row isn't actually new. An AI-generated actor is the same
category as the avatars you already checked in Section 4.1, whatever the
tool calls it. The genuinely new material here is music and third-party
brands, because nothing so far has asked you to think about either one.

Music is the easiest to get wrong, because a track that's free to use
personally is often not automatically cleared for paid commercial
placement. Check the specific licence tier a platform's music library
grants for ads, the same discipline Section 4.1 already taught you for
avatars, applied to a completely different asset.

Ask yourself, honestly: have you ever actually read a music library's
licence terms all the way through, or did you assume "free" meant
cleared for everything? Most people assume. That assumption is exactly
where a rights gap on music actually starts, quietly, months before
anyone notices.

@slide code
kicker: How this looks on Harlan's own set
## Four checks, run on one finished batch

```text
FACE / VOICE   Sam's avatar, commercial tier confirmed (4.1)
MUSIC          Ad-library track, checked for paid-social clearance,
               not just personal or organic use
BRAND          Background scenes checked for any competitor
               logo the generator added without being asked
AI "ACTOR"     Same avatar check, whatever the tool calls it
```

@narrate
Here's what running all four actually looks like on a real batch, not
four separate audits scattered across different weeks.

Sam runs this as one pass, right before a batch ships. The face and
voice check is the one he already had, from Section 4.1. Music gets the
same treatment: not "is this free," but "is this specifically cleared
for paid social," which is a different, narrower question a lot of
free-to-use libraries don't actually answer. And every generated scene
gets one visual sweep for a brand that shouldn't be there, the same
scan Section 3.3 taught him to run for his own product, aimed outward
this time.

Total time for all four checks on a full batch: about ten minutes, once
it's a habit rather than something relearned each time. Compare that to
the cost of any single one going wrong, a takedown, a licensing dispute,
a legal letter from a brand whose logo showed up uninvited, and ten
minutes stops looking like overhead. It's some of the cheapest insurance
in this entire workflow.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap on brands

::: callout Be straight about this
Showing a competitor's product, or a recognisable logo, in generated
creative can read as an implied endorsement or comparison neither brand
agreed to. ==If a real brand appears in your generated frame, ask
whether it needed to be there at all==.
:::

@narrate
One trap worth naming plainly, because it's easy to walk into without
noticing.

A generator asked for "a person doing laundry" might return a shot with a
recognisable detergent bottle in frame, purely because that's a common
object in its training data, not because anyone chose it deliberately. If
that bottle is a real, identifiable brand, you've now got someone else's
trademark in your ad without their knowledge. Check generated scenes for
exactly this, the same way Section 3.3 taught you to check your own
product for accuracy, just aimed outward instead of inward.

Why does this feel easy to skip? Because your attention, checking a
generated scene, naturally goes to your own product first, the way
Section 3.3 trained you to look. A background brand sits outside that
focus entirely, in the part of the frame you're least likely to be
studying closely. That's exactly why it needs its own deliberate glance,
not a hope that you'd notice it in passing.
