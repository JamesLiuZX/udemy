## Sam wrote Harlan's 30-second founder script first, then cut it down to fifteen. It read rushed.
lead: A shorter script isn't a trimmed long one. It's a different structure, built for a different job.

@narrate
Sam had two scripts to write for Harlan: a fifteen-second hook and a
thirty-second founder explainer. He wrote the thirty first, since it had
more to say, then took a red pen to it to make the fifteen. What came out
read like exactly what it was: a rushed thirty-second script, not a real
fifteen-second one.

Video ad scripts fail for the same reason. A fifteen-second script and a
thirty-second script aren't the same script at two lengths. They're
different structures, built for different jobs, and the difference starts
with how many words actually fit.

How many words is that, exactly? Not a guess. An actual number, worked
out the same way the volume maths in Section 1.4 was: from the constraint,
not from a feeling about what sounds right.

Sam's mistake is the common one: write what you want to say, time it,
find it runs long, then cut individual words until it fits. That's
editing under pressure, and it shows: a hook that used to land cleanly
reads like it's missing a word. Write to the budget from the first
sentence instead, and you never perform surgery on a script that was
already trying to say too much.

@slide table
kicker: The actual word budget
## What fits, at a natural spoken pace

| Format | Time | Word budget | What it can hold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 15 seconds | 15s | About 35-40 words | One hook, one proof point, one CTA. No room for an objection |
| 30 seconds | 30s | About 75-85 words | Hook, proof, one objection handled, CTA |

@narrate
Here's the actual arithmetic, not a rule of thumb pulled from nowhere. At a
natural, unhurried spoken pace, fifteen seconds holds somewhere around
thirty-five to forty words. Thirty seconds roughly doubles that.

That's not a lot. Thirty-five words is barely two sentences of normal
speech. A fifteen-second script has room for exactly one hook, one proof
point, and one call to action, and nothing left over to handle an
objection. Thirty seconds buys you the room to answer one hesitation
before the CTA. Neither format has room for two.

This is the same discipline Section 2.3 taught for a single hook line,
just applied to a whole script instead of one sentence. Specificity earned
you a better hook there. A hard word budget earns you a script here that
doesn't try to say everything and ends up saying nothing clearly.

Notice, too, that this budget isn't pessimistic. Thirty-five words is
plenty for one sharp idea, delivered plainly. The budget only feels tight
if you're trying to fit a second idea in alongside it, which is exactly
the instinct this whole lecture is asking you to resist.

@slide code
kicker: The structure, applied
## Harlan's 15-second script, hook to CTA

```text
HOOK (0-4s)
"POV: it's hour eleven of your shift and your feet
finally aren't screaming."

PROOF (4-11s)
"15 to 20 mmHg graduated compression. Built for
people who don't get to sit down."

CTA (11-15s)
"Harlan. Link in bio."
```

@narrate
Here's that budget applied to a real script, using the raw testimonial
hook from Section 2.3's matrix, the same one that ended up winning in
Section zero.

Four seconds to hook, seven to prove it, four to close. Thirty-one words,
comfortably inside budget with room to spare. Notice what's not in
here: no objection handling, no second proof point, no aside. Fifteen
seconds doesn't have room for a second idea, so this script doesn't
pretend it does.

Notice, too, that this script doesn't try to close every doubt a viewer
might have. It closes the one doubt a fifteen-second cold-audience ad can
actually afford to address, and leaves the rest for a warmer audience and
a longer format to handle later.

Read the proof line again: fifteen to twenty mmHg graduated compression,
built for people who don't get to sit down. That's the exact spec from
Harlan's product truth doc, the one Section 2.1 built, dropped into the
script word for word rather than softened into something vaguer and
easier to say. A script this short has no room to paraphrase a claim and
still land it clearly.

@slide two-col
kicker: What thirty seconds buys you
## The one thing 15 seconds can't afford

::: cols
:: card bad
### 15 seconds
- Hook, one proof point, CTA
- Zero room for an objection
- Best for a cold, unaware audience
:: card good
### 30 seconds
- Hook, proof, one objection answered, CTA
- Room for "still not sure? here's why" once
- Better for solution or product-aware audiences
:::

@narrate
The extra fifteen seconds buys you exactly one thing: room to answer a
single hesitation before you ask for the click.

That makes the thirty-second format the better fit for an audience further
up the ladder from Section 2.2, someone who already half-believes the
category and has one specific doubt left. A cold, unaware audience doesn't
have a doubt yet to answer. They need the hook to work, full stop, which
is exactly what the fifteen-second format is built to do.

Picking the wrong format for the audience is a subtler mistake than it
sounds. Give a cold audience a thirty-second script with an objection
handled, and you've spent ten seconds answering a doubt they hadn't
formed yet, seconds a fifteen-second hook would have spent earning their
attention in the first place. Give a warm audience only fifteen seconds,
and you never get to the one thing actually standing between them and a
click. The format isn't a budget choice. It's an audience choice that
happens to come with a budget attached.

Sam's rule of thumb: cold prospecting traffic gets the fifteen-second
cut, and anyone already engaged, a previous ad watched, a site visit,
gets the thirty-second version. Same product truth doc, two scripts,
matched to how much convincing each audience still needs.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest check

::: callout Be straight about this
Word counts are an estimate, not a guarantee. ==Read every script aloud
with a timer before you generate a single frame==. A script that reads
long on paper reads long on camera too.
:::

@narrate
One honest check before any of this goes near a generator.

Word budgets are a useful estimate, not a promise. Delivery pace varies by
person and by avatar, and a script that looks right on paper can still run
long once it's actually spoken. Read every script aloud, with an actual
timer, before you generate anything. It takes fifteen seconds to find out,
which is a much cheaper fifteen seconds than a generated clip that needs
trimming after the fact.

Read it at the pace you actually want the avatar to speak, not rushed to
make the number work. If it runs long at a natural pace, the fix is
cutting a word, not asking the avatar to talk faster.

There's a second reason to read it aloud, beyond timing. A script that
scans fine on a page can still trip on a real mouth: a phrase that's easy
to write and awkward to say, a word choice that reads clearly but sounds
ambiguous out loud. You'll catch both of those by voice long before an
avatar generator does, because the generator has no opinion about whether
a line is comfortable to deliver. It just performs whatever you gave it,
clumsy phrasing included.

Before that, write your own. Pull an angle from the map you built in
Section 2.4, one you haven't scripted yet, and write it to a fifteen-second
budget: one hook, one proof point, one CTA, thirty-five to forty words,
read aloud with a timer before you call it done. That script is what
you'll actually generate on in the next lecture, and it's the first field
in the B04 brief the workshop asks you to fill out four lectures from now.

Notice how much of this lecture was really about subtraction. Every draft
you write from here on will want a second proof point, a longer CTA, one
more sentence of context. The word budget's whole job is to keep telling
you no, so the version that actually ships is the one clean idea a viewer
can hold in their head after fifteen seconds, not the crowded one you
started with.
