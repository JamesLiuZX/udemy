## B05: the pre-flight QC checklist

```text
FOR EVERY BATCH, BEFORE IT SHIPS:

1. CLAIMS. Owner: whoever owns the product truth doc.
   Could each claim survive being said to a compliance officer?
2. RIGHTS. Owner: whoever runs the ad account.
   Licensed avatar, licensed music, no uninvited brand in frame?
3. ACCURACY. Owner: whoever built the brief.
   Would the founder recognise this as the real product?
4. DISCLOSURE. Owner: whoever uploads the ad.
   AI label added, on every generated or AI-edited asset?
```

@narrate
Here's the whole checklist, four questions, four owners, run at the
checkpoints Section 6.5 already laid out: claims and rights before
generation, accuracy right after, disclosure at upload.

Save this exactly as it is. The value isn't in the wording, it's in
having one document that answers "did we check everything" without
anyone having to reconstruct five lectures from memory under deadline
pressure.

Notice, too, how short this actually is. Four lines, four owners, no
paragraph of explanation attached to any of them, because the
explanation already happened, properly, across five separate lectures.
A checklist that's short enough to read in thirty seconds is one people
actually read. A checklist that reads like a policy document is one
people learn to skip past.

Notice the shape of each question, too. All four are yes-or-no, the same
discipline Section 6.1 insisted on from the start. "Could each claim
survive being said out loud" has a real answer. A vaguer version, "does
this feel honest," doesn't, and a checklist full of vague questions gets
skipped exactly as fast as no checklist at all.

@narrate
Notice this isn't a hypothetical run. Two of the four checks actually
caught something real on Harlan's own account, the two you've already
seen in full in Sections 6.2 and 6.4. This checklist isn't a formality
that happens to pass every time. It's the thing that actually caught two
genuine problems before either one spent a dollar.

Notice, too, which checks passed cleanly and why. Accuracy passed
because Section 3's colour and text fixes were already applied before
this batch ever reached pre-flight. That's not luck. It's what happens
when the earlier sections' discipline is already built in: pre-flight
catches what's left over, not everything at once.

That's actually the healthiest way to think about this checklist's job.
It isn't the only line of defence in this whole course. It's the last
one, catching whatever slipped through every earlier check, brand
control in Section 3, licence tiers in Section 4, the claims discipline
you've been building since Section 2.3's hook taxonomy first taught you
to be specific. Four checks here, not forty, because forty would mean
the earlier sections weren't doing their job.

@slide callout
kicker: An honest note on the result

::: callout Be straight about this
A batch that passes all four checks on the first run is not the norm to
expect. ==Two of Harlan's four checks failed on the first pass. Catching
that is the checklist working, not the checklist failing==.
:::

@narrate
One honest expectation before you run this on your own batch.

Passing zero for four on the first try would be unusual. Failing one or
two, the way Harlan's batch did, is the normal result of a real check
actually doing its job. Measure success the same way Section 3.6 and 4.6
already taught you to: not a perfect first pass, but every real problem
caught here, before Section seven's test finds it the expensive way
instead.

Ask yourself, honestly, what a zero-for-four result on your own first
run would actually mean. Either your batch is genuinely cleaner than
Harlan's, which is possible, or the checklist isn't being run with real
scrutiny, which is far more common. If nothing ever fails, go back and
check whether you're actually testing each question against the batch,
or just glancing at it and moving on.
