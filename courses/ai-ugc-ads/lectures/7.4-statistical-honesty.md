@slide statement
kicker: Section 7 · Reading small numbers honestly
## Three conversions beating one conversion is not a result. It's two coin flips landing the same way.
lead: The kill rule from last lecture protects your budget. This lecture protects you from fooling yourself with what's left.

@narrate
Here's a genuinely uncomfortable fact this section has been building
toward. At the budget levels most testing actually runs at, twenty-five
dollars a day, a handful of conversions, the difference between what
looks like a winner and what looks like a loser is often just noise.
Random variation, not a real signal.

That's not a reason to give up on testing. It's a reason to read results
with the same honesty this course has asked of every claim, every
generated image, every avatar clip. A number that looks like a win can
still be lying to you, and the fix isn't more taste. It's knowing what a
small sample can and can't actually tell you.

This connects straight back to the course's opening argument. "Winners
are found, not picked" only holds if the finding itself is honest. A
winner declared from three lucky conversions isn't found. It's picked,
dressed up in a percentage sign to look more rigorous than a guess.

Notice how quietly that substitution happens. Nobody sets out to fool
themselves. It just feels like reading a dashboard, not like making a
judgement call, which is exactly why the habit of asking "is this
actually enough data" has to be deliberate. It won't happen on its own.

@slide table
kicker: What small numbers can and can't tell you
## The same three conversions, two different stories

| Ad set A | Ad set B | What you can honestly conclude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 conversions from 200 clicks | 1 conversion from 200 clicks | Almost nothing, yet. Both are small samples |
| 30 conversions from 2,000 clicks | 10 conversions from 2,000 clicks | A real, meaningful gap, worth trusting |

@narrate
Same ratio, roughly three to one, in both rows. Wildly different
confidence in the answer. Three conversions against one is the kind of
gap a single lucky click can produce by pure chance. Thirty against ten,
at ten times the volume, is a gap that chance alone struggles to explain.

This is the part that catches people who are otherwise doing everything
right. The instinct to call a winner the moment one ad set pulls ahead
is completely natural, and it's exactly the instinct this lecture is
asking you to slow down.

Why does volume fix what ratio alone can't? Because a single lucky
click, one person who happened to convert for reasons that had nothing
to do with your creative, can swing a three-conversion sample
dramatically. The same lucky click barely moves a thirty-conversion
sample at all. More data doesn't make your creative better. It makes any
one stray result matter less, which is exactly what you want from a
measurement.

Notice this cuts both ways, too. Small samples produce noisy results in
both directions, not just disappointing ones. A row-one gap tempts you
to declare a winner too early. A row-one loss tempts you to kill an
angle that might genuinely have worked. The discipline is identical
either way: wait for the volume the top row never had.

@slide code
kicker: The honest question to ask before declaring a winner
## Not "which number is bigger." "Could this gap be luck."

```text
BEFORE CALLING A WINNER, ASK:

1. How many conversions does the leading ad set actually have?
   Single digits: treat the lead as a hint, not a verdict.
2. Would swapping one or two results change the story?
   If yes, the result is fragile, not real.
3. Has this ad set cleared the kill rule from Section 7.2
   with room to spare, not just barely?
```

@narrate
Three honest questions, and notice none of them require a statistics
degree to answer. Single-digit conversions mean treat a lead as a hint.
If flipping the outcome of one or two individual conversions would
change which ad set looks like the winner, the result hasn't earned your
confidence yet, whatever the dashboard's percentage sign implies.

That third question matters because the kill rule and a real winner call
are different bars. Clearing the kill rule means an ad set isn't clearly
dead. It doesn't automatically mean it's the best one. Give genuine
contenders more spend before you crown one, not just enough to avoid
killing it.

Run this same check against the losing ad sets too, not just the
apparent leader. An ad set at zero conversions after a small amount of
spend is a genuinely different situation from one at zero after clearing
Section 7.2's full kill-rule spend. The first is still too early to
judge honestly. The second already has your answer.

Notice these three questions don't ask you to wait indefinitely, either.
Section 7.2's kill rule already sets a floor, and once you're well past
it with a genuine gap that survives the swap test, that's a real result,
not an excuse to keep testing forever out of caution. The goal is
honesty about timing, not paralysis.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
This course won't teach you a formula and pretend it makes small-spend
testing certain. ==No formula changes how much data a twenty-five dollar
budget can actually produce. Honesty about that limit is the only real
substitute for more spend==.
:::

@narrate
One honest thing to say plainly, because it would be easy to promise
otherwise.

There's no formula in this lecture that turns three conversions into a
trustworthy result, because no formula can. Statistical tools can tell
you how confident to be given the data you have. They can't manufacture
data you don't have. The actual discipline here isn't a calculation.
It's the willingness to say "I don't know yet" instead of forcing an
early result into a decision it can't support.

Why does this feel harder than it should? Because "I don't know yet" is
an uncomfortable thing to report to a boss, a client, or yourself, when
everyone wants an answer today. Saying it anyway, and letting a
promising ad set run for real volume before you commit to it, is the
actual professional move here, even though it feels like the less
confident one in the moment.

This is exactly the credibility argument the whole course has been
making about honesty since Section 1. Naming a limit plainly, rather
than papering over it with false precision, is the strongest signal you
can give that the rest of what you're saying can be trusted. A vendor
who tells you their tool always works is worth less than one who tells
you exactly where it doesn't.
