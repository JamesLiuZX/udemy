## Every result you'll ever read sorts into exactly three buckets. Most people only use two.
lead: Winner and loser feel like the whole answer. The third bucket, noise, is the one that actually protects you.

@narrate
Most people reading a test result reach for one of two conclusions:
this worked, or this didn't. That's an honest instinct, and it's
incomplete. A huge share of real results belong in a third bucket
neither of those options covers.

Think about why a binary read feels so natural. Everything up to this
point in the course rewarded a clean decision: kill or keep, ship or
don't. A results read is different. The most common honest answer isn't
a decision at all. It's a deferral, and deferrals don't feel like
progress, even when they're the correct call.

Noise means exactly what Section 7.4 already taught you to look for:
a result too small, too fragile, or too recent to actually mean
anything yet. Sorting into winner or loser when the honest answer is
noise isn't a harmless simplification. It's the single most common way
a testing programme quietly stops being honest.

Why does this happen so easily? Because "still noise" is an
unsatisfying thing to report, especially after real budget's already
gone into a test. Winner and loser both feel like progress. Noise feels
like nothing happened, even though correctly identifying noise is
exactly the discipline Section 7.4 spent a whole lecture building.

@slide table
kicker: The actual decision tree
## Three questions, in order, before you call anything

| Question | If yes | If no |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Has it cleared the kill rule with real margin, not barely? | Continue | Loser |
| Does it beat your CAC ceiling by a meaningful, swap-proof gap? | Winner | Continue |
| Is the sample still small enough that Section 7.4's swap test flips it? | Noise, keep running | Loser |

@narrate
Run these three questions in order, and notice the tree only produces a
winner through one specific path: real margin past the kill rule, a
genuine gap past the CAC ceiling, and a sample robust enough to survive
the swap test. Miss any one of those and the honest label is noise or
loser, not winner.

This is deliberately harder to reach than just "beat the ceiling once."
A single result that clears the CAC line on a tiny sample still routes
back to noise, because Section 7.4 already showed you how easily a small
sample produces a gap that isn't real.

Notice, too, that a loser can arrive two different ways on this tree,
and both are genuinely final. Failing the kill rule outright is the
obvious one. Passing the kill rule but never producing a swap-proof gap
past the ceiling is quieter, and easy to miss if you stop checking once
an ad set survives its first threshold.

Walk through what happens to an ad set that never quite clears row two,
week after week. It's not failing outright, so it never gets killed. It's
also never actually winning. Left unchecked, that ad set can sit in your
account indefinitely, spending steadily, contributing nothing, simply
because nobody ran the third question against it a second time.

@slide code
kicker: What actually happened on Harlan's account
## The "12-hour shift" creative, read against this tree

```text
GUT-ERA BASELINE (before the testing machine)
0.9% CTR, no structured test behind it, just taste.

FIRST STRUCTURED TEST, "12-HOUR SHIFT" CREATIVE
2.6% CTR. 19 dollars CAC, under the 24-dollar ceiling.
Cleared the kill rule with real margin. Survived the swap test.

VERDICT: Winner.
```

@narrate
Here's a real result, read against the tree instead of against a
feeling. Two point six percent click-through, nearly three times the
old gut-era baseline of zero point nine percent. Nineteen dollars CAC,
comfortably under the twenty-four-dollar ceiling, not just scraping
past it.

Notice what made this a genuine winner rather than a lucky early lead:
it cleared every question on the tree, not just the flattering one.
Against six other ad sets that were mostly noise, this is the one result
that earned its verdict instead of being handed one.

Would this still count as a winner if the CAC had landed at
twenty-three dollars instead of nineteen, barely under the ceiling
rather than comfortably under it? Worth asking yourself that question
now, because a margin that thin is exactly the kind Section 7.4's swap
test exists to interrogate before you trust it.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest discipline

::: callout Be straight about this
Most ad sets in a real test land in the noise bucket, not winner or
loser. ==That's not a disappointing result. It's the accurate one, and
pretending otherwise is exactly the mistake this whole section exists to
prevent==.
:::

@narrate
One honest expectation before you run this tree on your own results.

Out of Harlan's first batch of ad sets, most of them landed in noise or
loser, and only one earned a clean winner verdict. That ratio isn't a
sign the testing machine failed. It's what an honest read of a real
batch actually looks like. A tree that called every ad set a winner or a
loser would be lying to you far more often than this one does.

Hold that ratio against Section 1.4's volume maths, because it's the
same argument showing up again from a different angle. You generate a
real batch, not one hero ad, precisely because most individual angles
won't earn a winner verdict. The system was never designed to make every
angle succeed. It was designed to find the one that does, honestly,
among several that don't.

That reframing matters for morale as much as method. If you expect most
of a batch to win, a normal result feels like a string of failures. If
you expect most of a batch to land in noise or loser by design, that
same result feels like exactly what a real test looks like, because it
is.
