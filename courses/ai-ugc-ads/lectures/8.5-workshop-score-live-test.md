@narrate
Time to run the whole section on something real, not a worked example
from Harlan's account. Pull up one ad set from the testing architecture
you built in Section 7.5, or the closest thing you have to live spend
right now.

Work through it in the same order the section taught it. First, name
the objective and the metric that actually answers it, from Section
8.1. Then run Section 8.2's three questions in order: kill rule margin,
CAC ceiling gap, swap-test robustness. Only once that produces an
actual verdict do you move on to fatigue and the iterate call, and only
if the verdict was winner.

Resist the urge to skip to the verdict. The whole point of scoring it
step by step is that a wrong answer at step one, the wrong metric for
the objective, quietly produces a wrong verdict at step three, and you
would never see the mistake if you jumped straight to the end.

Pick the ad set you're least certain about first, not the one you
already suspect is winning. Why start there? The scorecard earns its
keep on exactly that kind of ad set, the one where your gut hasn't
already made up its mind. Scoring an obvious winner mostly confirms
what you knew. Scoring an ambiguous one is where this section pays for
itself.

## B08: the results scorecard

```text
FOR ONE AD SET, RIGHT NOW:

1. Objective and the metric that actually answers it (8.1)
2. Cleared kill rule with real margin? [yes / no]
3. Beats CAC ceiling by a swap-proof gap? [yes / no]
4. Verdict: winner / loser / noise (8.2)

IF WINNER, CHECK FATIGUE (8.3):
5. Frequency: [number], climbing past 3, or still low?
6. Fatigued and refreshed before? Which pattern (8.4)?
7. Next action: keep running / refresh / retire angle
```

@narrate
Seven lines, and notice the structure mirrors the section itself: the
first four lines are Section 8.2's tree, the last three only apply once
that tree actually produces a winner. A loser or a noise verdict stops
at line four. There's nothing to check for fatigue on an angle that
never won in the first place.

Fill in line one before you look at any number. Decide what you were
actually testing, then find the metric that answers it, the same
discipline Section 8.1 built. Writing the objective down first, before
the numbers are in front of you, is what keeps line four honest.

Lines five through seven only get filled in once, per ad set, at
whatever point you're actually reading it. Don't pre-fill a fatigue
guess before you check the real frequency number. That's the same
mistake Section 8.3 already warned against: reacting to a feeling
instead of the column that's sitting right there on the dashboard.

Keep one filled-in scorecard per ad set, dated, rather than overwriting
the same sheet every time you check in. A single snapshot only tells
you where an ad set stands today. A dated series of them is what
actually lets you read the pattern across refresh cycles that Section
8.4 asked you to watch for, weeks apart, not just this week's number in
isolation.

@slide table
kicker: How to actually do this
## Roughly fifteen minutes, one ad set at a time

| Step | Time | What "done" looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name the objective and metric | 3 min | Written down before you look at results |
| Run the three-question tree | 5 min | An honest verdict, including noise if that's what it is |
| Check frequency, if it's a winner | 2 min | A real number, not a guess |
| Decide the next action | 5 min | Keep running, refresh, or retire, with a reason |

@narrate
About fifteen minutes per ad set, most of it in the tree and the final
decision rather than the fatigue check, which is a single number once
you know where to look. Run this on every ad set currently live, not
just the one you suspect is winning. A quiet loser sitting unflagged in
your account costs you exactly as much as a genuine winner you failed
to notice.

If this is your first time running the tree on real numbers, expect it
to feel slower than reading a dashboard normally does. That's the
scorecard doing its job. A dashboard read in ten seconds skips straight
to the verdict your gut already wanted. Fifteen minutes per ad set is
what it costs to check that verdict against the actual data instead.

It gets faster the second and third time through, once the three
questions stop needing conscious effort and start feeling like the
obvious way to read a result. That's the actual goal here, not
speed for its own sake. A scorecard you have to think hard about every
single time is one you'll quietly start skipping under real deadline
pressure. One that's become a habit survives a busy week.

::: callout Be straight about this
Most ad sets you score this way will land on noise or loser, not
winner. ==That's not this workshop going badly. It's the same honest
ratio Section 8.2 already told you to expect from a real batch==.
:::

@narrate
One honest expectation before you call this finished.

If you score five ad sets and only one earns a clean winner verdict,
check your work against Section 8.2's tree again before you assume
something's wrong. That ratio is what an honest batch actually looks
like, not a sign your testing architecture is failing.

The scorecard is only doing its job if it sometimes tells you something
you didn't want to hear: an ad set you liked lands on loser, or one
you'd already mentally written off turns out to have cleared every
question. Either result is the scorecard working. A scorecard that only
ever confirms what you already believed isn't measuring anything, it's
just restating your taste with extra steps.

Notice, too, that a noise verdict is not a failure to reach a
conclusion. It is the conclusion, exactly as real as winner or loser,
and Section 8.2 already asked you to write it down plainly rather than
treat it as an unfinished result waiting for a clearer answer. Some ad
sets stay noise for weeks. Recording that honestly, week after week, is
still more useful than forcing a verdict the data hasn't actually earned.
