@slide statement
kicker: Section 9 · Who runs it
## The sprint doesn't care who runs it. It cares that every step has an owner, every single week.
lead: Solo, in-house, or agency, the same five steps from 9.1 have to happen. Only who does them changes.

@narrate
Section 9.1 ended with a real question left open: if nobody owns
Monday's scoring step specifically, it's the first thing that slips.
This lecture answers who "somebody" actually is, across the three ways
this work genuinely gets done.

None of the three is the correct one in the abstract. Sam runs Harlan's
account solo, fifteen hours a week, and the sprint still runs. A larger
brand might split it across a media buyer and a creative lead. An
agency might run it for several accounts at once, each with a different
client relationship on top. The five steps don't change. Who's
accountable for each one does.

What doesn't survive any of the three models is vagueness. "The team
handles it" isn't an owner. A specific name against Monday's scoring
step, every week, is.

Which model is actually yours right now? Not the one you're planning to
grow into eventually, the one you're genuinely running this week, with
the hours and people you actually have. This lecture is easiest to use
if you answer that honestly before the table below, rather than reading
it as three options to choose between in the abstract.

@slide table
kicker: Three models, one rhythm
## Same five steps, different hands

| Model | Who scores and decides | Who generates and pre-flights |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solo | You, time-boxed, same slot every week | Also you, which is why the time-box matters |
| In-house | A media buyer or growth lead, close to the numbers | A creative or marketing generalist, briefed from the scoring |
| Agency | Whoever owns the account, reporting up to the client | Often a separate creative pod, coordinated against the same calendar |

@narrate
Notice the solo row is the tightest constraint, not the loosest. One
person doing all five steps means nothing gets caught by a second set
of eyes, and it means the time-box from Section 9.1 matters more here
than anywhere else. Without it, scoring quietly eats into the hours
meant for generating, and the whole week slips a day at a time.

The in-house row splits scoring from generating across two different
skill sets, which is closer to how Harlan's setup could grow if the
account scales further. That split only works if the two people
actually talk on the same weekly cadence. A media buyer scoring in
isolation, handing results to a creative lead who checks in
irregularly, quietly breaks the order Section 9.1 insisted on.

The agency row carries a responsibility the other two don't: reporting
this whole rhythm to someone who isn't in the room for any of it. That's
exactly what Section 9.5 covers next, and it's worth knowing now that
whichever model you're running, someone eventually has to explain it to
someone else.

@slide code
kicker: Choosing your own model
## Three honest questions, not a scorecard

```text
1. Do you have the fifteen hours a week Sam spends on Harlan?
   No -> you need at least one more person somewhere in the loop.

2. Does anyone else need to see this work to approve budget?
   Yes -> you're closer to the agency row, reporting included.

3. Is the account small enough that splitting scoring from
   generating would just add a handoff with no real benefit?
   Yes -> solo or a tight in-house pair, not a full team.
```

@narrate
Three questions, and none of them has a universally right answer. The
right model is whichever one actually gets all five of Section 9.1's
steps done, honestly, every week, given the real hours and approvals
you're working with.

Notice question one is really about capacity, not skill. Sam could
plausibly run a larger account than Harlan's solo, if the hours existed.
They don't, which is exactly why fifteen hours a week and one account is
the honest ceiling on what his current model can carry.

Answer these for your own situation before the next lecture, which
assumes you already know roughly which row you're in. The swipe file
Section 9.4 builds works differently depending on whether one person
feeds it or several.

None of these three questions asks which model sounds more
professional, and that's deliberate. A solo operator running Section
9.1's rhythm honestly, every week, is doing better work than a three-
person team that's quietly skipping Monday's scoring step because
nobody's individually accountable for it. The model is a means to the
rhythm, not the achievement itself.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap

::: callout Be straight about this
Every model has its own way of quietly skipping a step under pressure.
==Solo skips scoring when the week gets busy. Agencies skip it when the
client isn't watching closely. Neither absence shows up until a winner
goes untracked for a month==.
:::

@narrate
One honest trap worth naming, because it looks different depending on
which row you're in, and it's easy to assume it's someone else's
problem.

Working solo, the busiest weeks are exactly when scoring gets skipped,
because there's no one else to notice it didn't happen. Working at an
agency, the same skip happens for a different reason: a client who
isn't reviewing the weekly numbers closely enough to notice a quiet
week of unscored ad sets. Both failures look identical from the
account's side. A winner nobody checked on for a month.

Whichever model you're running, build in one honest check: something
that would make a skipped week visible to you, not just something that
trusts you'll remember. A shared calendar reminder, a client report due
every Friday, anything that turns "I should score this" into "I have to
show someone I scored this."

Notice that the fix is the same shape across all three models, even
though the failure mode looks different. Visibility, to someone other
than the person doing the work, is what closes the gap. Working
entirely alone removes that safeguard by definition, which is exactly
why a solo operator has to manufacture it deliberately rather than
assume a colleague will eventually notice.

Before that, name the actual person, even if it's only you, against
each of Section 9.1's five steps. An unnamed owner is how a good rhythm
quietly turns into an occasional one.

Sam's setup is worth returning to here, because it's the least
glamorous of the three models and the one that's actually working.
Fifteen hours a week, one account, every step owned by the same person,
with nothing more sophisticated than a calendar block protecting
Monday's scoring step from getting eaten by whatever else that week
brings. It scales further than it looks like it should, precisely
because the rhythm itself, not the org chart around it, is what's
actually carrying the account.
