The fix isn't a better prompt. It's not asking the generator to do this

@narrate
Five of Harlan's twenty statics garbled the mmHg overlay. Section 3.1
already told you why: long text baked into a generated image is still
genuinely unreliable, no matter how good the tool.

So the fix isn't a cleverer prompt. It's removing the job from the
generator entirely for anything longer than a couple of words. If you
recognise this pattern from earlier in the course, you're right to: it's
the same move as Section 3.3's colour fix, narrowing exactly what you're
asking the generator to be responsible for.

It's worth being honest about why a better prompt genuinely doesn't solve
this. You could spend an hour rewording the instruction, trying "render
this text exactly" or "double-check the spelling before finishing," and
the failure rate barely moves, because the model isn't ignoring an
instruction. It's attempting the same hard task, glyph by glyph, every
time, and a longer sentence is just more chances for one of those glyphs
to land wrong.

@slide table
kicker: The workflow change
## Batch two versus batch three

| | Batch two: text baked in | Batch three: text added after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overlay method | Generator renders the spec line directly | Image generated clean, no text at all |
| Text step | Part of the image prompt | Added afterward, in a design tool, from the product truth doc |
| Result on 20 statics | 15 legible, 5 garbled | 20 legible |

@narrate
Here's the actual change, and notice it isn't a new capability. It's a
different division of labour.

Batch two asked the generator to do two jobs at once: make a convincing
photo and typeset an exact sentence inside it. Batch three asks it to do
one job, the photo, and hands the typesetting to a tool built for
typesetting. Twenty clean images, twenty exact overlays added afterward,
zero garbled lines, because nothing was ever asked to render text it
couldn't reliably place.

You'll notice this also makes the overlay itself more flexible, not just
more reliable. Once your copy lives in a design tool instead of baked into
pixels, changing a word doesn't mean regenerating the whole image. You edit
a text layer and export again, in seconds rather than another trip through
the generator.

That flexibility compounds across a real testing programme. If Section 6.2
ever flags a claim that needs rewording, or if Section 8's test surfaces a
line that should say something slightly different, a baked-in version means
regenerating the image from scratch and hoping the rest of it comes out the
same. A separated overlay means changing one line of text and shipping the
correction the same afternoon.

@slide two-col
kicker: When baked-in text is still fine
## A genuine exception, not a contradiction

::: cols
:: card good
### Safe to bake in
- A single word: "Comfort." A number: "12."
- Short enough that one glyph error is the whole risk
:: card bad
### Always add afterward
- The exact 15-20 mmHg spec line
- Anything quoting the product truth document word for word
:::

@narrate
This isn't a blanket rule against any text in a generated image. A single
word or a bare number is short enough that the risk stays low, and
sometimes baking it in genuinely reads more natural than an obvious
overlay, sitting inside the scene rather than sitting on top of it like a
sticker.

The line to hold is specific: anything quoting the product truth document
exactly, where a dropped word changes the claim, goes through the
generate-clean-then-overlay workflow every time, without exception. The
mmHg spec is exactly that kind of line. It's not persuasive copy you can
approximate. It's a fact that has to be exact.

Ask yourself one question per line of copy before you decide where it
belongs: if a single word dropped out silently, would anyone notice, and
would it matter? For "Comfort", probably not. For "15 to 20 mmHg", it
changes what you're claiming entirely.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest cost

::: callout Be straight about this
This workflow is a manual step, per image, that a fully automated batch
doesn't need. ==Twenty overlays by hand is real time==, and it's cheaper
than one garbled spec claim reaching a real customer.
:::

@narrate
I want to be honest about what this actually costs, because "generate
clean, add text after" sounds free and isn't quite.

Twenty images, each needing an overlay placed and checked, is real manual
time that a fully automated batch doesn't require. It's also
non-negotiable for anything quoting Harlan's exact spec, because the
alternative is a claim that reads as garbled at best and wrong at worst,
reaching a real customer on a real ad. Twenty minutes of overlay work is
cheap against that.

Build the overlay step into your actual time budget for a batch, the same
way you budgeted twenty minutes for the angle map back in Section 2.6.
Treating it as a rounding error is how a workshop that should take an
afternoon quietly loses its schedule.
