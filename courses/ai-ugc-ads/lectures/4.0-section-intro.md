## Sam had Harlan's first avatar ad ready to publish. Its licence didn't cover the launch.
lead: Rights first, generation second. That order is what this section is built around.

@narrate
Sam had Harlan's first avatar video open in Ads Manager, ready to
schedule. It looked finished. It wasn't allowed to run.

The avatar (an AI-generated on-screen presenter, standing in for an
actor) had come from a stock library's cheapest plan, and that plan
covered personal, organic posts, not paid campaigns. Nobody had checked
the licence tier, what the plan you're paying for actually permits you
to do with the footage, before generating the clip. Sam caught it before
a dollar of spend, which is the only reason it became a five-minute fix
instead of an account problem.

This section is built around not repeating that mistake. It's about UGC
video, short ads styled like an ordinary customer's own clip rather than
a polished commercial, built without a shoot. Every lecture ahead assumes
you check rights before you generate, never after.

## Rights first, then the actual production

1. The AI UGC landscape: avatars, licensed actors, and what rights actually means
2. Scripting for fifteen and thirty seconds: hook, proof, CTA
3. Avatar video generation, walked through properly
4. Product-in-video: what a generator replaces, and what still needs a phone
5. Assembly: cuts, captions, and designing for sound off

@narrate
Five lectures, and the order matters. Rights and the avatar landscape
come first, because generating first and checking rights after is exactly
how Harlan's first draft got made. Scripting comes next, then the actual
generation, properly licensed this time. The section closes on assembly:
turning a generated clip into something that works with the sound off,
which is how most people actually watch it.

Before lecture one, do the five-minute version of the check yourself.
Open whichever avatar or stock tool you already use and find its licence
or pricing page. That's the exact five minutes that would have caught
Harlan's first draft, and it's the cheapest insurance in this whole
section.
