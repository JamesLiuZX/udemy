Time to build the whole thing, for a real product, not Harlan's.

Pick something you actually have access to: your own store, a client's, or
one you're studying closely enough to write about honestly. Everything from
the last five lectures gets assembled here into one document you'll
actually use.

If nothing comes to mind immediately, don't default to a hypothetical.

as if you'd been hired to run their next test. The constraint of a real
product, with a real price and a real audience, is what makes this
exercise teach you anything at all.

## B01: the creative brief system

```text
1. PRODUCT TRUTH
   Price, margin, provable spec, claims boundary

2. AWARENESS LADDER
   Five rungs, what each one already believes

3. HOOK MATRIX
   Six patterns, matched to rungs

4. ANGLE MAP
   Twenty rows: angle, rung, core feeling interrupted

5. CONGRUENCE CHECKLIST
   Headline, hero image, offer, proof point, all matched
```

@narrate
This is the whole artifact, and you've already built every section of it
piece by piece. Today's job is assembly, for a product that isn't Harlan's.

Section one is the product truth document from lecture one: price, margin,
spec, and the claims boundary. Section two is the ladder. Section three is
the hook matrix. Section four is the angle map, twenty rows deep. Section
five is the congruence checklist you'll run before any test launches.

Notice there's nothing here about creative direction, tone, or which
generator to use. That's deliberate. This document exists entirely to
answer questions before generation starts. The moment you're deciding what
a prompt should sound like, you've left this document and moved on to
Section three, which is exactly where that decision belongs.

## The order that works, and roughly how long each part takes

| Step | Time | What "done" looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product truth doc | 15 min | Facts and a claims boundary, nothing else |
| Awareness ladder | 10 min | Five rungs, one honest sentence each |
| Hook matrix | 10 min | Patterns matched to rungs, not all six forced in |
| Angle map | 20 min | Twenty rows, checked against the reskin test |
| Congruence checklist | 5 min | Your actual landing page confirmed against it |

@narrate
Here's the order that actually works, and a rough time budget for each
step, because "write a brief" without a budget tends to expand to fill an
entire afternoon.

Product truth first, because everything else depends on it, and it should
take about fifteen minutes if you're not overthinking the claims boundary.
Ten minutes each for the ladder and the matrix. Twenty for the angle map,
because that's genuinely the hardest part and deserves the time. Five
minutes at the end to check your actual landing page against the
checklist from last lecture.

If you're well past the sixty-minute mark and still on the product truth
document, stop and move on anyway. An imperfect ladder built on a good
enough product truth doc beats a perfect product truth doc with nothing
built on top of it. You can always come back and sharpen the earlier
sections once the whole document exists.

So which section are you actually going to get stuck on? For most people
it's the angle map, because twenty genuinely distinct rows is a real
creative stretch. Knowing that in advance is usually enough to push through
it instead of quietly settling for twelve good rows and eight reskins.

## Two shortcuts that cost you later

::: cols
:: card bad
### The shortcut
- Skip the claims boundary, "we'll figure it out later"
- Write angles for the rung that's easiest to write for
:: card good
### Why it costs you
- Section six catches the claim anyway, later and more expensively
- Ten strong angles on one rung, ten weak ones everywhere else
:::

@narrate
Two shortcuts people take under time pressure, and both cost more than
they save.

Skipping the claims boundary because "we'll sort it out later" doesn't
avoid the work. It just moves it to Section six, where the pre-flight
check catches the same problem after you've already generated variants
around it. And writing mostly for whichever rung is easiest, usually
product-aware, feels productive and quietly leaves four fifths of your
funnel with nothing built for it at all.

Both shortcuts share the same shape: they trade fifteen honest minutes now
for a much more expensive correction later, once real money or real time
is already committed to the mistake. That trade almost never looks good in
hindsight, even though it always feels reasonable in the moment.
