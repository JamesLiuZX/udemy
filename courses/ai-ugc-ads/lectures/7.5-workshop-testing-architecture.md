@narrate
Time to build the actual instrument, on your own account, using the
batch that's already cleared every pre-flight check from Section six.

Pull up your angle map and your cleared creative set. Every angle that
survived pre-flight gets its own isolated ad set, the same structure
Section 7.1 argued for. No mixing, no shared ad sets, one variable
changing at a time.

Notice what you're not doing here. You're not deciding whether an angle
is any good, that question is Section eight's job, once real data
exists to answer it. You're only deciding how many ad sets you need and
what each one is allowed to cost you before you'd call it dead. Keep
those two questions separate, the same discipline Section 7.4 already
asked of you.

Ask yourself, before you build a single ad set: which of these angles
would genuinely surprise you if it lost? If none of them would, that's
worth noticing. A batch built entirely from angles you already feel
confident about isn't much of a test. The angles worth including are
the ones you're genuinely unsure of, because those are the only ones
this architecture can teach you anything new about.

## B07: the testing architecture and kill criteria worksheet

```text
FOR YOUR OWN ACCOUNT:

1. Contribution margin per order: [your number]
2. CAC ceiling: roughly 70% of that margin
3. Ad sets: one per cleared angle, same audience, same placement
4. Daily budget per ad set: [your number]
5. Kill rule: about two days' spend, zero of your key signal
```

@narrate
This is the whole worksheet, five lines, and every line traces back to a
specific lecture you've already sat through this section. Your CAC
ceiling comes from Section 1.5's maths, redone for your own margin, not
borrowed from Harlan's.

Fill in the two blanks honestly. If you don't know your own contribution
margin off the top of your head, stop here and work it out properly
before you fill in anything else. A testing architecture built on a
guessed margin is exactly the "precision borrowed from someone else's
numbers" trap Section 7.2 already warned you about.

Line five is worth a second look before you move on. "About two days'
spend" only means something once you've filled in your own daily
budget from line four. Do these in order, top to bottom, or a
worksheet like this quietly ends up with numbers that don't agree.

Notice, too, that line five doesn't hardcode zero add-to-carts the way
Harlan's did. Your key signal might be something else, a lead form, a
demo booking, whatever sits closest to a real purchase for what you
sell. Pick that signal deliberately, the reasoning Section 7.2 walked
through for why add-to-cart was right for Harlan specifically.

## The order that works, and roughly how long each part takes

| Step | Time | What "done" looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirm your contribution margin | 10 min | A real number, worked out, not estimated |
| Set the CAC ceiling and kill rule | 5 min | Both written down, from your own maths |
| Build one ad set per cleared angle | 20 min | Same audience, same placement, only creative changes |
| Set the daily budget per ad set | 5 min | A total you can actually sustain across every ad set combined |

@narrate
About forty minutes, and notice more of it goes to the margin
calculation than to actually configuring the ad platform. That's not
backwards. Getting the number right matters more than clicking through
the setup screens, because a wrong number makes every ad set built on
top of it wrong too.

If you're not ready to spend real money yet, build the structure anyway
and hold it ready. A testing architecture sitting finished and unused
costs you nothing. One built in a rush, the week you finally decide to
spend, costs you the exact mistakes this whole section tried to prevent.

Notice, too, that this table's time estimate assumes your margin
calculation is the one genuinely new piece of work. Everything else,
the ad sets, the budgets, the kill rule, is mostly transcription once
that first number is right. Rush the margin step to save ten minutes
and you risk redoing the other thirty.

Forty minutes is also a useful number to hold onto for a different
reason: it's roughly what this whole architecture costs you to build,
once, compared to the hours a badly structured account can waste over
a single week of confused, unreadable results. That trade is the entire
argument this section has been making since Section 7.1.

@slide callout
kicker: An honest note on the result

::: callout Be straight about this
Your numbers will not match Harlan's, and they shouldn't. ==A testing
architecture that fits your actual margin is the only kind worth
building. Copying someone else's numbers is the mistake, not having
different ones==.
:::

@narrate
One honest expectation before you call this finished.

If your CAC ceiling, your daily budget, and your kill rule all look
completely different from Harlan's, that's the workshop working
correctly, not a sign you did something wrong. Different margins produce
different numbers. What matters is that every one of yours traces back
to your own actual economics, the same way Harlan's traced back to hers.

If your numbers happen to land close to Harlan's, don't treat that as
confirmation you did it right, either. Check the actual maths again. A
coincidental match is still a coincidence, not evidence, and this
worksheet only means something if every figure on it can be traced back
to your own margin, not to a number that happened to look familiar.

The same honesty applies to the key signal you chose on line five. If
you picked add-to-cart purely because that's what Harlan's example
used, go back and ask whether it's actually the right signal for what
you're selling. A subscription product, a service booking, a
high-ticket item: each of those has a different behaviour that sits
closest to real intent, and copying Harlan's choice without checking is
the same shortcut this whole callout is warning against.
