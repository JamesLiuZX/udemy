@slide statement
kicker: Section 1 · The shift
## Most people think AI made ads free. It made variants cheap. That's a much smaller claim.
lead: And the gap between those two claims is where most of the money in this course gets made or lost.

@narrate
Here's a belief worth checking before you spend a dollar on any of this.

"AI made advertising free" is the version that gets repeated at conferences.
It's also wrong in a way that costs people real money, because it quietly
implies testing and judgement got free too. They didn't. Only one part of
the job did.

I want to spend this whole lecture on that gap, because almost every bad
decision in this space traces back to collapsing the two claims into one.
Someone hears "AI made ads free", believes their testing budget is free too,
and burns real money finding out the hard way that it isn't.

@slide two-col
kicker: The actual change
## What got cheap, and what stayed exactly as expensive

::: cols
:: card bad
### Still costs what it always did
- Media spend to actually run a test
- Judgement about what to test in the first place
- Catching a claim that could get an account banned
- A human deciding what "good" means for this brand
:: card good
### Got cheap, fast
- A single static or video variant
- Producing twenty angles instead of two
- Trying an idea nobody was confident enough to shoot
:::

@narrate
Look at what actually moved.

Generating one more variant of an ad used to mean another day of a shoot, or
another few hundred dollars to a freelancer. Now it's minutes and a few
dollars. That's real, and it's the reason this course exists.

But read the left column again. Media spend to run an honest test still
costs what it always did. A platform doesn't discount your budget because
the creative was AI-made. Deciding which twenty angles are worth trying
still takes judgement software doesn't have, because a generator will happily
produce twenty confident variations of a bad idea just as fast as twenty good
ones. And catching a claim that could get an ad account suspended still needs
a human who knows the rules, arguably more urgently now, because it's never
been easier to accidentally generate one.

None of that got cheaper. If anything, it got more important, because
there's more of it to check, in less time, before it reaches an audience.

@slide table
kicker: The concrete case
## What this means for one real account

| | A video shoot, 4 variants | 20 AI statics, one brief |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time | A location, a crew, a week of scheduling | An afternoon |
| Total cost | Several thousand dollars | A small fraction of that, total |
| Cost per variant | High, and fixed whether you use the footage or not | Small, and disposable if it doesn't test well |

@narrate
Here's what that looks like against a real account.

Remember Harlan's numbers from the last section: fifty-eight dollars a pack,
thirty-four dollars of margin before any ad spend. A proper video shoot
easily runs into the thousands, split across whatever variants it produces.
Amortise that over four ad variants and the cost per variant alone can eat
most of one order's entire margin, before the ad has proven it can sell a
single pack. Dana would have to sell dozens of packs just to earn back the
shoot, regardless of whether the ad ever works.

Twenty AI statics from one brief costs a small fraction of that shoot, total.
Not per variant. Total. That's not a marginal improvement. It's the
difference between an experiment Harlan can actually afford to run and one
they can't, and it changes the entire posture of the decision. A shoot forces
you to bet big on your best guess. A batch lets you stop betting on a guess
at all, and test several honestly instead.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade
class: center

::: callout Be straight about this
Cheap variants don't remove risk. They ==multiply how many bad decisions you
can make per afternoon== if nothing catches them first.
:::

@narrate
I want to be straight about the part that doesn't get said in the "AI made
ads free" version.

When a variant costs almost nothing to make, it's tempting to skip the
thinking that used to be forced on you by the expense. A three-thousand
dollar shoot makes you plan the brief carefully, because you only get one
take, and everyone in the room knows it. Twenty free-feeling statics tempt
you to skip that discipline entirely, because no single one of them feels
like it's risking anything.

But cheap and low-stakes are not the same property. Any one of those twenty
statics can still say something untrue about the compression spec, still
carry a claim Meta or TikTok will reject, or still be the one Dana's
compliance instinct would have caught if she'd looked properly. Cheap made
more of them. It didn't make any single one of them safe by default.

So what actually has to happen instead? The brief and the pre-flight check
get more important, not less, precisely because nothing expensive is
stopping a bad idea from reaching a real customer anymore. Sections 2 and 6
build exactly that.

@slide statement
kicker: The real shift
class: center
## The moat moved from making the ad to testing it.
lead: When everyone can generate, the account that tests fastest and most honestly wins.

@narrate
So here's the actual shift, stated precisely.

It used to be that having the budget to shoot good creative was the
advantage. Most of your competitors couldn't afford to try twenty angles, so
trying five was already ahead of the market. Production budget was the moat,
and it was a real one.

That advantage is mostly gone. Everyone can generate now, at roughly the same
cost you can. So if generation itself isn't the edge anymore, what is? What's
left as an edge is the machine around the generation: the brief that decides
what's worth making, the check that keeps a bad one from spending money, and
the test that reads the result honestly instead of guessing at it. That
machine is what the rest of this course builds, piece by piece, using
Harlan's account as the working example throughout.
