@slide callout
kicker: Section 7 · Budgets and kill rules

::: callout The worst time to decide when to kill an ad set
Is while you're watching it underperform. Every number in this lecture
gets decided now, before a single result exists to argue you out of it.
:::

@narrate
Here's the failure mode this lecture exists to prevent. An ad set is
running, the numbers are mediocre, not clearly dead, not clearly working,
and someone decides to give it one more day. Then another. That's not
patience. That's a decision being made by hope instead of by a rule, and
hope has never once correctly identified a losing ad.

You've actually seen this trap before, from a different angle. Section
5.5 warned you that a platform's own automation optimises whatever you
hand it without judging whether it deserved optimising. An
unwilling-to-kill instinct is the human version of that same blind spot:
no rule, no judgement about when to stop, just momentum.

The fix is deciding the numbers before the test starts, while you have no
emotional stake in any single ad set's result yet. Harlan's account runs
on exact figures. Yours should too, built from your own margin the same
way Section 1.5 built the twenty-four dollar ceiling from Harlan's.

Why does this need to happen before the data arrives, specifically?
Because a number chosen mid-test is never really a number. It's a mood.
Watch a mediocre result for three days and your sense of "good enough"
quietly drifts to match whatever you're already seeing.

## Decided before the test, not during it

| Decision | Harlan's number | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ad sets in the first test | 8 | The strongest angles from the angle map |
| Daily budget per ad set | 25 dollars | Enough spend to reach a real signal without overcommitting |
| CAC pass line | 24 dollars | Section 1.5's ceiling, 70% of the 34-dollar margin |
| Kill rule | 50 dollars spent, zero add-to-carts | A hard stop, not a feeling |

@narrate
Eight ad sets, twenty-five dollars a day each, run against a
twenty-four-dollar CAC ceiling that Section 1.5 already worked out from
Harlan's own margin. None of that is arbitrary. Every number traces back
to something you've already seen this course establish.

Notice, too, that eight isn't a round number chosen for tidiness. It's
however many of the strongest angles from the angle map genuinely
earned a slot in this first test, not padded up to a nicer-looking
figure and not trimmed down to save budget. The number of ad sets should
follow from the strength of your angles, not the other way round.

Notice, too, how the daily budget interacts with the ad-set count.
Twenty-five dollars a day across eight ad sets is two hundred dollars a
day total, a real number worth looking at honestly before you commit to
it. If that figure makes you flinch, that's useful information. It means
either the angle count needs trimming to the genuinely strongest few, or
the per-set budget needs to come down until the total feels like a
number you can actually sustain for the length of a real test.

@slide code
kicker: What the kill rule actually prevents
## The decision that doesn't get relitigated

```text
AD SET 3, DAY TWO
Spend so far: 50 dollars. Add-to-carts: zero.

WITHOUT A RULE
"Give it one more day, the algorithm's still learning."
Spend continues. Still zero. Repeat.

WITH THE RULE
Fifty dollars, zero add-to-carts: killed.
Budget moves to an ad set that's actually showing signal.
```

@narrate
Here's the exact moment a kill rule earns its keep. Without one, "give it
another day" sounds reasonable every single time you say it, because it
always feels premature to call something dead. With the rule already
decided, there's nothing to relitigate. Fifty dollars, zero add-to-carts,
finished, and that budget moves to an ad set that's actually telling you
something.

Notice why zero add-to-carts specifically, rather than a softer metric
like click-through rate. An add-to-cart is someone doing something, not
just seeing something, and it sits closer to the outcome you actually
care about than a click ever does. Fifty dollars with genuinely zero of
those isn't a slow start. It's an angle that isn't landing.

Notice the rule isn't harsh for its own sake. It's specifically calibrated
to Harlan's twenty-five-dollar daily budget: fifty dollars is two days'
spend, enough to see a genuine signal, not so much that a dead ad set
burns a week of budget before anyone notices.

Ask yourself what the equivalent number is for your own daily budget.
The ratio matters more than the specific figure: roughly two days'
spend before you call it, calibrated so a genuinely dead angle doesn't
quietly drain a week's worth of budget while everyone assumes it just
needs more time.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest adaptation

::: callout Be straight about this
Harlan's numbers come from Harlan's margin. ==Copying twenty-five dollars
and fifty dollars onto your own account without redoing Section 1.5's
maths is the same mistake as copying someone else's claim without
checking it==.
:::

@narrate
One honest limit before you copy any of this onto your own account.

These specific figures exist because of Harlan's specific contribution
margin. Your CAC ceiling comes from your own economics, the same
calculation Section 1.5 walked through, not from borrowing Harlan's
number because it's the one this course happened to use as an example.
Redo that maths for your own product before you set a single budget.

Notice the same warning applies to the daily budget and the ad-set
count, not just the CAC ceiling. Twenty-five dollars a day made sense
against Harlan's total daily test spend of two hundred dollars, itself
a number sized to what her business could actually sustain. Your version
of that figure depends entirely on your own cash flow, not on matching
someone else's comfort level with risk.

This isn't a small caveat to skip past. A CAC ceiling copied from a
product with a different margin structure is worse than no ceiling at
all, because it looks precise and rigorous while actually measuring
nothing about your own business. Precision borrowed from someone else's
numbers is just a confident-sounding guess.
