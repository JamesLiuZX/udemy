## A winning test is a snapshot. Nothing about it guarantees there's another one ready next week.
lead: Everything from Sections 2 through 8 becomes a repeating operation here, not a one-off project you did once.

@narrate
Here's what a single successful test doesn't tell you. It doesn't tell
you what happens when that winner eventually fatigues. It doesn't tell
you whether a fresh angle is ready to test next, or whether you'll be
starting from a blank angle map the week fatigue actually shows up.

Every skill this course has built so far works on one batch at a time:
brief it, generate it, pre-flight it, test it, read it. None of those
lectures said how often to do it again. That's this lecture's entire
job: turning a one-time project into a rhythm the account runs on,
whether or not this week happens to be a busy one.

Without that rhythm, Section 8.4's iterate-or-new-angle call becomes a
scramble instead of a decision. A refresh built calmly, in advance,
because the sprint scheduled it, is a different thing entirely from one
built in a rush the day a winner's numbers finally crack.

Harlan's account is a useful example of this gap closing. The angle
that won its first small test no longer runs on twenty-five dollars a
day in an isolated ad set. It's scaled into real budget, and the sprint
you're about to build keeps a refresh ready for it before fatigue
forces a scramble, not a system that only exists on paper until the day
it's actually needed.

@slide table
kicker: The weekly rhythm
## Same five days, every week

| Day | What happens | Which lecture it draws on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Score every live ad set from last week | Section 8.5's scorecard |
| Tuesday | Decide: keep running, refresh, or new angle | Section 8.4's pattern read |
| Wednesday | Generate whatever Tuesday's decisions require | Sections 3 and 4 |
| Thursday | Pre-flight the new batch | Section 6.6's checklist |
| Friday | Launch what cleared, isolated per Section 7.1 | Section 7's architecture |

@narrate
Notice the order. Scoring comes first, every week, before any new
creative gets made. Generating first and scoring later is exactly the
taste-picks-the-winner habit Section 0 broke, just moved a few months
downstream and dressed up as a schedule.

Thursday's pre-flight step isn't optional just because Wednesday's batch
came from a proven brief. Section 6's checklist exists precisely because
speed is when a claim slips through or a licence gap goes unnoticed, and
a weekly sprint moving fast is exactly the condition that checklist was
built for.

This rhythm doesn't require a full week's worth of new creative every
single week. Some weeks, Monday's scoring turns up nothing that needs a
refresh, and Wednesday through Friday are genuinely quiet. That's the
sprint working correctly, not a sign it's been skipped. The schedule
exists so you notice when something does need attention, not to
manufacture busywork on weeks when nothing does.

Why does the order matter this much, rather than just the presence of
all five steps somewhere in the week? Because each step depends on the
one before it having actually happened. Generating before scoring means
guessing at what's needed. Launching before pre-flight means shipping
whatever was made instead of what actually cleared. The order isn't a
style preference, it's what makes each step's output the correct input
for the next one.

## B11: the weekly creative sprint runbook

```text
EVERY WEEK, SAME ORDER:

1. Score every live ad set (8.5's scorecard)
2. For each winner: still healthy, or does 8.4's
   pattern say refresh or retire?
3. Generate only what Monday and Tuesday actually require
4. Pre-flight everything new before it launches (6.6)
5. Launch what cleared, isolated per ad set (7.1)
```

@narrate
Five steps, and every one of them already exists somewhere earlier in
this course. Nothing here is new work to learn. The runbook's entire
value is the order and the cadence, not any individual step.

Fill in your own weekday mapping if Monday through Friday doesn't match
how your week actually runs. What matters is that scoring always comes
first in the cycle and launching always comes last, whatever days you
attach to them.

Notice, too, that step three says "only what Monday and Tuesday
actually require." A sprint that generates a fixed quota of new creative
every week, whether or not last week's scoring called for it, is
optimising for busyness, not for what the account actually needs.

Print this, pin it, or keep it wherever you'd actually see it on a
Monday morning. A runbook that lives in a folder nobody opens is worth
exactly as much as no runbook at all. The five steps only do their job
if something makes you look at them at the start of every single week.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap

::: callout Be straight about this
A genuinely good week, no fatigue, nothing to refresh, feels like a
reason to skip the sprint. ==That's exactly the week the habit quietly
starts dying, and you won't notice until the week you actually needed
it==.
:::

@narrate
One honest trap worth naming, because it's the kind that only shows up
months later.

A quiet Monday, nothing scored, nothing to refresh, feels like nothing
happened, and it's tempting to skip the check-in entirely next week
since this week didn't need it. Do that a few times and the sprint stops
being a habit. It becomes something you do only when a problem's already
visible, which is exactly the reactive posture this whole section exists
to prevent.

Run the scoring step every week regardless, even on the weeks you're
fairly confident nothing's changed. A five-minute check that confirms
nothing needs doing costs you almost nothing. Losing the habit entirely,
and rebuilding it from scratch the week a winner actually goes tired,
costs you considerably more.

Ask yourself honestly, at the end of each quiet week: did you actually
run the check, or did you just feel confident enough to assume the
answer? Those are not the same thing, and only one of them is the habit
this lecture is asking you to keep.

Before that, sketch out your own five-day version, even roughly. The
exact days matter less than the order: score first, decide second,
generate only what's needed, check it, then launch. Get that order
right and the schedule underneath it can flex to fit your actual week.

Notice what this rhythm quietly assumes, too: that scoring, deciding,
and generating are each somebody's actual job on a given day, not a
vague intention floating over the whole week. If nobody owns Monday's
scoring step specifically, it's the first thing that slips when a
busier priority shows up. Section 9.3 picks up exactly that question
next, who runs which day, once the rhythm itself is settled.
