## A hook is not a clever sentence. It is a decision about what to interrupt.
lead: The best hook you've ever written fails instantly on the wrong rung of the ladder from last lecture.

@narrate
Every ad lives or dies in about half a second, and that half second has a
name: the hook. Most people think a hook is a writing problem. Write it
sharper, punchier, funnier. It's actually a targeting problem wearing a
writing problem's clothes.

A hook's job is to interrupt one specific thought someone was already
having. Get the thought wrong, and no amount of cleverness saves the line.
Get it right, and a plain sentence outperforms a brilliant one aimed at the
wrong person.

That's a genuinely uncomfortable idea if you've spent years being praised
for clever copy. It means the skill worth building isn't a better turn of
phrase. It's a better guess about what's already running through someone's
head the moment before your ad appears.

@slide table
kicker: Six named patterns
## The hook taxonomy

| Pattern | What it interrupts | Works best on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem-agitate | A feeling they haven't named yet | Unaware, problem-aware |
| Direct address | "This is about you, specifically" | Problem-aware |
| Pattern interrupt | Scroll-speed autopilot | Any rung, cold especially |
| Spec proof | Scepticism about whether it works | Solution-aware |
| Myth-bust | An assumption they already hold | Solution-aware |
| Objection answer | The one thing stopping a purchase | Product-aware |

@narrate
Here are six patterns worth knowing by name, because naming them is what
lets you brief a generator instead of hoping it stumbles onto one.

Problem-agitate names a feeling before it's been put into words. Direct
address says "you, specifically" instead of speaking to everyone. Pattern
interrupt breaks the visual rhythm of a feed regardless of what rung the
viewer's on. Spec proof answers "does this actually work" with a number.
Myth-bust takes something the audience already believes and turns it
around. Objection answer meets the one hesitation stopping a warm viewer
from buying.

Read the right-hand column again and notice pattern interrupt is the odd
one out: it's the only pattern that works regardless of rung, because it
isn't arguing anything yet. It's just earning the half second everything
else in this taxonomy needs in order to land at all.

The other five patterns don't transfer as cleanly across formats, and
that's worth flagging now. A spec-proof line reads naturally as text over a
static image. The same line, spoken flat by an avatar in a video, can sound
like a warning label. You'll see this again properly in Sections three and
four: the pattern is the same, but the format decides how it actually has
to be delivered.

@slide code
kicker: The artifact
## Harlan's hook matrix, awareness level by pattern

```text
UNAWARE          Problem-agitate
"Your feet don't have to feel like this by hour eight."

PROBLEM-AWARE    Direct address
"If you're on your feet 10+ hours a shift, read this."

SOLUTION-AWARE   Spec proof
"15 to 20 mmHg graduated compression. Not a guess. A spec."

SOLUTION-AWARE   Myth-bust
"Compression socks aren't just for flights. Ask anyone on a 12-hour shift."

PRODUCT-AWARE    Objection answer
"Still deciding? Here's what a nurse actually thought after week one."
```

@narrate
This is the actual artifact, and notice the shape of it: one pattern per
rung, not one pattern stretched across all five.

Each line traces straight back to the product truth document and the
ladder from last lecture. The spec proof line uses the exact number Dana
can defend. The objection-answer line assumes a real hesitation, not a
generic nudge to buy. Nothing here is clever for its own sake. Every line
is doing one specific job, for one specific person.

Notice, too, that solution-aware gets two rows instead of one. That's
deliberate, not padding. It's the widest rung, the one holding the most
people, so it earns two genuinely different bets rather than a single
guess stretched to cover a crowd that doesn't think as one person.

And notice what's missing entirely: most-aware, the top rung from last
lecture's ladder. That's not an oversight either. Most-aware audiences are
existing customers, reached through retargeting and email, not through
cold hook-driven creative, so this particular matrix doesn't need a row for
them. A different matrix, built for retention, would.

@slide two-col
kicker: What separates a real hook from noise
## Specific versus generic

::: cols
:: card bad
### Generic, feels like a hook
- "You deserve comfort"
- "The sock you've been waiting for"
- Technically true, aimed at no one
:: card good
### Specific, actually is one
- "By hour eight" names a real moment
- "15 to 20 mmHg" is a checkable number
- Aimed at exactly one rung of the ladder
:::

@narrate
Here's the tell that separates a real hook from something that only sounds
like one.

"You deserve comfort" is true of almost every product ever sold, which
means it's aimed at no one in particular. "By hour eight" names an actual
moment a real person on a real shift recognises instantly. Specificity is
the whole trick, and it's also the thing a generic prompt to a generator
will quietly strip out unless you ask for it directly.

Here's a fast test you can run on any hook before it ships: could this line
run for a completely different product with a five-word edit? If yes, it's
generic, no matter how good it sounds read aloud. "Hour eight" barely
survives that test for Harlan. It doesn't survive it at all for anything
else.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
This matrix is a starting hypothesis, not a verified fact. ==Only the test
in Section seven tells you which pattern actually earns its rung==. Treat
it as your best twenty-variant guess, not gospel.
:::

@narrate
One honest limit before you build angles on top of this.

Everything on this matrix is an informed guess about which pattern fits
which rung, built from the ladder and the product truth document. It isn't
verified yet, and it shouldn't be treated as settled until a real test in
Section seven says so. The matrix earns you a better starting batch. It
doesn't replace the test.

It's worth remembering exactly why that matters, because it's the same
lesson from Section one, showing up again in a new place: a well-reasoned
guess about which pattern will win is still a guess. It's a much better
guess than a blank page, and it is still not the answer.
