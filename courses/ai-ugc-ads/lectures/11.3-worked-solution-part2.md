Part one ended with a cleared batch sitting ready to spend against.
This part is where it actually earns a verdict, the same sequence your
own capstone's back half has to run: build the architecture, spend
against it, read the result honestly, and decide what happens next.

Section 7.2 built the actual structure: eight ad sets, one per cleared
angle, twenty-five dollars a day each. A twenty-four-dollar CAC ceiling,
worked from Dana's own margin. A kill rule at fifty dollars spent with
zero add-to-carts. Every one of those numbers was decided before a
single dollar of real spend went out, exactly the discipline Section
7.2 insisted on.

Notice the order this happened in. The numbers came first, written
down, before Sam looked at a single result. That's the opposite of
picking a CAC ceiling that happens to make an early result look good.
By the time real data existed, there was no room left to quietly move
the goalposts to fit whatever the account was doing that week.

A number decided in advance is a genuine commitment, checkable by
anyone who looks at it later. A number decided after results are
already visible is just a story dressed up as a rule, bent to whatever
answer already felt right.

Two hundred dollars a day, total, across all eight ad sets. That's the
real number this whole test cost to run, against a margin structure
that could absorb it. Notice this table has no line for "which angle
will win." That question genuinely couldn't be answered yet, and
Section 7's entire structure exists to make it answerable honestly
once real spend has actually happened.

Every one of these four numbers traces back to an earlier lecture, not
a guess made in the moment. The budget and CAC ceiling came from
Section 1.5's margin maths. The kill rule came from Section 7.2's
reasoning about what "no signal at all" should cost to confirm. Nothing
here was invented specifically for this worked example.

Compare this against your own capstone's numbers, if you've reached
this stage. Yours won't match Harlan's, and that's correct, not a
problem to fix. A different margin produces a different ceiling. What
should match is the discipline: worked from your own real figures,
written down before any result existed to tempt you into adjusting
them.

```text

0.9% CTR, no structured test behind it.

THE "12-HOUR SHIFT" CREATIVE, READ AGAINST THE TREE
2.6% CTR. 19 dollars CAC, under the 24-dollar ceiling.
Cleared the kill rule with real margin. Survived the

VERDICT: Winner.
```

@narrate
One winner, against seven ad sets that mostly landed on noise. That
ratio isn't a disappointing result, it's what Section 8.2 already told
you an honest batch looks like. Two point six percent click-through,
nearly three times the old gut-era baseline, and a CAC comfortably
under the ceiling rather than barely scraping past it.

Notice what earned this creative its verdict. Not one flattering
number in isolation, all three questions from Section 8.2's tree
answered honestly in sequence: real margin past the kill rule, a
genuine gap past the CAC ceiling, and survival through the swap test
that catches a result too fragile to trust yet.

The other seven ad sets don't get a dramatic callout here, and that's
deliberate too. Six or seven quiet noise verdicts sitting alongside one
winner is exactly the ratio Section 8.2 told you to expect, not a
sign the other angles were poorly chosen. Most of a real batch losing
cleanly to noise is the honest, ordinary outcome, not the exception.

Read without Section 8's tree behind it, two point six percent alone
might have looked convincing enough to call a winner on day one, before
the kill rule or the swap test ever got applied. The tree exists
precisely to slow that instinct down until a result has actually
earned it.

## Fatigue, a refresh, and a scale-up

```text
WEEKS LATER: FATIGUE
Frequency past 3.4. CTR down to 1.0%, near the old
baseline. Same creative, same audience, genuinely tired.

THE REFRESH
Same angle, new cut, sent back through testing. It
recovers, clears the winner tree again on its own merits.

THE SCALE-UP
The angle moves from 25 dollars a day to 400 dollars a
day. CAC settles at 21 dollars, still under the ceiling.
```

@narrate
This is Section 8.3 and 8.4's entire argument, compressed into one
account's real timeline. The same winning creative went tired, and the
fix wasn't the kill rule, it was a refresh: same angle, new cut, tested
again rather than trusted on faith.

That refresh recovering is what actually licensed the scale-up. Four
hundred dollars a day is a real core-campaign budget, and it's only
defensible because a twenty-one-dollar CAC sits underneath it, tested
and proven, not assumed from a feeling about which creative deserved
to keep running.

Notice this refresh followed Section 8.4's Pattern A, not Pattern B.
It recovered close to the original cut's performance rather than
fading fast or failing to recover at all. That's why the angle kept
scaling instead of getting retired. A different refresh, one that
fatigued again quickly, would have sent this same account back to
Section 2.4's angle map for something genuinely new instead.

Hold the whole arc together for a second: twenty-five dollars a day, a
handful of ad sets, mostly noise. One winner, recognised honestly.
Fatigue, caught by frequency rather than a feeling. A refresh, tested
again rather than trusted. Then, and only then, four hundred dollars a
day. Every step earned the next one. None of it was skipped to get
here faster.

@narrate
One honest note before you finish your own capstone.

The gap between "eight ad sets launched" and "one confirmed winner" in
this walkthrough is a few minutes of narration. On the real account it
was days of accumulating spend before Section 7.4's small-sample
honesty allowed any verdict at all, and weeks more before fatigue
showed up and the refresh had to prove itself again.

If your own capstone's test is still sitting in noise after a few days,
that's not a sign something's wrong. It's Section 7.4's discipline
doing its job, refusing to hand you a verdict the data hasn't actually
earned yet.

Would you rather have a fast answer or a true one? Every shortcut this
course has warned against, guessing a winner from a feeling, reading a
result before enough spend accumulated, trades a true answer for a
faster one. Harlan's account only reached a genuine scaled winner by
consistently choosing true over fast, at every single one of these
stages.

Notice, too, that patience here didn't mean passivity. Waiting for an
honest read isn't the same as doing nothing while it arrives. Section
9's weekly sprint kept running the whole time, scoring, checking
fatigue, keeping the swipe file stocked, so the account was never
simply idle while the data slowly became trustworthy.
