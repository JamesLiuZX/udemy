@narrate
Time to make three real video ads, not one polished demo you're proud of.

Pull up the angle map from Section 2.4 and the script structure from
Section 4.2. Pick three angles you haven't scripted yet, different enough
from each other that a test between them would actually tell you
something. Then run each one through the full pipeline this section
just taught you: script, avatar, product shot, assembly.

Why three specifically, and not five, or one done properly? Because five
is more than a first workshop needs, and one tells you nothing at all. You
already know from Section 1.4 that a single variant can't reveal a
winner. Three is the smallest batch that actually behaves like a test
instead of a guess.

This is also the point where video stops being separate lectures and
becomes one continuous piece of work, the way it will every week once
you're running this for real. Nobody scripts a single ad, waits, generates
an avatar clip in isolation, waits again, then assembles it days later.
You'll move through all five steps for each of your three angles in one
sitting, which is exactly the rhythm the actual job runs on.

## B04: the UGC / avatar video brief template

```text
FOR EACH OF YOUR THREE ANGLES:

1. Format: 15s or 30s, decided by word budget, not by feel
2. Script: hook, proof, CTA, timed aloud before generating anything
3. Avatar: licence tier confirmed commercial, delivery style matched
   to the hook's tone
4. Product shot: filmed once, reused across every variant that needs it
5. Assembly: captions exact, timed to the script, muted-watch test passed
```

@narrate
This is the actual template, and every line traces back to a lecture
you've already sat through this section.

The format decision is Section 4.2's word budget, applied before a single
word gets written. The script step is that same lecture's structure: hook,
proof, CTA, nothing else, checked aloud with a timer. The avatar step
pulls straight from Section 4.3: licence tier confirmed first, delivery
style chosen to match the hook rather than a generic upbeat default. The
product shot is Section 4.4's honest split, one real clip, filmed once,
reused. And assembly is last lecture's discipline: captions exact, timed
right, the whole thing tested muted before you call it done.

Fill this out for each of your three angles and you're not improvising a
video ad from scratch. You're answering five questions you already know
the right answers to, in order, the same way the static workshop turned
Section 3 into a checklist instead of a blank page.

Notice what this template deliberately leaves out: nothing here asks
whether the ad is good. That question belongs to Section 8. This
template only asks whether the ad is correct: licensed, accurately
captioned, built to a real word budget, watchable on mute. Get those five
questions right on all three, and you've done everything a workshop can
ask of you before a test.

## The order that works, and roughly how long each part takes

| Step | Time | What "done" looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Write and time three scripts | 25 min | Each one read aloud, inside its word budget |
| Confirm avatar licence tier | 5 min | Commercial use verified, not assumed |
| Generate three avatar clips | 15 min | Watched in full, not judged from a thumbnail |
| Film one product clip | 10 min | One real ten-second shot, reused across all three |
| Assemble and caption all three | 25 min | Captions exact, muted-watch test passed on each |

@narrate
About an hour and twenty minutes, total, and notice the product filming
step only happens once even though it feeds three separate ads. That's
Section 4.4's reuse argument, showing up here as real time saved instead
of a lecture point.

If one of your three avatar clips comes back flat on the first
generation, regenerate it rather than settling, exactly like Section 4.3
told you to. A flat delivery on a strong script is a worse ad than either
problem alone, and it costs you nothing but a few minutes to try again.

Notice, too, where the time actually goes. Scripting and assembly take
longer than generation itself, fifty minutes between them against twenty
for the two generation steps combined. The bottleneck in this workflow was
never the AI. It's the same checking and timing work Section 4.2 and 4.5
spent two lectures teaching you properly.

Treat that split as the honest shape of this job, not a workshop quirk.
Once you're running this weekly, generation will keep getting faster and
cheaper. The scripting and checking time won't shrink nearly as much,
because it's you doing it, not a model. Budget your own weeks around that
reality rather than around whatever a tool's marketing page promises
about speed.

## Two shortcuts, same shape as Section 3.6's, showing up again in video

::: cols
:: card bad
### The shortcut
- Skip the licence-tier check because the first draft looks fine
- Skip the muted-watch test because the cut sounds right with audio on
:: card good
### Why it costs you
- The exact gap that caught Harlan's first avatar draft, in Section 4.1
- An ad that quietly fails the one condition most viewers watch under
:::

@narrate
Both of these are the exact mistakes Harlan's first video pass already
made, and they're easy to repeat because neither one shows up as an error
on screen.

Skip the licence check because a draft looks convincing, and you get
Section 4.1's exact problem: a video that renders perfectly and still
isn't cleared to run as a paid ad. Skip the muted-watch test because it
sounds right with your own headphones on, and you ship an ad built for a
condition most of your real audience isn't watching under.

Neither mistake announces itself. A licence gap doesn't show up in the
clip, it shows up in a takedown notice. A sound-dependent cut doesn't fail
in your edit software, it fails silently in someone's feed, on mute, where
you'll never see it happen. That's exactly why both checks are steps in
the template rather than things to remember, so catching them doesn't
depend on you catching yourself.

Ask yourself, honestly, which of the two you're more likely to skip under
time pressure. Most people skip the muted-watch test, because it takes
thirty seconds and feels like the least important step on the list. It
isn't. It's the step that decides whether most of your audience ever
receives the ad you spent the other seventy minutes building.

@slide callout
kicker: An honest note on the result

::: callout Be straight about this
Three ads finished to a checklist beats one ad finished to a feeling.
==The point of this workshop isn't your favourite cut, it's a real spread
you can actually test==.
:::

@narrate
One honest expectation before you start cutting.

You will end this workshop with an opinion about which of your three ads
is best. Hold that opinion loosely. Section 0 already showed you, on
Harlan's own set, that a founder's favourite and the actual winner aren't
reliably the same clip. The point of finishing three isn't finding the one
you like. It's building a real spread, different hooks, different proof
points, different avatar deliveries, that a test in Section 7 can actually
tell apart.

Measure yourself against that standard when you finish: not "did I make a
great ad," but "did I make three ads different enough from each other that
losing two of them would actually mean something." If all three feel like
minor variations on the same idea, you haven't built a test yet. You've
built one ad three times.

If that happens, don't reshoot all three. Go back to the angle map from
Section 2.4 and swap out whichever one is doing the least work, the
weakest hook or the most similar proof point, and rebuild just that one.
The goal was never three perfect ads on the first pass. It was a spread
worth testing, and a spread with one weak link swapped out still counts.
