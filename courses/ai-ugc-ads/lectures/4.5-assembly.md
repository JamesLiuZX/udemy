kicker: Section 4 · The muted-scroll test
## Sam thumbed the volume to zero and watched Harlan's finished cut the way most of the audience actually would.
lead: If the ad only works with the sound on, it fails the one condition most viewers meet it under.

@narrate
Sam pulled up the assembled "hour eleven" cut on his phone, thumbed the
volume to zero, and scrolled past it the way a stranger on a break would.
Assembly, cutting the avatar clip, the product footage and the captions
into one finished ad, is what this lecture is about, and Sam's test is
the one it all has to pass: does the ad still work with no sound?

When did you last watch a short-form ad on mute, on purpose, to check?
Most people making these ads never have, which is exactly why so many of
them quietly fail the one condition most of their real audience is
watching under.

Short-form video defaults to silent, autoplaying, on a phone in someone's
hand in a public place. If your ad only works with audio, you've lost most
of your audience before the hook even lands. Design for silence first, and
sound becomes something extra your ad has, not something it depends on.

This isn't a platform quirk you can wait out. It's a fact about where
your ad gets watched: on a bus, in a meeting, next to a sleeping baby.
Nobody's turning the sound on to give your fifteen seconds a fair
hearing. They decide in the first second whether it's worth the tap, and
that decision has to survive on the picture alone.

## Harlan's "hour eleven" ad, assembled

| Time | Shot | Caption |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-4s | AVATAR, hook | Word for word, on screen |
| 4-9s | PRODUCT clip | Continues: the proof line |
| 9-15s | AVATAR, CTA | Plus on-screen "Harlan · link in bio" |

Captions run exact script text throughout. Not a summary. Not a paraphrase.

@narrate
Here's the assembly, and notice the captions run under every second of
it, not just the parts where the avatar's on screen.

That's deliberate. During the product clip, there's no mouth for your
viewer to read lips from, so the caption is the only thing carrying the
proof line at all if the sound's off. And you write the caption text
exact, word for word from the script, not a paraphrase, for the same
reason Section 3.4 wouldn't let you let a spec claim drift in an image
overlay. A caption is still a claim, just delivered differently.

Notice the third line too: "Harlan · link in bio" stays on screen as
on-screen text, separate from the spoken caption track. That's not
decoration. It's the one instruction a muted viewer actually needs to
act, and it has to survive being read at a glance, not studied.

Timing matters here as much as wording. A caption that appears half a
second after the word is spoken reads as sloppy, and a caption that
outruns the audio reads as a mismatch even when nobody can hear the gap.
Cut your captions to the same timing marks you built the script against
in Section 4.2, not to a rough guess at where a sentence probably ends.

@slide two-col
kicker: What "designed for silence" actually means
## Not just adding captions after the fact

::: cols
:: card bad
### Sound-dependent
- The hook only lands as spoken audio
- Product proof only stated aloud, never on screen
- CTA relies on a voiceover nobody's listening to
:: card good
### Silence-proof
- The hook's key words appear as text, on screen, in the first second
- The proof line is captioned even over b-roll with no face
- CTA text stays on screen long enough to actually read
:::

@narrate
There's a real difference between adding captions to a sound-dependent ad
and actually designing for silence, and it shows up fastest in the first
second.

A silence-proof hook puts its key words on your screen immediately, not
trailing half a second behind the spoken line. A silence-proof CTA holds
its text on screen long enough for someone to actually read it, not just
long enough to flash by under a voice track. Here's the test you can run
on your own cut, every time: watch the whole ad muted. If it still makes
sense, you've actually designed it for how most people are going to see
it. If you find yourself narrating along in your head to fill a gap,
you haven't.

Run that test on Harlan's own cut and it holds up. "Hour eleven" appears
as caption text at the exact second it's spoken, and "Harlan · link in
bio" sits on screen for the full four seconds of the CTA, not just a
flash at the end.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest check

::: callout Be straight about this
A caption is still a claim. ==Proofread it against the product truth
document exactly the way you'd check an image overlay==. A typo in a
caption is the same risk as a typo baked into a static.
:::

@narrate
One honest check before this ships anywhere.

It's easy to treat captions as an afterthought, auto-generated and barely
glanced at, because they feel like a formatting detail rather than
content. They aren't. If you let a caption misquote the mmHg spec, that's
exactly the same risk as the garbled overlay from Section 3.2, just
delivered through a different part of the frame. Give it the same care
you'd give any other line a customer might screenshot.

Auto-generated captions make this easier to get wrong, not harder. A
speech-to-text pass can mishear a number, drop a unit, or smooth an exact
phrase into something that sounds similar and isn't the same claim. Read
every generated caption against the script it came from, line by line,
before you trust it. That's a two-minute check against a mistake that,
once it's running as a paid ad, is a great deal more expensive to catch.

@narrate
One thing worth holding onto before you leave this lecture: there's a real
gap between an ad that technically has captions and one that was actually
designed for silence. Plenty of ads that fail the muted-watch test still
have captions. They just got bolted on after a cut built for sound,
instead of built into the cut from the start. Design silence-first, and
captioning stops being an extra pass at the end and becomes part of the
same decision as the shot list itself.
