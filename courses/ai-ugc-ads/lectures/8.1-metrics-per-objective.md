@slide statement
kicker: Section 8 · The right metric
## A great CTR on the wrong objective is not a good result. It's an answer to a question you weren't asking.
lead: Before you read any number, know exactly what you were actually testing for.

@narrate
Here's a mistake that looks like careful analysis and isn't. Someone
runs a cold-audience awareness test, gets a strong click-through rate,
and calls it a win. The click-through rate really was strong. It just
wasn't the thing that test was supposed to prove.

Every metric answers a specific question. Read the wrong metric and
you'll get a confident, precise, completely irrelevant answer. The fix
isn't a better dashboard. It's knowing which number actually maps to
what you were testing before you look at any of them.

Why does this trap catch experienced people as often as beginners?
Because every number on a results page looks equally official. A
dashboard doesn't label one metric "relevant to your actual question"
and another "impressive but beside the point." That judgement has to
come from you, decided before the numbers arrive, the same discipline
Section 7.2 already asked of budgets and kill rules.

@slide table
kicker: Match the metric to the objective
## Different questions need different answers

| Objective | The real question | The metric that answers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cold awareness | Does the hook actually stop the scroll | Thumb-stop rate, hook retention |
| Consideration | Does the offer earn a real look | Click-through rate, landing page engagement |
| Conversion | Does the whole funnel actually sell | Cost per purchase, against your CAC ceiling |

@narrate
Notice each row asks a genuinely different question, not a smaller
version of the same one. A cold-awareness ad earning strong click-through
but weak hook retention is telling you something real: people who saw it
stopped scrolling for the wrong reason, and it's not converting attention
into anything durable.

This maps directly onto Section 2.2's awareness ladder. A cold audience
and a warm one aren't just different targeting settings. They need
different proof, and reading them against the same metric hides that
difference instead of revealing it.

Notice, too, that the three rows aren't ranked by importance. Cost per
purchase isn't a "better" metric than hook retention, it's the right
one for a different question. An awareness campaign judged on cost per
purchase looks like a failure by a standard it was never trying to meet,
and a conversion campaign judged on hook retention looks impressive
while quietly losing money.

If you're only running one type of campaign right now, conversion,
most likely, it's still worth knowing the other two rows exist. The
moment you add a genuinely cold prospecting push or a warm retargeting
layer, you'll need a different metric on standby, not a scramble to work
out which number actually matters once that campaign is already live.

@slide code
kicker: How this looks on Harlan's account
## Same ad, read against the right question

```text
"HOUR ELEVEN" AD, COLD AUDIENCE
Objective: awareness. Question: does the hook land.
Metric that matters: hook retention past the first 3 seconds.

SAME AD, RETARGETING WARMER AUDIENCE
Objective: conversion. Question: does it actually sell.
Metric that matters: cost per purchase, against the 24-dollar ceiling.
```

@narrate
Same creative, two completely different reads, because it's doing two
different jobs depending on who's watching it. Judging the cold-audience
placement on cost per purchase is unfair to an ad that was never trying
to close a sale in the first four seconds. Judging the retargeting
placement on hook retention misses the actual question, whether it
converts.

Notice what stayed identical across both rows: the actual video file.
Nothing about the creative itself changed between the cold placement and
the retargeting one. Only the audience, the objective, and the metric
that judges it changed. That's worth sitting with, because it means the
same asset can be a genuine success in one context and simply irrelevant
in another, without a single frame of it being different.

This is why Section 7.1's isolated ad-set structure matters here too.
Mixed objectives in one ad set make this kind of clean read impossible.
Keep them separate, and the right metric for each one is obvious rather
than something you have to reconstruct after the fact.

Ask yourself, before your next test even launches: what's the one
metric that would tell you this specific ad set worked, decided now,
written down, not chosen after the results are already in front of you.
If you can't answer that question before launch, you haven't actually
decided what you're testing yet.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap

::: callout Be straight about this
A vanity metric moving in the right direction feels like good news.
==Check it against the objective you actually set before the test
started, not the one that happens to look flattering now==.
:::

@narrate
One honest trap worth naming, because it's genuinely tempting.

If cost per purchase came back disappointing but click-through rate
looked great, it's natural to lead with the number that looks better.
Resist that. The objective you set before the test started is the one
that gets to judge the result. Switching which metric matters after
seeing the numbers isn't analysis. It's choosing the answer you wanted
and finding a question that fits it.

This is the same honesty Section 7.4 asked of small sample sizes, aimed
at a different failure mode. There, the risk was reading real noise as
a signal. Here, the risk is reading the wrong signal as the right one.
Both mistakes produce a confident, wrong conclusion, and both get fixed
the same way: deciding what counts as evidence before you're looking at
results that might tempt you to redefine it.

Next lecture takes the metric you now know to read and turns it into an
actual decision: winner, loser, or still just noise, using the same
honesty Section 7.4 already asked of small numbers.

Before that, go back through whatever tests you already have live or
recently finished, and check each one against the table from this
lecture. Ask, honestly, whether the metric you used to judge it actually
matched the objective you set. If any of them don't line up, that's not
a reason to redo the test. It's a reason to read it again, properly,
before you draw a conclusion from it.

Do this now, while it costs you nothing but a few minutes of review.
The alternative is carrying a wrong conclusion forward into Section
nine's weekly sprint, where it quietly shapes which angles you decide
are worth building on.
