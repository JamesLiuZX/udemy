@slide statement
kicker: Section 0 · The shift
## Every "we know what works" meeting in this industry rests on one wrong assumption.
lead: That your taste can tell a winning ad from a losing one before the data does.

@narrate
I want to give you one idea in this lecture.

If you take nothing else from this course, take this. It explains why your best
creative instinct is right about as often as it's wrong, why volume beats
polish, and why the account, not you, is the one who finds the winner.

Here it is. You have been trained, by every job before this one, to have taste.
To look at two options and know which is better. That skill is real, and it is
almost useless for this specific job. Nobody told you the job had changed.

@narrate
Here's the shape of the thing you're actually working with.

Twelve variants, one brief, generated by the same system on the same day.
Eight of them lose money outright. Three are noise: not bad enough to kill on
sight, not good enough to matter. One is a real winner, the kind you scale.

Now here's the part that should bother you. Nobody could have told you which
one, in advance, by looking at them. The eleven that didn't work aren't badly
made. They're just not the draw that landed.

@slide definition
kicker: Stating it precisely
## What "found, not picked" actually means

::: definition Key idea
Ad performance is a **draw from a distribution** across the audience, the
moment, and the platform. You cannot inspect a variant and know where in that
distribution it sits. You can only run it and measure where it landed.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Look at your last ten ad tests. Count how many times your pre-launch favourite
was also the actual winner. For most people, that number is close to a coin flip.
:::

@narrate
Let me state it precisely, because the whole course depends on this.

You cannot look at an ad and know its number. You can only run it and read the
number back. Anyone who tells you they can spot a winner on sight is
describing a hunch, dressed up as expertise.

Here's how to feel that in about a minute. Pull your last ten real tests and
count how often the variant you personally liked best going in was also the one
that actually won. If you're honest, that number is uncomfortably close to a
coin flip. It was for me the first time I counted, and it's the reason this
entire course exists.

@slide two-col
kicker: What changes for you
## Two ways to run creative, two different jobs

::: cols
:: card bad
### Picking
- You choose the winner before a dollar is spent
- A loser is a taste failure, and it's personal
- "Which one wins" has an answer in the room
:: card good
### Finding
- You choose the batch, the account finds the winner
- A loser is expected. Most variants are supposed to lose
- "Which one wins" has an answer after the test, never before
:::

@narrate
Look at what that does to the actual job.

On the left, you're picking. You decide the winner before it ever runs, a bad
result is a mark against your judgement, and "which one wins" is a question
somebody in the room is expected to answer with confidence.

On the right, you're finding. You decide the batch and the rules, the account
runs the experiment, and most of what you generated is supposed to lose. A bad
result isn't a defect. It's eleven twelfths of the process working exactly as
designed.

@slide sidenote
kicker: How it plays out
## The pattern, every time

::: split
:: main
1. Someone generates four variants and instantly likes one best
2. That one gets the whole budget, out of confidence, not evidence
3. It underperforms, or worse, does fine and nobody knows why
4. The next round starts from the same untested hunch
:: aside Why step 1 isn't a character flaw
Having a fast, confident opinion about creative is exactly what good marketers
are trained to have. It's the right instinct, aimed at the wrong moment.
:::

@narrate
Watch how predictably this goes wrong.

Someone generates a handful of variants, and one of them just feels right. That
one gets the whole test budget on day one, on confidence rather than evidence.
Sometimes it underperforms and the account eats the loss quietly. Sometimes it
does fine, and everyone credits the person who picked it, learning nothing
about why.

Either way, the next round starts from the same place: an untested hunch,
dressed as a decision. Is that a character flaw? No. Having a fast, confident
read on creative is exactly what this industry has always rewarded. It's the
right instinct, aimed at the wrong moment in the process.

@slide statement
kicker: The root cause
class: center
## Taste cannot beat a distribution.
lead: A hunch about one draw tells you nothing about the shape of the whole range.

@narrate
The root cause is simple, and it's the same shift a sibling course of mine
makes about AI product features: the thing you're judging isn't a fixed object,
it's a distribution, and a single glance is one draw from it.

Your taste is a real, trained skill. It's just answering a question that
doesn't apply here. "Which of these do I like" tells you about you. It tells
you almost nothing about which one twenty thousand strangers, mid-scroll, on a
platform's algorithm, are going to act on.

So what do you do with a skill you can't turn off, but can't trust?

@slide two-col
kicker: The replacement
## Same instinct, now pointed at the machine

::: cols
:: card bad
### What decides today
- "I think this one's the winner"
- A gut call about a single ad
- Budget follows confidence
:: card good
### What decides by Section 7
- A kill rule, written before any data exists
- A batch of variants, tested together
- Budget follows the numbers the test returns
:::

@narrate
Here's the replacement, and it's the spine of everything after this lecture.

You stop trying to pick the winner and start building the system that finds
it: a brief wide enough to cover real angles, a batch generated from it, a
pre-flight check that kills the unshippable ones, and a test with a kill rule
you wrote down before you saw a single result.

Your taste doesn't disappear. It moves. Instead of judging the ad, you judge
the brief, the angles, and the test design. That's a job taste is actually good
at.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
This costs more variants and more small spend than picking one favourite and
running it. You buy that back the first time a test tells you the truth in
three days instead of ==three months of quietly underperforming ads==.
:::

@narrate
I want to be straight about the cost, because most courses selling this skip
it.

Testing means generating more than you'll use and spending real money on
variants that are going to lose. That's not a bug you can engineer away. It's
what finding, instead of picking, actually costs.

Here's the argument that wins it. You were already paying for that loss. It
was just hidden in months of an underperforming favourite nobody tested. A
short, honest test surfaces the same cost in days, with a number attached
instead of a shrug.
