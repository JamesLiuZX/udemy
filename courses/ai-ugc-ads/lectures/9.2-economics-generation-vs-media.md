## Everyone worries about what the generator costs. Almost nobody worries enough about what a bad decision built on top of it costs.
lead: Generation spend and media spend look like the same kind of cost. They aren't, and confusing them leads to the wrong worry.

@narrate
Here's a number worth sitting with before anything else in this lecture.
A batch of twenty statics, the kind Section 3 walked you through
generating, costs somewhere around a dollar or two in raw generation
fees. A single day of Section 7's testing architecture, eight ad sets
at twenty-five dollars each, costs two hundred.

That gap is not a rounding error. Generation is close to free. Media
spend is the real cost this whole system exists to protect. Worrying
about generation cost the way you'd worry about media spend gets the
proportions backwards, and it's an easy mistake to make, because both
show up as numbers on an invoice.

The honest question isn't "can I afford to generate a big batch." At
current prices, almost always yes. It's "can I afford to properly test
what I generate," which is a media-spend question, not a generation-spend
one, and it's the question this lecture actually answers.

Why does this confusion happen so easily? Because generation is the
part you actively do, click a button, wait a few seconds, watch a
result appear, while media spend just quietly accrues in the
background across a whole campaign. The visible cost feels like the
real one. The one compounding silently in an ad account is where your
actual money goes.

## Two very different orders of magnitude

| Item | Rough cost, August 2026 | What it buys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One static image | 1 to 8 cents | One testable variant, per Section 3 |
| One AI UGC-style video clip | 4 to 11 dollars | One testable video variant, per Section 4 |
| One day of Harlan's first test | $200 (8 ad sets, 25 dollars each) | Real signal on which angle wins |
| Harlan's winning angle, scaled | $400 a day, 21-dollar CAC | The core campaign, still under the 24-dollar ceiling |

@narrate
Read down that table and notice where the real money sits. Generating
even a large batch, statics and video variants together, lands under
fifty dollars total. Testing that batch properly, even at Harlan's
modest scale, costs four times that in a single day.

This is why Section 1's volume maths held up economically, not just
statistically. Generating more variants to test barely moves your total
cost, because generation was never the expensive part. Media spend was
always the expensive part, which is why Section 7's kill rules and CAC
ceiling matter so much more than anything about the generator.

Once an angle actually wins, the gap widens further. Harlan's winning
angle now runs at four hundred dollars a day, a real core-campaign
budget, while the generation cost that produced the winning cut was a
handful of dollars, spent once, months ago. The return on that original
spend, at this point, is enormous.

Hold both figures from that bottom row together for a moment. A
core-campaign budget of four hundred dollars a day is only defensible
because the twenty-one-dollar CAC underneath it is real, tested, proven
against Section 8's decision tree, not assumed from a feeling about
which creative "should" work. The generation cost that got here is
trivial. The discipline that turned a cheap batch into a budget worth
scaling to is not.

## B10: the generation-vs-media cost model

```text
FOR YOUR OWN NUMBERS:

1. Cost per static: [tool's price] x images per batch
2. Cost per UGC video: [tool's price] x clips per batch
3. Total generation cost for one batch: line 1 + line 2
4. One day of testing: (daily budget) x (number of ad sets)
5. Ratio: line 4 divided by line 3

IF THE RATIO IS LARGE, GENERATION ISN'T YOUR
CONSTRAINT. TESTING DISCIPLINE IS.
```

@narrate
Five lines, and the entire point is line five. For almost every account
at almost any real scale, that ratio comes out large, meaning one day of
testing costs many times more than the entire batch that made the test
possible.

Fill in your own numbers rather than borrowing Harlan's or this
lecture's. Generation pricing moves quickly, tool to tool and month to
month, and whatever specific price you pay today is worth checking
again before you trust this model six months from now.

Notice what this model is not for. It won't tell you whether to spend
more on testing, that's Section 7's job, decided from your margin, not
from how cheap generation happens to be. It only tells you where your
actual cost pressure sits, so you stop rationing batch size to save
pennies while the real budget decision happens somewhere else entirely.

Line three deserves a second look before you move on. It's tempting to
round it to zero once you've compared it against line four, and that
temptation is itself worth resisting. A batch that costs almost nothing
still costs something, and tracking that real number, however small, is
what keeps this whole model honest rather than becoming an excuse to
stop counting generation spend at all.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap

::: callout Be straight about this
Because generation is nearly free, it's tempting to skip Section 6's
pre-flight discipline on the theory that a bad cut costs nothing to
remake. ==Remaking the creative is free. The media spend wasted before
anyone caught the problem never comes back==.
:::

@narrate
One honest trap worth naming, because cheap generation makes it
genuinely tempting.

If a static costs eight cents and a video clip costs a few dollars, it's
easy to reason that a mistake barely matters, you'll just generate
another one. That's true for the generation cost itself. It is not true
for what happens if the mistake ships before Section 6's checklist
catches it. A claim that oversteps the boundary, or a licence gap on an
avatar, costs you in policy strikes and wasted media spend, not in
generation fees.

Keep the pre-flight discipline exactly as tight as this course already
taught it, regardless of how cheap the generator gets. Cheap generation
is a reason to test more variants. It is never a reason to check them
less carefully before they spend a single dollar of real budget.

Would you skip a safety check on something expensive just because
replacing it would be cheap? Framed that way, the trap is obvious. It's
only the cheapness of generation that makes it feel like an exception,
and nothing about a claim, a rights gap, or a missing disclosure caption
actually gets cheaper to fix once it's already spending real media
budget against a live audience.

@narrate
Next lecture asks a different question about the same economics: who
should actually be doing this work, given how little of the real cost
sits in generation and how much sits in disciplined testing.

Before that, run your own numbers through this lecture's five-line
model, using today's actual prices, not the ones quoted here. Prices in
this category move fast, and a stale number is worse than no number at
all if you end up trusting it.

Hold onto the ratio you calculate, not just the raw numbers either side
of it. A ratio of fifty to one tells a different story than a ratio of
five to one, even if both accounts spend roughly the same on media.
The lower the ratio, the closer generation cost actually is to
mattering, and for most accounts running current tools, that ratio sits
high enough that generation genuinely isn't the constraint. Check your
own, though, rather than assuming that holds for you specifically.
Tool pricing and account scale both vary enough that the honest number
is worth the two minutes it takes to calculate.
