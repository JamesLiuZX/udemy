@narrate
Nobody outside the account sees Section 9.1's Wednesday step, or the
scoring that happens every Monday. What a client, a boss, or your own
future self actually sees is whatever makes it into a report, and
that's a real gap between the work and how it's understood.

This is where Section 8.2's honest ratio gets tested for real. It's one
thing to write "noise" on your own scorecard where nobody's watching.
It's a different thing to write it in a document going to someone
who's expecting good news. The temptation to quietly upgrade a noise
result into something more flattering is strongest exactly here, in the
report, not in the scoring itself.

A good report doesn't hide the honest ratio. It explains it, so the
person reading it understands why most ad sets landing on noise or
loser is the system working, not the system failing.

Why does this explaining matter so much, rather than just reporting the
numbers straight? Because a reader who's never sat through Section 8.2
has no reason to know that a mostly-noise batch is normal. Without that
context, the honest numbers alone can read as failure to someone
who doesn't already share this course's framework for judging them.

@slide table
kicker: What actually belongs in it
## Report the discipline, not just the highlight

| Include | Why | Leave out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Every ad set's verdict, per 8.2's tree | Shows the honest ratio, not a curated one | Cherry-picked wins only |
| Fatigue and refresh status on live winners | Proves the account is being watched, not just launched | Vanity metrics with no decision attached |
| Spend against the CAC ceiling | The number that actually matters to whoever's paying | Impressions or reach alone |
| What changed since last report, and why | Shows the sprint is a process, not a lucky month | A new set of numbers with no story connecting them |

@narrate
Notice the right column isn't wrong information, exactly. Impressions
and reach are real numbers. They're just not the ones that answer
whether the account is making money, and reporting them alongside the
CAC ceiling without that context lets a reader draw whatever conclusion
flatters the numbers most, rather than the one the account actually
supports.

The fourth row is easy to skip and shouldn't be. A report that only
ever shows this week's snapshot asks the reader to trust that progress
is happening without ever showing the trend. Connecting this week to
last week, even briefly, is what turns a report into evidence of a
system rather than a single lucky result repeated on a schedule.

Notice, too, that the left column deliberately mixes good and bad news
in the same rows. Fatigue status includes a winner going tired, not
just a winner performing well. A report that reads this way consistently
signals something a highlight reel never can: nothing here is being
filtered before it reaches you.

@slide code
kicker: The shape of an honest report
## Short, and specific enough to defend

```text
THIS PERIOD:

- Ad sets tested: [count]. Winners: [count]. Losers: [count].
  Still noise: [count].
- Winning angle(s): verdict, CAC against the ceiling, fatigue status
- What changed since last report, and the decision behind it
- What's queued next, from the swipe file or a fresh angle
```

@narrate
Four lines, and every one of them traces back to a lecture you've
already sat through this section. The counts come straight from
Section 8.5's scorecard. Fatigue status comes from 8.3. What's queued
next comes from 9.4's swipe file.

Keep it short on purpose. A report stuffed with every number available
reads as either padding or a distraction from one uncomfortable line
buried in the middle. A short, honest report earns more trust than a
long one, because there's nowhere for anything to hide behind volume.

This same shape works whether you're reporting to a client, a boss, or
just your own records. The version you write for yourself doesn't need
softer framing. If anything, it's the one place brutal honesty costs
you nothing at all.

Fill in the third line even in a quiet week where nothing dramatic
changed. "No change this period, still watching frequency on the
current winner" is a genuine, honest line, and skipping it because
nothing exciting happened is how a report quietly becomes a highlight
reel without anyone deciding that on purpose.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap

::: callout Be straight about this
Reporting only the wins, this quarter, feels safer than the honest
ratio. ==It buys you one good-looking report and a much worse
conversation the first time a client or boss asks why nothing before
this quarter ever got mentioned==.
:::

@narrate
One honest trap worth naming, because it's genuinely tempting under
real pressure to look competent.

A report that only surfaces winners looks better in the short term.
It also can't survive a single follow-up question about last month's
quieter results, and the moment that question lands, every previous
report looks retroactively dishonest, even the parts that were true.
Trust, once it breaks that way, is expensive to rebuild.

The honest ratio, reported consistently from the start, does the
opposite. It sets the expectation early that most weeks are ordinary,
so the weeks with a genuine winner actually stand out, and the reader
already trusts the report enough to believe it when it does.

Would you trust a report that never once mentioned a disappointing
result? Hold that question against your own reaction the next time
you're tempted to leave one out. Whatever answer you'd give as the
reader is the same standard worth holding yourself to as the writer.
