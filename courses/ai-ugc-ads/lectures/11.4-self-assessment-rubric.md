@slide statement
kicker: Section 11 · The rubric
## A capstone you can't honestly score isn't finished. Scoring it is the last step, not an afterthought.
lead: Five checks, each traceable to a specific moment in the two worked passes you just watched.

@narrate
This rubric isn't a new standard. It's the same five checkpoints from
Section 11.1, read against what actually happened in 11.2 and 11.3, so
you can score your own capstone honestly rather than guessing whether
it went well.

Score each line as clear, or genuinely worth revisiting. Don't round up
a shaky answer to make the total look better. The whole point of this
rubric is the same honesty Section 8 asked of every result on Harlan's
account, aimed at your own work instead.

Do this alone, without anyone else grading it for you. Nobody else is
going to check your capstone the way you'd check a real client account,
which means this rubric only works if you hold yourself to the exact
standard you'd expect from someone else's honest report.

@slide table
kicker: The five checks
## Score each honestly, clear or needs work

| Check | What "clear" looks like | Where it's demonstrated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product truth doc | Every claim traces to a real fact, nothing invented | 11.2, opening |
| Batch and pre-flight | Cleared, not just generated, checked against real claims | 11.2, closing |
| Testing architecture | Numbers set before any data existed, not after | 11.3, opening |
| Honest read | A tree verdict, noise included, not a feeling | 11.3, middle |
| Scorecard and report | Written down per Section 8.5, even if the result was noise | 11.3, closing |

@narrate
Work through these in order, the same order the two worked passes used.
A weak product truth doc quietly weakens every check after it, so
starting anywhere else risks scoring a later stage as clear when the
foundation underneath it wasn't.

"Needs work" on any line isn't a failing grade. It's information: go
back to the specific lecture that check traces to, fix that one piece,
and move on. A capstone honestly scored as three clear and two needs
work is more useful to you than one you told yourself was five for
five without actually checking.

Notice the right-hand column points you at a specific worked moment in
11.2 or 11.3, not just a section number. If a line reads unclear, watch
that exact moment again before rewriting anything. Seeing how the
worked example handled it is usually faster than guessing at a fix.

@slide code
kicker: The assembled sheet
## Write this down, don't just read it

```text
FOR YOUR OWN CAPSTONE:

1. Product truth doc: [clear / needs work] because...
2. Batch and pre-flight: [clear / needs work] because...
3. Testing architecture: [clear / needs work] because...
4. Honest read: [clear / needs work] because...
5. Scorecard and report: [clear / needs work] because...

OVERALL: [ready to scale / one more sprint cycle first]
```

@narrate
Fill in the "because" on every line, not just the verdict. "Clear"
without a reason is the same unearned confidence Section 8.2 already
warned you against. A real reason forces you to point at the specific
evidence, not just a feeling that it probably went fine.

The overall line at the bottom isn't automatic from the five above it.
Five clears with a genuine winner, read honestly, might still mean one
more cycle before scaling makes sense, the same patience Section 8.4
asked of Harlan's own refresh before it earned real budget.

Keep this sheet, whatever it says. If every line comes back clear on
the first try, that's a genuinely strong result worth being honest with
yourself about too, not something to second-guess out of habit. Either
way, this sheet is the actual record of where your capstone stood, and
it's worth having on hand the next time you run a real sprint.
