## Sam queued twenty rows on a Wednesday afternoon and watched two mistakes come back with them
lead: Batch generation means turning one brief into many images at once, from a single sheet of prompts.

::: split
:: main
The angle map from Section two becomes twenty prompts. Watch what the
first batch actually ships with, mistakes included.
:: aside Why the mistakes stay in
A batch that worked perfectly first try would teach you nothing about what
to check. This one didn't, and that's more useful.
:::

@narrate
Sam sat down on a Wednesday afternoon to turn the angle map into pixels,
coffee in hand, expecting this to be the easy part of the day. This works
the same way regardless of which specific tool you're using, so I'll show
the mechanics with one real example rather than a tour of every option.

As of this recording, tools like Ideogram support batch generation from a
spreadsheet: one row per prompt, up to hundreds at once, with in-image
text handled directly. Twenty rows on the angle map becomes twenty rows on
a prompt sheet becomes twenty images in one batch.

Before this course's approach, Sam would have opened a single-image tool,
typed one prompt, waited, and either kept the result or started over.
Twenty variants meant twenty separate rounds of that, each one a fresh
wait and a fresh chance to second-guess himself halfway through. A
spreadsheet queue skips all of that: write every row once, submit the
whole sheet, and walk away rather than babysitting one image at a time.

Notice what didn't change between Section two and now. You're not
inventing twenty new ideas at the generation stage. You're transcribing
twenty ideas you already had. If you find yourself improvising a new angle
while writing prompts, that's a sign the angle map wasn't finished, not a
sign you're being efficient.

## One row of the angle map, turned into a prompt

```text
ANGLE MAP ROW
"The 15-20 spec" | Solution-aware | "Do compression socks even work?"

BECOMES THIS PROMPT ROW
Photorealistic product shot, Harlan Crew Sock in navy,
worn by a person mid-shift, clean studio lighting.
Overlay text: "15 to 20 mmHg graduated compression."
Brand colour: Harlan Navy, hex locked.
```

*[SCREENSHOT-NEEDED: Ideogram, batch spreadsheet with this row filled in, the subject/overlay-text/brand-colour fields as they actually appear in the tool]*

@narrate
Here's the actual translation, row by row. The angle map already told you
the rung and the feeling. Turning that into a prompt is mostly transcription:
what's in frame, what the overlay says, and which exact brand colour has to
hold.

A full prompt row usually holds four or five fields: what's in frame, the
pose or setting, the exact overlay text, the brand colour, and a style note
like lighting or crop. Fill those in once per row and the sheet does the
repetition for you. This is where twenty rows earns its keep over four: the
marginal cost of row twenty is the same few minutes of typing as row one,
not another booking, another studio, another invoice.

Do this for all twenty rows, queue the batch, and in the time it takes to
get a coffee, you have twenty statics. That part is genuinely as easy as it
sounds now. What comes back is where it gets interesting.

One detail worth locking in the prompt every time: the exact brand colour,
stated as a hex value if the tool accepts one, not just "navy." "Navy" is
an instruction a model can interpret several honest ways. A hex code is
not. You'll see in a moment why that distinction matters more than it
sounds like it should.

## Twenty statics, two real problems

| Check | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Generated on brief, on time | 20 of 20 |
| Correct product, correct pose | 18 of 20 |
| Correct brand colour, Harlan Navy | 13 of 20. Several rendered a lighter royal blue |
| Overlay text fully legible | 15 of 20. The mmHg spec garbled on five |

*[SCREENSHOT-NEEDED: Ideogram, generated batch results grid, thumbnail view of the completed 20-image batch]*

@narrate
Here's what actually came back, and it's worth sitting with the real
numbers rather than a tidy summary.

All twenty generated on brief and on time. Most got the product right.
But only thirteen of twenty held the exact Harlan Navy: several variants
drifted to a lighter, more generic royal blue instead. And the spec overlay,
the exact fifteen to twenty millimetres of mercury language from the
product truth document, garbled on five of the twenty, exactly the kind of
failure Section 3.1 told you to expect from longer on-image text.

Notice these two failures didn't show up in the same seven statics. They're
independent problems with independent causes, which matters, because it
means fixing one won't accidentally fix the other. Each needs its own
answer, and each gets one in the next two lectures.

Worth naming why the colour drifted at all. A generator reads "navy" as a
broad instruction, not a locked value, and it will happily produce anything
a reasonable person might call navy: sometimes exactly Harlan's shade,
sometimes a brighter, more generic blue. That's not the tool
malfunctioning. It's doing exactly what an unlocked instruction asked for,
which is the whole argument for stating the colour as a hex value instead
of a word, the fix the next lecture actually walks through.

## Not a failed batch. An expected one

::: cols
:: card bad
### What it looks like
- Seven of twenty need real fixes before they're usable
- A brand colour drifting off-spec
- Spec text unreadable on nearly a quarter of the set
:: card good
### What it actually is
- Thirteen still need no fix at all
- Both failures are named, specific, and fixable
- Exactly what Section 3.1 predicted, not a surprise
:::

@narrate
It's tempting to read "seven of twenty need fixes" as a failed batch. Is
it? Look at the two columns again before deciding.

Thirteen of twenty needed nothing at all. The seven that did fail, failed
in two specific, named, entirely expected ways: a colour consistency
problem and a long-text problem, both flagged in the last lecture before
generation even started. Neither failure is mysterious, and neither is
expensive to fix. That's a very different batch from one where you don't
know why anything went wrong.

Compare that to what a batch looks like when nobody read Section 3.1
first. Same failure rate, roughly, but every flawed static reads as a
mystery instead of a checklist item, and the instinct becomes "maybe this
tool just isn't good enough" rather than "fix the colour and the overlay."
Knowing what to expect changes the entire emotional experience of getting
these results back, not just the technical response to them.

It also changes what you do next. Someone who reads this as a failed batch
starts second-guessing the brief, the angle, even the product. Someone who
reads it as an expected batch opens the next lecture and fixes a colour
value. Same twenty statics, two completely different afternoons.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
Do not send a batch like this straight to a test. ==Thirteen good statics
and seven flawed ones are not a twenty-variant test yet==. Sections 3.3 and
3.4 exist to close that exact gap.
:::

@narrate
One honest limit before you get excited about how fast this was.

A batch with seven known problems in it is not ready for Section seven's
test. Running it anyway means testing seven ads that fail for a reason you
already know about, which wastes real budget confirming something you
didn't need a test to tell you. The next two lectures fix exactly these two
failures, properly, so the batch that actually launches is twenty for
twenty on the things you can control.

Put a number on that waste, roughly. If seven of twenty statics are already
known to be flawed and they run in a test anyway, you're not learning
whether Harlan's message works. You're re-discovering, at real media
spend, a fact the checklist already told you for free. That's the
expensive way to learn something you already knew, and it's exactly the
mistake this checklist exists to make unnecessary.

## Count your own batch the same way

- Generate twenty statics from your own angle map, or open a batch you already have, and check exactly two things: how many hold your exact brand colour, and how many keep every word of on-image text fully legible
- Write both numbers down next to the batch. They're what Section 3.3 and 3.4 fix against, and they feed straight into artifact B03

@narrate
Next lecture takes on the colour problem directly, and it's a smaller fix
than the failure rate makes it look.

Before that, do Harlan's count on your own batch. Don't fix anything yet,
just check: how many statics hold your exact brand colour, and how many
keep every word of on-image text legible. Thirteen of twenty is roughly
where Harlan started, and it's a fine bar to compare yourself against, not
a score to beat on the first try.

Write both numbers down next to your batch. They become artifact B03's
actual before picture: the flawed batch a fix gets measured against,
rather than a vague sense that some of it needs work. Knowing your own two
numbers now is what makes Section 3.3's fix land immediately instead of
staying abstract.
