::: callout "Finally, someone gets it." "Wait, what's compression?" Same ad, minutes apart.
Sam found both comments under Harlan's best-performing static on a Tuesday

:::

@narrate
Sam noticed it while checking comments on a Tuesday: two replies, minutes
apart, under the exact same ad. One reader wrote "finally, someone gets it."
Another asked, genuinely confused, "wait, what's compression?" Same sock,
same headline, same day. Only one of those two people was actually ready
to hear it.

That's not a copywriting problem. It's a mistake that looks like efficiency
and is actually the opposite of it: write one strong angle, generate twenty
variations of it, run all twenty at one broad audience. It feels thorough.
It's actually one bet, repeated twenty times, because it ignores the single
biggest thing that changes whether an ad works: someone's awareness level,
meaning how far up the ladder from "never thought about this" to "already a
customer" they already are before they ever see your ad.

This is easy to miss because the twenty variants really do look different
from each other. Different colours, different hooks, different formats. What
they share, underneath the surface variety, is that every one of them was
written for the same imagined viewer, and that viewer doesn't actually
exist as a single person in your audience.

@slide table
kicker: The ladder
## Five states, and what Harlan says at each one

| Awareness level | What they already believe | What the ad has to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware | Feet hurt. Hasn't connected it to a category | Name the problem before selling anything |
| Problem-aware | Knows their feet hurt after a shift | Introduce compression as a real category |
| Solution-aware | Knows compression socks exist | Say why Harlan's spec is the one that matters |
| Product-aware | Knows Harlan, hasn't bought | Handle the objection that's actually stopping them |
| Most aware | Already a customer | Remind, don't persuade |

@narrate
Here's the ladder, and Harlan's actual audience sits on every rung of it at
once.

Someone who's never thought about their sore feet as a solvable problem is
at the bottom. Someone who already searched "compression socks for a
twelve hour shift" last week is much higher up, and already knows the
category exists. Someone who added a pack to their cart and didn't check out
is nearly at the top, and needs a completely different sentence than the
person on the bottom rung.

Read down the right-hand column and notice it isn't a ladder of "harder
sell" to "easier sell." It's a ladder of different jobs entirely. The
bottom rung is an education job. The top rung is a memory job. Treating
both as "convince them to buy" is where a single generic angle quietly
fails at both ends at once.

The comment Sam found belongs on this table too, if you want to place it.
"Finally, someone gets it" reads like solution-aware or higher: someone who
already knew the category and just needed Harlan's specific proof. "Wait,
what's compression?" is unaware, full stop, and the exact reader that
comment was answering never got asked for.

@slide two-col
kicker: Why this matters more than the hook itself
## Same product, right level versus wrong level

::: cols
:: card bad
### Wrong level
- "Introducing Harlan" to someone who already knows the brand
- A hard sell to someone who's never heard of compression socks
- Feels generic to everyone, persuasive to no one
:: card good
### Right level
- A cold audience gets the problem named first
- A warm audience gets the objection actually answered
- Feels specific, because it's speaking to what they already think
:::

@narrate
Watch what happens when the level is wrong, because it's not a small miss.

Introduce a brand to someone who already knows it, and you sound like
you're not paying attention. Open with a hard sell to someone who's never
heard of compression socks, and you've skipped the part where they'd need
to believe the category matters at all. Both ads can be well written. Both
will still underperform, because the mismatch isn't a copy problem. It's a
targeting problem wearing copy as a disguise.

This also explains a specific, confusing pattern you'll see in real
accounts: an ad that performs brilliantly on one audience and falls flat on
another, with nothing about the ad itself changed. Usually the audience
changed rungs, and the ad didn't move with it.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming the actual skill
## What "matching the angle" really means

::: definition Key idea
Matching means writing the *one sentence the audience needs next*, not the
one sentence you'd most like to say. A cold audience needs the problem
named. A warm audience needs the objection answered. Neither needs your
best line if it's aimed at the wrong rung.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Take one ad you've already run. Guess which rung it was actually written
for. If you can't answer in one word, it probably wasn't written for any
rung in particular.
:::

@narrate
Let's name the actual skill, because "know your audience" is true and
useless as advice.

Matching means writing the one sentence the audience needs next, not the
one you're proudest of. A cold audience needs the problem named before
anything else lands. A warm audience needs their specific hesitation
answered, directly, not a better version of the pitch they've already seen
twice.

Try this on something you've already shipped. Pick a real ad and guess,
honestly, which rung it was written for. If the honest answer is "I'm not
sure", that's usually because it wasn't written for a rung at all. It was
written to sound good, and sounding good is a different job.

This is also a useful way to read someone else's ad, not just your own.
Scroll past a competitor's ad and ask the same question: what does this
assume I already believe? Most ads answer that question by accident. Yours
should answer it on purpose.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest cost

::: callout Be straight about this
Matching angles to awareness levels means writing five times the strategy
for the same number of ads. ==Most cheap batches skip this entirely==, which
is exactly why most cheap batches underperform.
:::

@narrate
I want to be honest about what this costs, because it's real work, and
skipping it is exactly how most AI-generated batches end up mediocre.

Matching properly means deciding, for every angle, which rung it's aimed
at, before you write a word of copy. That's real thinking time, on top of
the generation itself, and it's tempting to skip straight to "make me
twenty ads." The batches that skip it are the ones that generate twenty
technically-fine variants that all quietly say the same thing to everyone.

The good news is that this cost is paid once, not twenty times. You decide
the ladder for a product once, and every future batch for that product
reuses it. It's expensive the first time and nearly free after that, which
is a trade worth making early rather than avoiding forever.

It's also the cost that would have caught Sam's comment problem before it
ever ran. A ladder decided in advance means you're choosing, on purpose,
which rungs a given ad is for, instead of finding out from a confused
comment after the spend is already gone.
