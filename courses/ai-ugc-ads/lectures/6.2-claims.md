::: callout A disclaimer doesn't fix a false claim
It just adds a second sentence admitting you know it might be false.
"Results may vary" has never once made an untrue claim true. It only
proves you saw the problem and shipped anyway.
:::

@narrate
Somewhere in every marketer's instincts is the idea that a small-print
disclaimer buys cover for a big, bold claim above it. It doesn't, and
it's worth being precise about why, because this is the single most
common way an ad crosses a line its writer didn't mean to cross.

A claim is either true and defensible, or it isn't. Adding "results may
vary" underneath a number you invented doesn't move that number into the
defensible column. It just documents, in writing, that you knew the claim
needed a caveat and shipped it anyway.

Where does this habit come from? A disclaimer genuinely works around a
true but variable outcome, "individual results vary" under an honest
before-and-after photo. That's legitimate. The mistake is pointing that
same small print at a claim that isn't variable, it's simply false.

Ask which situation you're actually in before you reach for that line.
"Some customers feel a difference sooner than others" is honestly
hedged. "Reduces swelling by 40%" isn't hedging a range around a true
effect, it's asserting a number that was never measured. No small print
turns the second sentence into the first.

kicker: The actual test
## Defensible, or not, and the disclaimer doesn't move the row

| Claim | Defensible? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "15 to 20 mmHg graduated compression" | Yes | A real, documented spec, checkable against the product |
| "Built for people who don't get to sit down" | Yes | A description of the audience, not a medical outcome |
| "Reduces swelling by 40%" | No | A specific, invented number, no study behind it |
| "Reduces swelling by 40%*" with a disclaimer | Still no | The asterisk doesn't create the missing study |

@narrate
Notice the actual test running underneath this table: could you produce
the evidence, right now, if someone asked? The spec claim passes because
it's a manufacturing fact, checkable against the product itself. The
audience description passes because it's not a claim about an outcome at
all. The swelling number fails, disclaimer or not, because the evidence
it implies simply doesn't exist.

That's the whole test, in one question. Not "does this sound
defensible." Could you actually defend it, out loud, to someone whose job
is to ask you to.

Notice the two safe rows pass for different reasons, and that
distinction matters. One survives as a checkable fact, the other because
it never claimed an outcome at all. Knowing them apart tells you where
the real creative freedom lives: you don't lose specificity or a strong
hook by staying honest. You lose only the shortcut of inventing an
outcome instead of describing the product accurately enough that the
description alone does the persuading.

## The claim that never shipped

```text
DRAFT
"Clinically proven to reduce swelling by 40%."
No study. No number Dana had ever measured. Invented
because it sounded more credible than the real spec.

CAUGHT
Pre-flight claims check, before any spend.

REWRITE
"15 to 20 mmHg graduated compression. Built for
people who don't get to sit down."
Same product. A claim Dana can actually defend.
```

@narrate
Here's what actually happened, and it's worth sitting with why the
invented number got written in the first place. Nobody set out to lie.
Whoever wrote that draft was reaching for something that sounded more
concrete than the real spec, and a specific percentage does sound more
concrete. It's also completely fabricated, and fabricated is fabricated
whether or not the person writing it meant harm.

The fix wasn't softening the claim with a disclaimer. It was replacing an
invented number with a real one Dana could stand behind in front of
anyone who asked. Same product, same confidence in the delivery, a
claim that's actually true instead of one that merely sounds true.

Notice, too, what this rewrite didn't cost. The delivery is still
specific, still concrete, still exactly the kind of detail Section 2.3
taught you a good hook needs. Nothing about honesty here required going
vague. It required going true instead of going invented, which turned
out to be the same amount of work as the fabricated version, just aimed
at a real fact instead of a convenient one.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest check

::: callout Be straight about this
If you wouldn't say a claim out loud to a compliance officer, or to the
customer it's about, ==don't ship it with an asterisk instead. Rewrite it
to something you actually can defend==.
:::

@narrate
One test, worth running on every claim before it ships, not just the
ones that feel risky.

Say the line out loud, exactly as written, to an imagined compliance
officer, or better, to the actual customer the ad is trying to reach. If
that sentence would survive being asked "how do you know that," ship it.
If it wouldn't, no disclaimer fixes that gap. Only a different sentence
does.

Do this before you generate, not after. It's far easier to write a
defensible line from the start than to catch an overreach buried in a
finished script, especially once it's been performed by an avatar and
sounds convincing coming out of someone's mouth. A false claim delivered
with real conviction is not easier to catch. It's harder, because the
performance itself is doing work to make the words sound truer than they
are.

That's a genuinely new risk this course's own workflow introduces, worth
naming plainly rather than glossing over. A flat script on a page invites
scrutiny. A confidently performed avatar clip invites trust. Check the
words before the performance gets a chance to earn trust they haven't
actually earned.

hesitate even slightly, that hesitation is the signal. Rewrite it now,

does.
