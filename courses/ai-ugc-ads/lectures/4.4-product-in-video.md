## An avatar can talk convincingly. The product in their hands is a harder problem.
lead: The same product-accuracy risk from Section 3.3, made worse by twenty-five frames a second instead of one.

@narrate
An avatar's face is one problem, and Section three-point-three already
gave you the tools for it. The product in that avatar's hands is a
separate, harder problem, because now it has to look right in every
single frame, not just one.

A generated image only has to be accurate once. A generated video has to
hold that same accuracy across hundreds of frames without drifting, and
that's a meaningfully harder task than the one Section three already
solved.

Why does this matter more than it sounds like it should? Because a static
that drifts slightly off-brand is one flawed image out of twenty. A video
that drifts partway through is one flawed ad, in full, that a viewer
watches start to finish.

@slide table
kicker: The honest split
## What to generate, what to actually film

| Shot | Generate | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Avatar talking, no product in frame | Yes | Section 4.3's problem, already solved |
| Generic background, setting, mood | Yes | Low stakes, nothing to misrepresent |
| Product being worn, used, demonstrated | No, film it | Frame-to-frame accuracy risk is real |

@narrate
Here's the honest split, and it follows directly from that mechanism.

An avatar talking, with no product in shot, is exactly what Section 4.3
already handles well. A generic background or setting carries almost no
risk, because nothing about it is a specific claim. But the moment the
actual product needs to be seen being worn, used, or demonstrated across
several seconds, generate it and you're betting that accuracy holds for
every one of those frames. Film it instead, on an actual phone, and that
risk simply isn't there to manage.

Run the same test you'd run on a static: would Dana recognise this as the
actual product she sells, held for the full length of the shot, not just
the first frame? For a filmed clip, that's automatically yes. For a
generated one, it's a question you'd have to keep checking, frame after
frame, for as long as the product stays on screen.

## Harlan's "hour eleven" ad, shot by shot

```text
0-4s   AVATAR, generated: the hook, spoken to camera
4-9s   PRODUCT, filmed: a real ten-second phone clip
       of the actual sock, adjusted mid-shift
9-15s  AVATAR, generated: the CTA, spoken to camera
```

@narrate
Here's what that actually looks like cut together, and notice it isn't
an all-or-nothing choice between generated and filmed.

The avatar carries the hook and the close, both generated, both handled
well by Section 4.3's workflow. The middle five seconds, the part where
the sock itself has to be seen clearly and accurately, is a real phone
clip, filmed once, reused across every variant that needs this exact
product shot. One short filming session buys accuracy for the one part of
the ad that actually needed it.

That reuse is the part worth holding onto. You're not filming per ad.
You're filming once per product, then cutting that same clip into as many
variants as the angle map calls for, which keeps the one-time cost small
against a whole batch's worth of generated avatar footage.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest cost

::: callout Be straight about this
This means keeping one phone in the workflow. ==A single ten-second clip,
filmed once, is a small cost against the risk of a product that quietly
looks wrong for hundreds of frames==.
:::

@narrate
I want to be honest about what "video ads without a shoot" actually means
in practice, because this course isn't claiming zero filming, ever.

It means no studio, no crew, no shoot day. It doesn't mean zero footage.
One short clip of the real product, filmed once on a phone, gets reused
across every variant that needs it. That's a small, one-time cost against
the risk of a product that drifts subtly wrong across an entire generated
clip, and nobody catching it until the ad's already running.
