## Dana Got Two Lines Into "Brand Voice: Warm, Confident, Trustworthy." Then She Deleted Both.
lead: What she wrote instead is the one page every later angle, hook and claim in this course has to answer to.

@narrate
Sam had asked for the usual creative brief: tone words, a mood board,
something to hand a generator. Dana opened a blank doc, typed "brand voice:
warm, confident, trustworthy," and stopped. None of that was actually true
or false. None of it was something a generator, or Dana herself at
midnight, could check a line of ad copy against.

So she deleted it and wrote three things instead: what Harlan actually
charges, what it actually costs to make and ship a pair, and the one claim
about the socks she will never let ship, no matter how well it tests. That
short page has a name: the product truth document, the page that says what's
provably true about a product, not what sounds good about it. Its whole job
is answering one question: what can this brand actually prove?

Notice what that question isn't. It isn't "what sounds good", and it isn't
"what would a competitor say." Those questions come later, and they come
easily. This one comes first because it's the only question a generator, a
copywriter, or an entire batch of twenty variants can be checked against
after the fact.

@slide code
kicker: What it actually looks like
## Harlan's product truth document

```text
PRODUCT: Harlan Crew Sock, 3-pack
PRICE: $58

PROVABLE SPEC
15 to 20 mmHg graduated compression.
This is the number Dana can defend without a study behind it.

WHAT WE CAN CLAIM
"Engineered for 15 to 20 mmHg graduated compression"
"Built for people on their feet 10+ hours a shift"

WHAT WE CANNOT CLAIM
Any medical outcome ("reduces swelling", "prevents clots")
Any invented percentage or statistic
Any claim Dana has not personally reviewed
```

@narrate
Here's Harlan's, in full, and notice how short it is. That's not a first
draft. That's the finished document.

The price and the spec are just facts, copied over from what you already
know about the account. The interesting part is the bottom two sections. One
lists exactly what the brand is allowed to say. The other lists exactly what
it isn't, and that second list exists because Dana has watched people
overclaim products far less regulated than a compression sock and pay for it
later.

Read the "what we can claim" section again, and notice how unglamorous it
is on its own. Neither line is a hook. Neither is meant to be. This document
isn't trying to stop the scroll. It's trying to make sure whatever does stop
the scroll, written later, doesn't say anything Dana can't stand behind if
someone screenshots it.

@slide two-col
kicker: What belongs here, and what doesn't
## A narrower document than most people expect

::: cols
:: card good
### Belongs in the product truth doc
- Price, margin, and anything a number depends on
- Specs you can defend without inventing a study
- An explicit list of claims that are off-limits
:: card bad
### Doesn't belong here
- Tone, mood, or "brand voice" guidance
- Angle ideas or hooks
- Anything aspirational that isn't yet true
:::

@narrate
It's worth being strict about what stays out of this document, because the
temptation is to let it become a mood board.

Tone, angle ideas (a specific product story you could test), hooks, all of
that belongs later, once you're actually briefing creative. If you let
those in here, you dilute the one job this
document has: being the fact-check every later claim gets measured against.
An angle can be bold. A fact either survives Dana reading it or it doesn't.

This is also why the document is short. If it ran to three pages, it would
start accumulating opinions disguised as facts, and opinions are exactly
what it exists to keep out. A page, or less, forces the discipline: only
things that are actually true make the cut.

@narrate
Here's the line from the code slide, made concrete with actual sentences,
because "spec versus outcome" is easy to agree with in the abstract and
easy to miss in a real draft.

The first two rows describe the product. They're checkable against the
spec sheet, and they stay true no matter who's reading them. The bottom
three describe a result in someone's body, or borrow a credibility Harlan
hasn't earned. Notice that all five sentences could plausibly come out of a
generator asked for "persuasive copy about compression socks." Only two of
them are Harlan's to say.

@slide definition
kicker: Why the boundary matters
## The claims boundary, defined precisely

::: definition Key idea
A **claims boundary** is an explicit list of statements a brand will not
make, decided in advance, by the person who has to answer for them. It
exists so a generator, or a tired copywriter at midnight, has something
firmer than "does this sound okay" to check a line against.
:::

::: example Why this specific boundary
Compression socks sit near a real regulatory line: claim a medical outcome
and you've implied a device claim you can't support. "15 to 20 mmHg" is a
spec. "Reduces swelling" is a promise about a body. Only one of those is Dana's to make.
:::

@narrate
Let's be precise about why the boundary matters, because "don't overclaim"
sounds obvious until you're the one under deadline pressure, staring at a
draft that would perform better with one small exaggeration in it.

A claims boundary is a line drawn in advance, by the person who has to
answer for it, before that pressure exists. For Harlan, the line runs
between a spec and an outcome. Fifteen to twenty millimetres of mercury of
graduated compression is a fact about the product. Reducing swelling is a
claim about somebody's body, and that's a promise Dana hasn't earned the
right to make. You'll see exactly what happens when a draft crosses that
line in Section six, and it isn't a small problem when it does.

Why draw the line at "outcome" specifically, rather than somewhere looser?
Because an outcome claim is the one kind of statement a generator will
produce confidently and completely unprompted. Ask for persuasive copy
about a compression sock and a health-outcome sentence is a natural, fluent
thing for a model to write. It isn't lying exactly. It's doing what
persuasive copy about health products has always tended to sound like,
without knowing that this specific brand hasn't run the study to back it up.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest cost

::: callout Be straight about this
A tight claims boundary will kill some genuinely persuasive lines. That's
not a bug. ==A claim you can't defend is a liability wearing a good headline==.
:::

@narrate
I want to be honest about what this costs you, because a real claims
boundary does cost something.

Some of the most persuasive lines a generator will hand you will also be
the ones that cross the line Dana drew. "Doctor recommended." "Clinically
proven." Cutting those isn't caution for its own sake. Every one of them is a
liability wearing a good headline, and the account that keeps one in because
it tested well is one platform review away from losing the whole ad account,
not just that ad.

That trade only feels bad in the abstract. In practice, the variants that
survive the boundary still have plenty to work with: a real spec number, a
real audience, a real problem. You're not trading away persuasion. You're
trading away the specific persuasion that was never actually yours to use.

document, viewed from a different angle each time.

Before you write anything else this week, get your own three lines down:
price, margin, and the one claim you would not want to defend under bright
lights. That's page one of the document the workshop in 2.6 turns into
artifact B01, and it takes fifteen minutes if you don't let yourself
overthink the claims boundary yet.
