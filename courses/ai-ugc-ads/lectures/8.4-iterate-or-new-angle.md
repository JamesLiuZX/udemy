@slide statement
kicker: Section 8 · Iterate or new
## "Make something new" hides two completely different jobs. Confusing them wastes a fast fix on a slow problem, or the reverse.
lead: A refresh reuses what already worked. A new angle starts over from the map. The data tells you which one you actually need.

@narrate
Section 8.3 answered the first question: a fatigued winner gets
refreshed, not killed. It deliberately left a second question open. What
happens when the refresh itself goes tired?

Two different moves both get called "making something new," and they
cost completely different amounts of effort. A refresh keeps the angle,
the claim, the argument, and changes only the surface: a new hook, a new
cut, a new pace. A new angle goes back to Section 2.4's map and starts
an entirely different argument, aimed at the same audience from a
different direction.

Reaching for the wrong one is expensive in a specific way. Refresh an
angle that has genuinely run its course, and you're polishing something
the audience is done with, for the second or third time, before you'll
admit it. Rebuild from scratch when a simple refresh would have worked,
and you've thrown away an angle that still had life left in it, along
with the effort it took to prove that angle in the first place.

Neither mistake announces itself at the time. Both look like reasonable
decisions in the moment, made with the numbers you had. The only way to
tell them apart in advance is to know which signal actually separates
them, which is what the rest of this lecture is for.

@slide table
kicker: Reading the signal
## Three situations, three different calls

| Situation | What it means | The move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Never cleared the kill rule (Section 8.2) | The angle itself never landed | New angle, never a refresh |
| Winner, tired for the first time (Section 8.3) | The cut wore out, the angle didn't | Refresh: same angle, new cut |
| Refreshed once already, tired again quickly | The whole angle is wearing out, not just the cut | New angle, retire this one |

@narrate
Notice the third row is the one Section 8.3 didn't cover, and it's the
one that actually needs judgement. A loser never earns a refresh, that
call is easy. A winner's first fatigue gets refreshed, that call is
easy too, because the same cut simply ran its course against a fixed
audience.

The genuinely hard call is what happens after that refresh. If the
refreshed cut recovers something close to its original performance and
runs a healthy stretch before fatigue returns, the angle is still
alive, and refreshing again is the right move. If it fatigues faster
than the original, or never fully recovers, that's a different signal.
The audience isn't tired of one cut anymore. It's tired of the whole
argument.

That distinction is why frequency alone was never enough on its own.
Section 8.3 asked you to watch it per cut. This decision asks you to
watch it across cuts, over the life of one angle, a longer memory than
a single ad set's dashboard usually keeps for you.

Nothing here fixes how many refresh cycles is too many, and that's
deliberate. A strong angle might earn three or four before it's spent.
A weaker one might only earn one. The count isn't the signal. The
pattern across cycles is, which is what the next slide reads.

@slide code
kicker: Reading the pattern across cycles
## Two very different second acts

```text
PATTERN A: THE ANGLE STILL HAS LIFE
Cut 1 wins, runs its course, fatigues. Refresh ships.
Cut 2 recovers close to Cut 1's original performance,
runs a comparable healthy stretch before fatigue returns.
READ: refresh again. The angle isn't the problem.

PATTERN B: THE ANGLE IS SPENT
Cut 1 wins, fatigues. Refresh ships.
Cut 2 never fully recovers, or fatigues in a fraction
of Cut 1's runtime.
READ: retire the angle. Time for the map, not another cut.
```

@narrate
This is the actual continuation of the "hour eleven" story from Section
8.3. That refresh follows Pattern A: it recovers, clears the winner
tree again on its own merits, and keeps earning its place in the
account. That's exactly why it's still the creative Section 9 scales
from twenty-five dollars a day to real budget, not a creative that got
one lucky cut and then quietly died.

Not every refresh goes that way, and that's the entire point of
watching the pattern instead of assuming it. An angle genuinely does
wear out eventually, and Pattern B is what that looks like on a
dashboard: a refresh that can't recover what the original cut had, or
recovers it for a much shorter stretch. When you see that, more
refreshing isn't stubbornness paying off, it's spending effort on
diminishing returns.

Either read takes two data points, not one. A single cut's fatigue
never tells you which pattern you're in. Only a refresh, read against
the cut before it, does.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap

::: callout Be straight about this
A refresh is cheap: same footage, same claim, same hook to reuse. ==That
cheapness quietly biases you toward refreshing even when the data is
telling you Pattern B. Easy is not the same test as correct==.
:::

@narrate
One honest trap worth naming, because it's the kind of bias that never
announces itself.

A refresh reuses almost everything you already have. A new angle means
going back to Section 2.4's map, picking a different one of the twenty,
and building it out properly. One of those is a smaller job than the
other, and smaller jobs get chosen by default far more often than they
get chosen because the data actually supports them.

Guard against that by deciding the call from the pattern first, before
you weigh how much work either option costs. If Pattern B is genuinely
what the numbers show, the extra effort of a new angle isn't waste,
it's the honest read. Spending it there beats spending less effort
refreshing something the audience has already told you it's done with.

There's a second version of this same trap, quieter than the first.
It shows up as reluctance rather than laziness: an angle you're
personally attached to, maybe the one that proved the whole system
worked in the first place, gets an extra refresh cycle it hasn't
earned, simply because retiring it feels like giving something up. The
data doesn't know which angle you're fond of. Reading Pattern B
correctly means acting on it even when the angle in question is the
one you'd least like to let go of.

Hold this decision next to Section 8.2's tree one more time, because
they share the same discipline underneath. There, the honest answer was
often noise, not winner or loser, and resisting the urge to force a
clean verdict was the whole lesson. Here, the honest answer is often
Pattern A, an angle that still has life left, not the more dramatic
story of an angle finally spent. Both cases reward the same patience:
read what the numbers actually show, not the more interesting
conclusion you'd rather be reporting back to the account.
