@slide callout
kicker: Section 1 · The morning Dana pointed at the winner

::: callout Dana pointed at the screen. "That one." She was confident, and she was wrong.
Confidence is a feeling, not a correlate of which variant wins: not weakly
correlated, not correlated less than you would like. Uncorrelated.
:::

@narrate
Section zero showed you this exact moment: Dana and Sam each privately
picked a winner before any data existed, both confident, both wrong about
which one actually won. This lecture explains why it happens, mechanically,
so it stops feeling like bad luck and starts feeling like something you can
plan around.

Here's the claim, stated as sharply as I can state it. How confident someone
is that a variant will win tells you almost nothing about whether it
actually will. Not "somewhat less than you'd hope". Close to nothing.

That's a hard claim to sit with if you've built a career on having good
instincts, so let's not just assert it. What does it actually look like when
you write confidence down before a test and compare it to what happens
after?

## Confidence rated before the test, rank after it

| Variant | Team's confidence, before | Actual rank, of 12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The one they loved | High | 8th |
| The safe, obvious choice | Medium | 6th |
| "Included it to be thorough" | Low | 1st, the winner |
| Everyone's second favourite | High | 11th |

@narrate
This is a realistic shape for what happens when you rate confidence before a
test and rank results after it, and it's worth sitting with for a second.

The variant the team loved most landed near the bottom. The one nobody
believed in, generated almost as an afterthought to round out the batch,
won outright. This isn't a rigged example to make a point. It's the
ordinary, unremarkable shape of a real test, and if you've run more than a
handful of these yourself, some version of this table probably matches your
own results.

Notice, too, that "safe and obvious" didn't protect the second row either.
It landed squarely in the middle, neither embarrassing nor useful. Playing
it safe doesn't buy you a mediocre-but-reliable result. It just buys you a
guess with less conviction behind it, which is not the same thing as a
better guess.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming the mechanism
## Why confidence and outcome come apart

::: definition Key idea
A winning variant wins because of an interaction between the creative, the
exact audience, the platform's delivery, and the moment. Confidence is
judged from the creative alone. It cannot see the other three factors, so it
cannot predict an outcome that depends on all four.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Before your next test, have everyone rank the variants blind, then compare
rankings to each other before you compare them to results. If your team
doesn't even agree with each other, agreeing with reality was never on the table.
:::

@narrate
Here's the actual mechanism, not just the observation.

A variant wins because of how it interacts with the audience seeing it, the
placement it lands in, the algorithm's delivery choices that day, and the
mood of someone mid-scroll at that exact moment. Looking at a static image or
a script gives you exactly one of those four inputs. Confidence built from
one input out of four isn't weak evidence. It's evidence about a different
question than the one you're actually trying to answer.

Try this before your next real test, because it's a fast way to feel the
scale of the problem. Have two or three people rank the variants blind,
without discussing it first, then compare their rankings to each other
before anyone looks at results. Most of the time, they won't even agree with
each other. If two experienced people can't agree on the ranking, expecting
either of them to agree with reality was never realistic.

@slide sidenote
kicker: How this shows up in a real account
## The loudest guess usually wins the budget

::: split
:: main
1. A senior person states a confident opinion in the room
2. Nobody has data yet, so confidence is the only signal available
3. That opinion gets the budget, quietly, by default
4. The result confirms or embarrasses them, and either way nothing was learned about the other variants
:: aside The pattern has a name
Marketers call it the HiPPO effect: the Highest Paid Person's Opinion wins
the room, not because it's more accurate, but because it's the only
confident voice in a room with no data yet.
:::

@narrate
Here's how this plays out organisationally, and it's worth naming because
it's not really about any one person's judgement.

In a room with no data yet, the most confident voice fills the vacuum, and
the most confident voice usually belongs to whoever's most senior or most
invested. That opinion gets the budget by default, not through a deliberate
decision that it's more likely to be right. Marketers have a blunt name for
this: the HiPPO effect, the highest paid person's opinion, winning not on
accuracy but on volume and rank.

It's not a character flaw in senior people. It's what happens by default
when confidence is the only currency in the room. The fix isn't a better
opinion. It's removing opinion from the decision entirely, which is exactly
what a kill rule, decided before anyone's seen a result, does.

There's a quieter cost to this pattern too, beyond the wasted budget. When
the loudest guess always wins, the people with the least confident voice in
the room, often the ones actually running the account day to day, learn to
stop offering their read at all. You lose their signal permanently, not just
on this one test.

@slide two-col
kicker: What taste is still for
## Not worthless. Wrongly deployed

::: cols
:: card bad
### Where taste has no signal
- Ranking which variant will convert best
- Predicting a winner from the creative alone
- Settling "which one's the winner" in a room
:: card good
### Where taste still matters completely
- Deciding what's on-brand and what isn't
- Catching a claim that reads as dishonest
- Writing a brief wide enough to cover real angles
:::

@narrate
I don't want you to walk away thinking judgement is useless now, because
that's not the argument and it isn't true.

Taste has no predictive signal for ranking winners, and that's the specific,
narrow claim of this lecture. It has plenty of signal for deciding what's on
brand, catching a claim before it embarrasses the account, and writing a
brief wide enough to cover angles a narrower brief would have missed. Those
are real skills, and none of this course asks you to switch them off.

The discipline is knowing which job you're doing. Judging the brief: your
call. Judging which finished ad wins: not your call, not anyone's, until the
test says so.

If you find that distinction hard to hold onto in the moment, here's a
rough test. If the question is "would I be comfortable this went out under
our name", that's taste, and it's yours to answer. If the question is
"which one will perform best", that's not a question taste can answer at
all, no matter how it's phrased.
