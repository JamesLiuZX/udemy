## Sam pushed the clinical-authority cut and the raw "12-hour shift" cut live on Meta and TikTok inside the same hour, sure one would win everywhere. Meta answered first.
lead: Whichever cut wins here still has to survive a shape it wasn't necessarily built for, the same problem Section 4 never had to solve.

@narrate
Picture Sam that Tuesday: the clinical-authority cut, ex-ER nurse
credentials and the spec first, against the raw cut with no framing at
all, both live the same hour. He bet one would win everywhere.

Meta answered first, and the clinical cut took it, clearly enough that
Sam stopped there. Meta doesn't have one feed. It has Feed, Stories, and
Reels, and each one crops, frames, and behaves differently.

As of this recording, three ratios cover nearly all of Meta's delivery:
four-by-five vertical for Feed, nine-by-sixteen for Stories and Reels, and
one-by-one square as the safe fallback when you're not sure which
placement will pick up an ad. Nine-by-sixteen has only grown more central
since Instagram folded its separate Explore feed into Reels earlier this
year, pushing more discovery traffic into the full-screen format.

Confirm these specifics in your own ads manager before you rely on them.
Meta reshapes its placements on its own schedule, not yours.

Why does this matter more now than it used to? A landscape ad used to be
close enough for every placement. That's no longer true: as more of
Meta's own traffic moves into full-screen vertical formats, a landscape
ad loses the top and bottom of frame to letterboxing on the placement
most people now actually use. "Close enough" and "built for the format"
have drifted further apart, not closer.

@slide table
kicker: The actual shapes
## Three ratios, three jobs

| Placement | Ratio | What it's for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feed | 4:5 vertical | Meta's own data favours this over 16:9 for completion on mobile |
| Stories / Reels | 9:16 vertical | Full-screen, the format most of Meta's discovery traffic now sees |
| Fallback | 1:1 square | Safe default when the exact placement isn't confirmed |

@narrate
Notice none of these is landscape. Every shape Meta actually rewards is
taller than it is wide, because every one of them is designed to fill a
phone held upright, not a screen turned sideways.

This matters for the assembly discipline you already built in Section
4.5. If you cut your avatar and product footage in nine-by-sixteen from
the start, one export covers Stories and Reels outright, and a centred
crop covers Feed's four-by-five without losing the frame. Cut in
landscape first, and you're fighting a crop on every single placement
Meta actually delivers into.

There's a practical order worth locking in here. Shoot and generate at
nine-by-sixteen first, treat everything else as a derivative export.
Reframing a square or landscape source up to full-screen usually means
losing edges or padding them with empty space. Reframing full-screen down
to a square or a four-by-five almost never costs you anything, because
you're cropping into a taller canvas with room to spare.

One more reason to get this right at generation time: Section 4.5's
caption and CTA placement was tuned to a full-screen frame. Crop it down
after the fact and captions can drift relative to the new edges, exactly
the kind of gap a muted-watch test on the wrong export would catch too
late.

## Safe zones on a full-screen placement

```text
STORIES / REELS, 9:16 FRAME

Top ~14%     Covered by the profile bar and mute icon
Middle       Your actual creative lives here
Bottom ~14%  Covered by the caption line and the CTA button

Rule: keep your hook text and your CTA text
inside the middle band, not flush to either edge.
```

@narrate
Here's the part that catches people who cut a full-screen ad without
checking it against the actual interface first.

The top and bottom of a Stories or Reels frame aren't fully yours. Meta's
own profile bar, mute icon, caption line and CTA button sit over roughly
the top and bottom seventh of the screen each. Put your hook text at the
very top of frame, the way you might on a plain canvas, and the platform
covers it before a viewer ever reads it.

Check this the same way you checked a caption in Section 4.5: play the
actual export inside the actual platform, not just inside your edit
tool's preview window. An edit tool has no idea where Meta's interface is
going to sit on top of your creative.

Where does this bite hardest? The CTA. You spent Section 4.5 making sure
"Harlan, link in bio" stayed on screen long enough to read. Put that same
line flush against the bottom edge of a full-screen export, and Meta's own
CTA button sits directly on top of it, unread, for the entire time it's
on screen. The line was designed correctly. The frame it landed in wasn't
checked against the platform that was actually going to show it.

kicker: What actually converts here
## Raw and native beats polished, most of the time

::: cols
:: card good
### What tends to work
- UGC-style, avatar or filmed, feels native to the feed it's in
- Hook and product visible in the opening frame, sound off
- Ten to twenty creative variants running, not one polished hero ad
:: card bad
### What tends to underperform
- Studio-polished creative that reads as an ad before the hook lands
- A single "best" creative asked to carry the whole campaign
- Landscape or centred crops that lose the top and bottom of frame
:::

@narrate
As of this recording, the pattern holds across most of what's published
about Meta performance: creative that looks native to the feed it's
sitting in tends to outperform creative that announces itself as an ad on
sight. That's the exact case for everything you built in Section 4. An
avatar-led, sound-off-designed ad isn't a compromise on Meta. It's closer
to what the platform already rewards.

The other half of that pattern is volume, not polish. Meta's own
automated delivery tools, grouped under the Advantage+ name, are built to
test a real spread of creative variants against each other and shift
spend toward whichever ones actually perform. Feed them one hero ad and
you've given the system nothing to compare. Feed them the eight to twenty
variants a real angle map produces, and you've given it something to
actually learn from.

Notice how directly this connects back to Section 1.4's volume maths.
Advantage+ isn't a separate skill from what you've already learned. It's
the same argument, one video can't reveal a winner, applied inside Meta's
own delivery system instead of inside your own testing architecture. The
system doesn't reward the one ad you're proudest of. It rewards whichever
one earns its keep against the others you gave it to compare against.

@narrate
One honest note before this goes any further.

Everything in this lecture, the ratios, the safe zones, the variant
counts, is accurate as checked at the time of writing. Meta reshapes its
own placements more often than most advertisers notice, because most
advertisers aren't checking. Five minutes in your own ads manager before
a campaign launches costs you nothing. Finding out mid-flight that a
safe zone moved costs you a whole batch of creative that's already live.

Ask yourself the same question this course keeps asking about every tool
it names: what would actually break if this specific detail changed
tomorrow? A ratio shift breaks an export setting, fixable in minutes once
you notice it. It doesn't break the underlying discipline, generate in
full-screen first, check the actual platform before you trust a preview,
give the delivery system a real spread to test. That discipline is what
this lecture is actually teaching. The numbers are just today's version
of it.
