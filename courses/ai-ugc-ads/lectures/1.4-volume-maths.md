@slide statement
kicker: Section 1 · Sam almost generated four
## Sam typed "four variants" into the brief, out of habit. Dana asked if four was actually enough.
lead: Same brief, same generator. The only thing that changed is how many you made.

@narrate
Sam was about to run Harlan's next batch the old way, out of habit: four
variants, the same number a shoot would have given him, because four felt
like plenty. Dana asked one question before he hit generate: is four
actually enough? Neither of them had an answer that wasn't a guess, so
that's what this lecture turns "generate more variants" into: an actual
number, because as advice goes, "more" is useless. More than what? Enough
for what?

Here's the honest answer, worked out properly rather than guessed at. If a
real winner shows up roughly once in every twelve variants, the illustrative
rate from Section zero, then generating only four of them gives you worse
odds than a coin flip of catching it at all.

That should bother you a little, because four variants is roughly what a
traditional shoot gets you, and it's exactly the batch size most accounts
were running before any of this got cheap. The old constraint wasn't just
expensive. It was, without anyone realising it, statistically unlikely to
work.

@slide math
kicker: The one formula this lecture needs
## The probability of catching at least one winner

$P(\text{at least one winner}) = 1 - (1-p)^n$

@narrate
If you want the mechanism behind this number, here it is, once. "P" is the
hit rate: how often a variant turns out to be a real winner. Say it's about
one in twelve, the illustrative rate from earlier in this section. "N" is
how many variants you generate. The formula says your chance of catching at
least one winner is one, minus the chance you miss every single time, and
because each extra variant multiplies that miss chance down, a modest
increase in batch size buys a surprisingly large jump in your actual odds.

If you don't want the mechanism, skip straight to the number that actually
matters, on the next slide. Twenty variants gets you to roughly four-to-one
odds of finding a winner. That's the decision this whole formula is
standing in for, and it's the only part you need to carry forward.

@slide table
kicker: What the maths actually says
## Chance of finding at least one real winner, at a one-in-twelve hit rate

| Variants generated | Chance of catching a real winner |
| --- | --- |
| 4 | About 29% |
| 8 | About 50% |
| 12 | About 65% |
| 20 | About 82% |
| 40 | About 97% |

@narrate
Read this table slowly, because every row is a real decision someone makes.

At four variants, a single shoot's worth, you're below a coin flip. At
eight, you're roughly even odds. At twelve, you're better than two in three.
At twenty, you're past four in five. Notice what happens after that: forty
variants, double the batch, only buys you another fifteen points. Most of
the value is already banked by twenty.

Read the gap between the first three rows and the last two again. Going from
four to twelve variants, a threefold increase, takes you from a losing bet to
a comfortably good one. Going from twenty to forty, also a doubling, buys you
almost nothing by comparison. The curve is steep early and flat late, and
knowing where the flat part starts is the actual skill this lecture is
teaching.

@slide two-col
kicker: The two mistakes this table prevents
## Too few, and too many, for the same underlying reason

::: cols
:: card bad
### Generating too few
- Four or five variants, "to keep it manageable"
- Worse than a coin flip of finding the winner
- A failed test gets blamed on the product, not the sample size
:: card good
### Generating past the point of return
- Forty, fifty variants, chasing certainty
- Small further gain for real extra media spend
- Money better spent testing the winner harder, not finding a second one
:::

@narrate
This table actually prevents two separate mistakes, and they cost you in
opposite directions.

Too few variants, and you'll conclude a perfectly good product or angle
"doesn't work on paid social", when the honest conclusion is that you never
gave the test a fair chance to find its winner. Too many, chasing the last
few points of certainty, and you're spending real testing budget for
almost no extra chance of a better outcome, money that would do more good
scaling the winner you already found.

Both mistakes get blamed on the wrong thing, too. A too-small batch that
finds nothing gets written up as "AI creative doesn't work for us." A
too-large batch that finds a winner gets written up as thoroughness paying
off, when really it just spent extra money to arrive at roughly the answer
twenty variants would already have given.

@slide statement
kicker: The number to actually use
class: center
## Somewhere around twenty is the sweet spot for most real briefs.
lead: Enough to make the tail show up. Not so much that you're paying for certainty you don't need.

@narrate
So here's the number worth carrying forward: somewhere around twenty.

You'll see that exact batch size again more than once in this course, and
it's not a coincidence. It's the smallest number that gets you comfortably
past four-in-five odds without tipping into the flat part of the curve
where extra variants stop earning their keep.

It's not a universal constant, either. If your product sits in a more
forgiving category, or the hit rate on your account genuinely runs higher
than one in twelve, the same table gets you to a good number faster. What
matters is the method, not the specific twenty: work out roughly how often
you find a real winner, then read the table until the curve goes flat.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
This maths assumes your brief was wide enough to actually cover twenty real
angles. ==Twenty near-identical variants don't behave like twenty
independent shots== at the tail. That's what Section two is for.
:::

@narrate
One honest limit before you take this number and run with it.

The formula assumes each variant is a genuinely separate shot at finding the
tail. If your twenty variants are really four ideas with five colour swaps
each, you don't have twenty shots, you have four, and the odds in that table
collapse back down to the losing end. Volume only works if it's covering
real ground, and covering real ground is exactly what the brief in Section
two exists to guarantee.

This is also the honest answer to "why not just generate two hundred
variants and be done with it". Past a certain point, you're not adding new
angles, you're adding near-duplicates of ones you already tried, and
near-duplicates don't behave like independent shots no matter how the
maths on the formula slide would like to treat them. Coverage, not raw
count, is what the number twenty is actually standing in for.
