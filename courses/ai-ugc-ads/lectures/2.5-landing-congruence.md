## Sam Clicked His Own Ad, on His Phone, the Way a Stranger Would.
lead: What he landed on was technically Harlan's site. It didn't feel like the same ad.

@narrate
Sam did something nobody on the team had done in weeks: he clicked Harlan's
best-performing ad on his own phone, the way a stranger scrolling past it
would. The ad read "it's hour eight and your feet finally aren't
screaming." The page it opened on read "quality socks for everyone."
Nothing about the click was broken. It just didn't feel like the same
promise anymore.

One more document before the workshop, and it's the one people skip because
it isn't technically an ad at all. You can build a perfect product truth
document, a sharp hook matrix, and twenty genuinely distinct angles, and
still watch a real winner lose, because the page it lands on breaks the
promise the ad just made. That gap has a name, and it's worth understanding
before Section seven's test tells you an angle failed when the angle was
actually fine.

It's called congruence: whether the page keeps talking about the exact
thing that made someone click. It's the part of the funnel that belongs to
nobody in particular. The person writing the ad usually isn't the person
who owns the landing page, so the seam between them is exactly where things
quietly fall apart without anyone deciding they should.

@slide two-col
kicker: The gap, made concrete
## Same click, two different landing experiences

::: cols
:: card bad
### Broken congruence
- Ad: "It's hour eight and your feet finally aren't screaming"
- Page: generic homepage, "Quality socks for everyone"
- No mention of shifts, hours, or the feeling that got the click
:: card good
### Matched congruence
- Ad: "It's hour eight and your feet finally aren't screaming"
- Page headline: "Built for the second half of a long shift"
- Same feeling, same language, continued without a seam
:::

@narrate
Here's the gap, made concrete with one real click.

Someone stops scrolling because "hour eight" named something they actually
feel. They click, expecting the page to keep talking about that. Instead
they land on a homepage that's technically about the same product but
speaks a completely different, generic language. That half-second of "wait,
is this the right place" is where a winning angle quietly becomes a losing
ad, and nothing about the creative itself was ever the problem.

This is easy to underestimate because nothing about it looks broken. The
page loads fine, the product's right there, the price is correct. The
failure is entirely about continuity of feeling, and that kind of failure
doesn't throw an error. It just quietly loses the sale and leaves a number
in a report that looks like the ad's fault.

That's exactly the report Sam almost acted on before he clicked the ad
himself. The dashboard said the angle underperformed. It never said why,
and "why" turned out to have nothing to do with the angle at all.

@slide table
kicker: What actually has to match
## The congruence checklist

| Element | What breaks if it doesn't match |
| --- | --- |
| Headline | The visitor re-reads to check they're in the right place |
| Hero image | The visual promise from the ad isn't continued |
| Offer or price | A mismatch here reads as bait and switch |
| Proof point | The exact claim that earned the click goes unmentioned |

@narrate
Four things actually have to carry through, and none of them require a
rebuild of the whole site.

The headline should echo the ad's language closely enough that a visitor
doesn't have to re-read to check they're in the right place. The hero
image should continue the visual promise, not switch to something generic.
The offer and price need to match exactly, because any gap there reads as
bait and switch even when it's an honest mistake. And whatever proof point
earned the click, the spec, the review, the number, needs to actually
appear on the page, not just live in the ad.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
Sometimes the fix isn't copy. ==It's a landing page the media buyer doesn't
own or can't rebuild in time==. Say so plainly rather than blaming the ad
for a page problem.
:::

@narrate
One honest limit, because this isn't always a five-minute fix.

Sometimes the real problem is a landing page that belongs to someone else,
built for a different purpose, that nobody has the time or authority to
rebuild before the test needs to run. When that's true, say so plainly.
Blaming the ad, or worse, killing a genuinely good angle because the page
undersold it, is a worse outcome than admitting the fix is a page problem
you can't solve this week.

There's a smaller version of this fix that's almost always available even
when a full rebuild isn't: a dedicated section, or even a single line, that
speaks the ad's exact language, added above whatever generic page already
exists. It's not elegant, and it's not the real solution. It's often enough
to close the worst of the gap without waiting on a project nobody's
scheduled yet.

That's the version Sam actually shipped after his own phone test: one new
line at the top of the homepage, echoing "hour eight" word for word,
nothing else on the page touched. It cost him twenty minutes, not a
rebuild.

## A losing test result and a losing angle are not always the same thing.
lead: Check congruence before you write an angle off for good.

@narrate
Here's why this lecture earns its place before the workshop rather than
after it.

When Section eight teaches you to read a result honestly, "winner, loser,
or noise" assumes the ad and the page were telling the same story. If
they weren't, a loss might be telling you about the page, not the angle.
Before you retire an angle for good, it's worth asking one question first:
did it actually get a fair, congruent landing experience, or did it lose to
a mismatch nobody checked for?

Add this check to whatever kill rule you build in Section seven, right
alongside the spend threshold. It costs thirty seconds per angle before a
test launches, and it's cheaper than every one of those thirty seconds
spent re-litigating a false negative after the spend is already gone.

Make it a habit rather than a memory. A checklist you have to remember to
run gets skipped on the one week it would have mattered most, which is
usually the busiest week, not the calmest one.
