## Twenty angles, built from three questions asked five times

::: split
:: main
Sam built his first angle map for Harlan in eleven minutes flat and hit
twenty rows. Reading it back the next morning, two of the rows in the
middle turned out to be the same idea wearing different sentences. He
hadn't noticed until he read them side by side.
:: aside Why hands-on
An angle map you watch someone else build teaches the idea. One you build
yourself teaches the twenty-minute habit you'll repeat for every product.
:::

@narrate
Sam's fast first draft is the mistake this lecture exists to prevent, and
it's worth knowing before you start yours.

The angle map is the document that turns twenty from a number in Section
one into a real list of twenty genuinely different bets, not twenty
confident restatements of the first idea that came to mind. Pull up the
product truth doc and the hook matrix from the last two lectures if you
have them, because this works better done alongside me than watched.

If you don't have them open, pause and get them now. What follows only
sticks if you're actually filling in rows as we go, not nodding along and
planning to do it later. Later rarely happens, and a half-built angle map
is worse than none, because it looks finished without being useful: the
exact trap Sam walked into.

@slide code
kicker: The method
## Three questions, asked once per rung

```text
For each awareness level, ask:

1. What does THIS person specifically feel or believe right now?
2. Which hook pattern from the matrix actually fits that?
3. What's the ONE proof point from the product truth doc
   that backs it up?

Five rungs. Answer all three, twice per rung. Ten angles.
Answer them a different, honest way a second time. Twenty.
```

@narrate
Here's the actual method, and it's less mysterious than "twenty testable
angles" sounds.

For each of the five awareness rungs, you ask three questions: what does
this specific person feel right now, which hook pattern from last lecture's
matrix actually fits that feeling, and which one proof point from the
product truth document backs it up. Answer that twice per rung, honestly,
in two genuinely different ways, and five rungs times two angles times two
passes gets you to twenty.

Notice the arithmetic is doing something specific: it forces you through
every rung, every time, rather than letting you write ten angles for the
rung that's easiest to write for and calling it done. That discipline is
the entire value of the method. Anyone can generate twenty variants on the
one feeling they thought of first.

It's also why Sam's eleven-minute draft failed. He answered the three
questions once, fast, then reworded the same answer nineteen more times
instead of going back through the rungs a genuinely second way. The method
only works if the second pass is honestly different from the first, not a
faster version of it.

## Six of the twenty, to show the range

| Angle | Rung | Core feeling it interrupts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hour eight | Unaware | The ache hasn't been named yet |
| Twelve-hour shift | Problem-aware | "Is this just normal now?" |
| The 15-20 spec | Solution-aware | "Do compression socks even work?" |
| Not just for flights | Solution-aware | "That's not for people like me" |
| A nurse's honest review | Product-aware | "Is this brand actually legit?" |
| Cost of a bad pair | Product-aware | "Is this worth the money?" |

@narrate
Here are six of the twenty, and notice they're not six versions of one
idea wearing different outfits.

Hour eight and twelve-hour shift both sit low on the ladder, but interrupt
different feelings: one is physical, one is a creeping normalisation the
audience hasn't questioned yet. The 15-20 spec and not-just-for-flights sit
on the same rung and answer two completely different doubts. That's the
actual test for whether your twenty angles are real: could you swap two of
them and lose something specific, or would nobody notice?

Run that test on the two product-aware rows too. "A nurse's honest review"
is answering a trust question. "Cost of a bad pair" is answering a value
question. Someone hesitating over Harlan for either reason exists, and
they're not the same person, which is exactly why one row for
"product-aware, but persuasive" would have quietly served neither of them well.

kicker: The trap from Section one
## A real angle versus a reskin

::: cols
:: card bad
### A reskin
- Same core feeling, new adjective
- Same proof point, reworded
- Same rung, no new question answered
:: card good
### A real angle
- A genuinely different feeling or doubt
- A different proof point doing the work
- Earns its own row on the map, not a copy of one
:::

@narrate
Remember the warning from the volume maths lecture: twenty near-identical
variants don't behave like twenty independent shots. This is where that
warning gets concrete.

A reskin swaps an adjective and calls it a new angle. A real angle answers
a different question entirely, even if it lands on the same rung as
another one. If you can't say, in one sentence, what specific doubt or
feeling an angle is aimed at that the others aren't, it's not a new angle.
It's a reskin wearing a different hook, exactly what Sam found buried in
his own first draft at the start of this lecture.

Here's a quick check you can run on your own map once it's built: read
just the middle column, the core feeling each row interrupts, top to
bottom, with the angle names covered. If two rows read as the same
sentence with different words, you've found a reskin, and it's worth
replacing before you generate a single static from it.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
A good angle map guarantees twenty genuinely different bets. ==It does not
guarantee twenty good ones==. That's what the pre-flight check and the test
are for, not this document.
:::

@narrate
One honest limit before you treat this map as finished work.

Building a real angle map is real craft, and it's tempting to feel done
once you've hit twenty rows that all look distinct. Distinct isn't the same
as good. Some of these twenty will be flatly wrong for Harlan, and that's
fine. The map's job is coverage, not quality control. Quality control
starts in Section six, and the test in Section seven is the only thing
that actually tells you which of these twenty was worth the afternoon.

If that feels like a lot of trust to place in twenty rows on a page, that's
the right instinct. It is a lot of trust, and it's exactly the amount the
maths in Section one said was reasonable. Not more, chasing certainty the
method can't give you. Not less, and quietly recreating the four-variant
problem this whole section exists to fix.
