## Meta rewards a native-feeling ad. TikTok rewards an ad that isn't trying to be an ad at all.
lead: Same product, same claims boundary, a genuinely different grammar for what "wins" here.

@narrate
Section 5.1 asked you to check ratios and safe zones. TikTok asks
something harder: whether your ad reads as a TikTok in the first place,
before anyone gets far enough to judge the hook.

As of this recording, native, creator-style content consistently
outperforms studio-polished footage here, for a specific reason:
TikTok's audience has a trained reflex for skipping anything that
announces itself as an ad in the first frame. A cut that looks like an
ordinary post survives that reflex. A cut that looks like a commercial
doesn't.

This isn't the same lesson as Meta's, restated. Meta rewarded native
framing as one factor among several. On TikTok, it's the whole game.

What actually earns that pass past the skip reflex? Not sloppiness. The
best-performing native content is casual in look, not in construction:
a real hook line in the first second, legible on-screen text sitting
inside the frame's safe zone, clear audio, a specific call to action. The
difference from a studio ad isn't less craft. It's craft that hides
itself behind the look of something a viewer wasn't paid to make.

That's good news for the workflow you already built. An avatar-led,
sound-off-designed ad from Section 4.5 was never trying to look like a
commercial. TikTok doesn't ask you to build something new. It asks you to
stop softening the one you've already got.

kicker: The format that actually leans into this
## Spark Ads: boosting a real post, not uploading a new ad

```text
WHAT A SPARK AD IS
An ad built from an existing organic TikTok post,
yours or a creator's, keeping the original account
name, sound, caption, and engagement counts intact.

WHY IT WORKS DIFFERENTLY
Appears in the feed exactly as it would organically.
A post with real organic engagement gets matched to
similar viewers faster than a cold, freshly uploaded ad.
```

@narrate
Here's the format built specifically around the grammar this lecture just
named. A Spark Ad isn't a new upload dressed as an ad. It's an existing
post, boosted, keeping every social signal that made it look like a real
post in the first place: the account name, the sound, the likes and
comments already sitting on it.

That matters mechanically, not just visually. As of this recording,
TikTok's delivery reads engagement on the underlying post as a signal.
An ad built from a post that already earned real engagement organically
gets matched to similar viewers faster than an ad starting cold with zero
history behind it.

Notice what this changes about how you'd actually use your Section 4
workflow here. On Meta, you generate a batch, upload it, and let the
delivery system sort winners from losers. On TikTok, the strongest move
is often to post an angle organically first, let it earn real engagement
on its own, and only then spend behind whichever one actually took off.
The generation stays the same. The path from generated clip to paid
spend doesn't.

That's not a small difference. Your posting calendar becomes part of
your testing architecture here in a way it never was on Meta. An angle
ignored organically is telling you something before you've spent a cent
finding that out through paid delivery instead.

## The creator authorization flow

| Step | Who does it | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Post exists | Creator or your own brand account | Organic post, already published |
| Generate a code | Creator, from the post's ad settings | A time-limited authorization code |
| Enter the code | You, in Ads Manager | The post becomes usable as paid creative |
| Code expires | Automatic | Typically 30 to 60 days, confirm on your account |

@narrate
This is the part that catches people who've only ever run Meta ads,
where you simply upload a file. Spark Ads need permission, not just
footage.

A creator generates their own authorization code from inside the app,
tied to that specific post, for a limited window. You enter that code in
Ads Manager, and only then can the post run as paid media under your
budget. Miss the expiry and the code stops working, mid-flight, whether
or not the ad is still performing.

Two practical habits follow directly from that. First, track expiry
dates the same way you'd track a licence tier from Section 4.1, in the
same file as the brief, not in memory. Second, when you're using your own
brand account rather than an outside creator's, you're both the poster
and the authoriser, which simplifies the flow but doesn't remove the
step. The code still has to be generated and entered, even when you're
authorising your own post to yourself.

## The same brief, two different winners

::: cols
:: card good
### What wins on TikTok
- The raw "12-hour shift" cut, almost no framing
- Feels like a real nurse talking, not an ad about socks
:: card bad
### What wins on Meta instead
- The clinical-authority framing: ex-ER nurse, the compression spec
- Credentialed, specific, closer to a claim than a moment
:::

@narrate
This is exactly where Harlan's own set stopped behaving the same way on
both platforms. The clinical framing that performed on Meta, credentials
and a spec upfront, reads as slightly too polished here. The raw,
unframed cut wins instead, the one that plays like a real shift ending
rather than a brand making a case for itself.

Why would the same claim, the same product, split like that? Because
Meta's feed still tolerates a moment that reads as an advertisement, if
it's a good one. TikTok's doesn't tolerate it nearly as well. The
platform didn't change what's true about the product. It changed which
true thing lands as a real post instead of a pitch.

Notice what didn't change: the claims boundary underneath both cuts. The
raw version doesn't say anything the credentialed version couldn't
defend, it just says less of it, and lets the moment carry what the
credentials would otherwise have to state outright. That's worth holding
onto going into Section six. A cut that reads as more casual isn't
automatically a looser cut, claims-wise. It can be the exact same claim,
delivered with less scaffolding around it.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest pattern, verified

::: callout Be straight about this
As of this recording, TikTok requires a visible label or disclosure on
ad creative that's AI-generated or significantly AI-edited. ==This isn't
optional flair. Confirm your current obligation before every campaign,
and see Section 6.3 for how to build it in properly==.
:::

@narrate
One honest note that applies to almost everything you've built since
Section three.

TikTok's policy on AI-generated and AI-edited content requires a clear
label or disclosure on ad creative, not just organic posts. That covers
generated avatars, generated statics, and anything significantly altered
by AI, which is most of what this course teaches you to make. Section 6.3
builds this in properly. For now, just hold the fact: disclosure on
TikTok isn't a nice-to-have layered on top of a finished ad. It's part of
what makes the ad allowed to run at all.

Think about why this rule exists on this specific platform, more than
somewhere your ad announces itself as a commercial anyway. TikTok's whole
grammar is built on ads that pass as real posts. A platform that rewards
that illusion has an obvious reason to require the one honest signal
that tells a viewer what they're actually looking at. The disclosure
requirement and the native-grammar advantage aren't in tension. The
second one is exactly why the first one matters here more than most
places.
