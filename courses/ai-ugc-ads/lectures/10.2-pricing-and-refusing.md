@slide statement
kicker: Section 10 · Pricing
## Price the sprint, not the hours. The moment you price by the hour, the incentive to run it properly quietly disappears.
lead: What you charge for matters as much as how much. Get the unit wrong and the whole relationship pulls against the system you built.

@narrate
Here's a trap specific to this kind of work. Price by the hour, or by
the individual asset, and you've just built an incentive to generate
more and test less, exactly backwards from Section 9.2's economics.
Generation is cheap. The value is in the discipline around it, and
hourly pricing charges for the wrong half of the work.

Price the sprint itself instead, the whole five-step rhythm from
Section 9.1, as one unit. That aligns what you're paid for with what
actually matters: running the discipline every week, not producing the
largest possible pile of creative.

This isn't about charging more or less. It's about charging for the
right thing, so the incentive built into the price agrees with the
incentive built into the system, instead of quietly working against it.

Ask yourself what a prospective client is actually paying for, honestly,
before you decide how to price it. If the honest answer is "the weekly
discipline that finds a winner and protects it," price that directly.
If the honest answer is still "a pile of video files," you haven't
finished explaining the system yet, and that's worth fixing before the
pricing conversation, not during it.

@slide table
kicker: Three ways to price it
## What each one actually rewards

| Model | What it rewards | The risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| By the hour or asset | Volume of output | Pressure to skip the discipline to hit a number |
| A flat weekly or monthly retainer | Running the full sprint, consistently | Needs a client who trusts the process, not just outcomes |
| A share of media spend managed | Scaling what's already proven to work | Only fair once a real testing architecture exists to scale |

@narrate
The retainer row is the closest match to what Section 9.1 actually
built. You're not selling creative pieces, you're selling the weekly
rhythm that finds and protects a winner, and a retainer prices exactly
that, regardless of whether a given week produces one dramatic result
or five quiet ones.

The third row deserves a caution the table doesn't fully carry. Pricing
against media spend only makes sense once Section 7's testing
architecture is actually in place and the CAC ceiling is real, not
guessed at. Offering that structure before the testing architecture
exists ties your income to a number nobody's actually earned control
over yet.

Whichever model you choose, resist blending it with per-asset pricing
on the side. A retainer with an hourly add-on for "extra" creative
quietly reintroduces the exact incentive problem the retainer was
supposed to solve.

This is also where Section 9.3's ownership question comes back around.
A solo operator pricing a retainer is pricing their own fifteen hours,
honestly, against what those hours actually protect. An agency pricing
the same retainer across a pod of people needs the same honesty, just
spread across more salaries. The unit changes. The underlying question,
what is this actually worth to protect, doesn't.

@slide code
kicker: A simple structure to start from
## Two components, not one

```text
COMPONENT ONE: the sprint retainer
Covers Section 9.1's full weekly rhythm, scoring through
reporting, regardless of how many ad sets are live.

COMPONENT TWO: a scale trigger, agreed in advance
Once a winner clears Section 8.2's tree and gets scaled per
Section 9's playbook, pricing shifts to reflect the larger
media budget now being protected.
```

@narrate
Two components, and notice the second only activates once a winner is
real, not hoped for. That sequencing matters. Pricing for scale before
anything has actually scaled is pricing against a result you can't yet
promise, the same mistake Section 10.1 already warned against making
in the pitch itself.

Agree on the scale trigger's terms before you need them, in writing,
during the same conversation that sets the retainer. Negotiating a
price increase in the middle of a winning streak is an awkward
conversation for everyone involved. Agreeing to the terms while nothing
dramatic is happening yet is a much easier one.

Why does the order matter this much? Because a scale trigger negotiated
after a winner already exists puts both sides in an unfair spot. The
client feels pressured to accept a higher number to keep momentum
going. You feel pressured to accept whatever they'll agree to, rather
than what the larger media budget genuinely justifies protecting.
Deciding the terms before either pressure exists protects the
relationship as much as the price.

@slide callout
kicker: What to refuse

::: callout Be straight about this
A client who wants a guaranteed result, or wants Section 6's pre-flight
checklist skipped to move faster, isn't asking for a faster version of
this system. ==They're asking for a different, worse one, and taking
the work means your name is on what it produces==.
:::

@narrate
Two requests are worth refusing outright, not negotiating down.

A guaranteed result asks you to promise something Section 8's entire
tree exists to prove nobody can promise honestly. Taking that deal
means either lying to win the client, or quietly padding results later
to look like you kept a promise you never should have made.

Skipping pre-flight to launch faster asks you to remove the exact check
that catches an overreaching claim or a licence gap before it spends
real money. Section 6 built that gate for a reason that doesn't go away
just because a client's in a hurry. Refusing this one is also the
easier of the two conversations: it's not your judgement being
overruled, it's the platform's own compliance risk being ignored on the
client's behalf.

Both refusals get easier with practice, and both get harder to hold if
you've already accepted the client's money before the request arrives.
Name these two boundaries in the same conversation as the pricing
itself, before any retainer is signed, so refusing later isn't a
surprise to either side.

@narrate
Next lecture builds the portfolio that makes this pricing conversation
credible in the first place, using exactly the reports Section 9.5
already taught you to write.

Before that, write down your own version of the two refusals from this
lecture, in your own words. Having them ready before a client asks is
the difference between refusing calmly and getting talked out of a
boundary you never actually decided on in advance.

Would either refusal actually cost you the client? Maybe, on a bad day
with the wrong prospect. Weigh that honestly against what saying yes
would cost you instead: a promise you can't keep, or your name attached
to a claim Section 6 would have caught. Most clients worth keeping
respect the boundary once you explain it clearly, and the ones who
don't were never going to be an easy relationship regardless.
