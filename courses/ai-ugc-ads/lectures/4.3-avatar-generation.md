## From script to generated clip

```text
BEFORE GENERATING
1. Avatar's licence tier: commercial, confirmed, not assumed
2. Script: word-budget checked, read aloud with a timer
3. Delivery style: matched to the hook, not generic enthusiasm

GENERATE
Script + avatar + delivery style -> rendered clip

AFTER GENERATING
4. Watch the full clip before judging from a thumbnail
5. Check pacing against the script's timing marks
```

@narrate
Here's the actual sequence, and notice three of five steps happen before
you generate anything at all.

Licence tier first, confirmed rather than assumed, because that's the
exact gap that caught Harlan's first draft. The script, word-budget
checked and read aloud, from last lecture. And a delivery style that
matches the hook: the "hour eleven" line needs to land tired and relieved,
not upbeat, or the performance undercuts the exact words doing the work.

Ask yourself, before you hit generate: does the delivery style you've
chosen match the feeling the script is actually going for? A mismatch
here is invisible in the settings panel and obvious the moment you press
play.

@slide table
kicker: What to check on the result
## The clip isn't done just because it rendered

| Check | What you're looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Lip-sync accuracy | Does the mouth match the audio at normal speed, not just paused |
| Pacing against script marks | Does it land the CTA inside the format's actual time budget |
| Delivery tone | Does it match the hook, not a generic upbeat default |
| Full watch, not a thumbnail | Problems in the middle third are the ones a preview frame misses |

@narrate
A clip that renders successfully isn't automatically a clip that's ready.
Four checks, every time, before it goes anywhere near a test.

Lip-sync can drift subtly enough to miss on a glance and read as wrong on
a full watch. Pacing can run long even from a word-budget-checked script,
because delivery speed varies by avatar. Tone can default to a generic
upbeat register that fights the actual words. And problems love to hide in
the middle third of a clip, which is exactly the part a thumbnail preview
never shows you.

Give yourself the full fifteen or thirty seconds, every time, before you
sign off on a clip. Skimming to save a minute here is how a pacing problem
survives all the way to a real test, where it costs a great deal more than
a minute to notice.

## Real progress, with a real limit

::: cols
:: card good
### What's genuinely solved
- A studio, a crew, a shoot day: all gone
- Multiple delivery takes in minutes, not a reshoot
- Properly licensed, paid-social-ready output by default
:: card bad
### What still needs a human
- Judging whether the delivery actually lands
- Catching pacing drift a script alone can't predict
- The claims boundary check, still Section six's job
:::

@narrate
It's worth being honest about how much this actually replaced, in both
directions.

A shoot day, a studio, a crew: genuinely gone, and that's real. What
didn't go away is judgement. Something still has to decide whether a
delivery actually lands, catch pacing drift the script alone couldn't
predict, and check every line against the claims boundary from Section
2.1. Generation replaced production. It didn't replace the checking.

That's worth saying plainly, because it's the opposite of how this
technology usually gets sold. The pitch is "video ads without the work."
The honest version is "video ads without the shoot, with a different kind
of work in its place." Less expensive work, and much faster. Still work.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
The same script and avatar can generate a flat take and a genuinely good
one back to back. ==Regenerate rather than settle==. A weak delivery of a
strong script is a worse ad than either flaw alone would be.
:::

@narrate
One honest limit before this goes any further.

Performance quality varies between generations of the exact same script
and avatar, sometimes noticeably. If the first take feels flat, regenerate
rather than ship it. A strong script delivered flatly reads worse than
either problem on its own, because it promises energy the delivery doesn't
back up.
