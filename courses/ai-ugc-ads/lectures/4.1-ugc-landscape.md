## Sam had Harlan's first avatar ad rendered and ready to upload. Then he found the line that said "personal use only."
lead: Not a design choice. A rights question, and it comes before the script, before anything.

@narrate
Sam had the clip open, ready to schedule. It looked finished: the avatar
(an AI-generated on-screen presenter, standing in for an actor) delivered
the line well, nothing suggested a problem. Then he scrolled the tool's
pricing page out of habit and found it. The plan he'd generated on
covered personal, organic posts only. Paid campaigns needed a separate
commercial licence tier, meaning what your plan actually permits you to
do with the footage, and this draft was never covered by it.

He hadn't been careless. Everything up to now generated a product, a
scene, a colour, nothing that needed consent, because none of it was a
person. An avatar is different: a real person licensed their likeness, or
a synthetic performer stands in for one, and what you're allowed to do
with it depends on which.

So what actually changes about how you work? One habit, the exact one
Sam had just skipped: check rights first, generate second, every time.
That runs against how these tools are built. A generator renders a
convincing clip whether or not the licence behind the face covers your
plan, and a rendered clip gives no visual sign anything's wrong. That's
why it lands on you, not the platform. Meta and TikTok police their own
policy, not your avatar tool's terms.

## Three kinds of avatar, three different rights pictures

| Kind | What's behind it | Rights question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stock avatar | A real actor who licensed their likeness to the platform | Does your plan's tier actually cover paid social use? |
| Custom avatar | A specific person: a founder, a real creator | Do you have their documented, specific consent? |
| Synthetic avatar | No real person at all | Usually the simplest rights picture, still needs disclosure |

@narrate
Here's the landscape as it actually stands, in three categories rather
than a list of brand names that will be out of date within months.

A stock avatar is a real actor's likeness, licensed to whichever platform
you're using. A custom avatar clones a specific real person, a founder or
a real creator, and needs their documented consent, not just a general
terms-of-service click-through. A synthetic avatar has no real person
behind it at all, which is usually the simplest rights picture, though it
still needs the same disclosure you'll build properly in Section 6.3.

Notice all three still end in the same place: some form of disclosure,
some form of documentation. Nothing in this landscape lets you skip
paperwork entirely. It only changes whose paperwork it is.

Most people choosing between the three ask the wrong first question,
which is which one looks best. Ask instead which rights picture you're
actually prepared to manage. A stock avatar means checking a licence tier
before every campaign. A custom avatar means holding someone's documented
consent and being ready to produce it. A synthetic avatar means the
lightest paperwork of the three, but still real paperwork, not none. Pick
based on which obligation you'll actually keep up with, not which face you
like best in a demo reel.

## The licence-tier gap

```text
DRAFT ONE
Stock avatar from a library's entry-level plan.
Video generated, looked right, ready to test.

THE CATCH
Entry-level plan: personal and organic use only.
Paid social campaigns require the commercial tier,
a separate licence that draft one was never covered by.

RESULT
Caught before a dollar of spend. Recast on the
commercial tier. Same avatar, same script, cleared.
```

@narrate
Here's what actually happened on Harlan's account, and it's a genuinely
easy mistake to make, not a careless one.

Sam generated a strong first draft on a library's entry-level plan. The
video looked right, the performance was convincing, and nothing about
watching it back suggested a problem. The problem was in the terms: that
tier licensed the avatar for personal and organic use, not paid campaigns.
Paid social needed a separate commercial tier, one draft one was never
actually covered by. It got caught before any spend, and the fix was a
recast on the right tier, not a different avatar or a different script.

Notice what didn't need to change: the script, the avatar's appearance,
the whole creative direction. One line item in a pricing page was the
entire gap between a compliant ad and one that wasn't. That's usually how
this mistake looks in practice, small and easy to miss, not dramatic.

Sam's habit now: before any avatar clip goes near an ad account, he
screenshots the licence tier page and drops it in the brief folder. Not
distrust of the tool, just a refusal to repeat "I checked once, months
ago", the exact assumption that let draft one through the first time.

@narrate
I want to be precise about what's actually true here, checked at the
time of writing rather than assumed.

Tools genuinely differ on this. Some platforms ship commercial rights on
every plan, entry-level included. Others reserve paid, commercial use for
a higher tier, exactly the gap that caught Harlan's first draft. Neither
approach is hidden or unusual. It's just a detail that's easy to miss once,
expensive to miss on a live campaign, and worth checking fresh before
every single campaign, not just the first time you signed up for the tool.

Where do you actually go to check? The tool's own pricing or licence page,
not a support article someone linked you a year ago, and not a forum
thread. Pricing pages change more often than the tools themselves get
rebuilt, and a tier that was commercial-inclusive when you signed up can
be restructured later without your account automatically catching up.
Five minutes on the actual current page beats an assumption carried over
from whenever you first read it.

This is also why the course is built the way it is. Naming one tool's
exact terms here would be accurate for a few months and then quietly
wrong, which is a worse outcome than teaching you the check itself. The
check survives every pricing page rewrite a vendor ever ships.

@narrate
Licence tier isn't the only rights question, and it's worth naming the
other one before you leave this lecture.

If an avatar is meant to represent a real, specific person, a founder
using their own likeness, or a real customer, that needs documented
consent, not an assumption that it's fine because they agreed once. Some
platforms now ask for that consent to be uploaded directly, with a name, a
description of use, and a signature, not just kept in a filing cabinet.
Both threads, the licence and the likeness, get their full treatment in
Section six.

Think about why platforms have tightened this specifically for likeness
and voice, rather than for generated products or scenes. A generated
scene can't be defamed and doesn't have a reputation to protect. A real
person's face and voice, cloned convincingly enough to sell something,
absolutely can be, which is exactly the harm this documentation
requirement exists to prevent. It's not friction for its own sake. It's
the platform asking the same question you should already be asking
yourself: does this specific person actually know their likeness is
selling this specific product?
