@slide statement
kicker: Section 10 · The plan
## Everything this course taught is useless until it's sequenced. Doing all of it at once is the same as doing none of it well.
lead: Thirty days to build the machine. Sixty to run it honestly. Ninety to prove it, whether that's to a boss or a client.

@narrate
You've now got every piece: a brief system, a generation pipeline, a
pre-flight checklist, a testing architecture, an honest read, a weekly
sprint, and a pitch. That's a lot to hold at once, and trying to
stand up all of it in the first week is how a genuinely good system
collapses under its own ambition.

This lecture sequences it into three phases instead. Not because the
pieces have to happen in strict isolation, but because each one needs
the last one in place to actually work. A testing architecture built
before a cleared batch exists has nothing to test. A pitch built before
a single honest report exists has nothing to prove.

Ninety days is a real target, not an arbitrary round number. It's
roughly the time it takes to run several full sprint cycles, enough for
Section 9.1's rhythm to become a genuine habit rather than something
you're still consciously assembling week to week.

Why three phases specifically, rather than a longer list of smaller
milestones? Because each phase maps to a genuine dependency, not an
arbitrary checkpoint. You can't run honestly before you've built
properly, and you can't credibly prove anything before you've run it
for real. Collapsing the three into one rushed month skips straight
past the dependencies that make each phase actually mean something.

@slide table
kicker: Three phases
## What each thirty days is actually for

| Phase | Focus | What "done" looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Days 1-30 | Build: brief, generate, pre-flight, testing architecture | One cleared batch, live in an isolated ad-set structure |
| Days 31-60 | Run: score honestly, catch fatigue, keep the sprint going | Two or three full sprint cycles, reported per Section 9.5 |
| Days 61-90 | Prove: portfolio, pricing, the pitch, if you're taking this further | A real case study, built from your own reports, not a hypothetical |

@narrate
Notice phase one has no scoring in it at all, and that's deliberate.
Section 8's honest read needs real data to read, and real data needs a
properly built, isolated testing architecture running first. Rushing
into scoring before the structure exists just produces noise you can't
actually trust.

Phase two is where the real habit forms. One sprint cycle proves you
can run the system once. Two or three prove you can run it as a
rhythm, which is the entire difference Section 9 spent five lectures
building. Don't skip to phase three until this phase has actually
happened, not just been planned.

Phase three only applies if you're taking this toward outside work or
a bigger internal mandate. If you're running this purely for your own
account, days sixty-one through ninety are simply more sprint cycles,
the habit continuing to compound rather than a new phase starting.

Adjust the exact day counts to your own reality without abandoning the
order underneath them. A slower-moving account might need forty-five
days to reach a genuinely clean first batch, not thirty. That's fine.
What matters is that building still comes before running, and running
still comes before proving, whatever the calendar actually says.

@slide code
kicker: The checklist version
## What to actually have finished, by day

```text
BY DAY 30:
- Product truth doc, hook taxonomy, angle map (Sections 2)
- One cleared, pre-flighted batch (Sections 3, 4, 6)
- A live testing architecture, budgets and kill rules set (7)

BY DAY 60:
- At least two full sprint cycles run and reported (8, 9)
- A swipe file with real entries, not a placeholder (9.4)

BY DAY 90:
- A redacted case study from your own reports (10.3)
- A pitch or internal proposal, practiced out loud (10.1)
```

@narrate
Notice every line traces back to a specific section, not a vague
intention. That's on purpose. A thirty-sixty-ninety plan built from
generic goals drifts the moment real work gets busy. One built from
this course's actual artifacts has a concrete definition of done at
every checkpoint.

If you're behind this schedule, that's genuinely fine. The order
matters more than the pace. Someone who takes six months to reach day
ninety, in this sequence, is in a stronger position than someone who
rushed all three phases into three weeks and skipped straight to
pitching without ever running an honest cycle.

Keep this checklist somewhere you'll actually revisit it, the same
advice Section 9.4 already gave the swipe file. A plan checked once and
then forgotten is a plan in name only. Checked weekly, even briefly, it
does the actual job of telling you which phase you're honestly in
right now, not which phase you assumed you'd be in by this point.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap

::: callout Be straight about this
Pitching or reporting results before you've actually run a full sprint
cycle is tempting, especially under pressure to show progress fast.
==It also means selling a system you haven't yet proven works on real
data, which is the exact overclaiming this course has spent ten
sections teaching you to avoid==.
:::

@narrate
One honest trap worth naming, because the pressure to show results
early is real, whether it's coming from a client or your own manager.

Jumping to phase three before phase two has actually happened means
pitching a case study that doesn't exist yet, or reporting a ratio
you're guessing at rather than one you've measured. That's the same
mistake Section 9.5 warned against in a report, just moved earlier in
the timeline. It doesn't stop being dishonest because the pressure
came before the work instead of after it.

If someone's asking for results before day sixty, tell them honestly
what phase you're actually in. "Building the testing architecture,
first real read expected in two weeks" is a true, useful sentence. A
fabricated early win is neither.

Would that honest sentence actually satisfy the person asking? Often,
yes, more than you'd expect. Most reasonable stakeholders can handle
"here's exactly where we are" far better than they can handle
discovering later that an early result was inflated to buy time. The
honest answer costs a moment of discomfort now. The fabricated one
costs trust later, all at once.

@narrate
Section eleven is the capstone: one full product, one full sprint,
brief to honest read, exactly as this plan's first sixty days
describes, compressed into a guided exercise you can complete before
starting the real thing.

Before that, pick your actual day one. Not a hypothetical future
Monday, the specific date you'll open Section 2's product truth doc for
whatever you're actually building this on. A plan without a start date
is a plan that starts whenever motivation happens to be highest, which
is a schedule that rarely arrives on its own.

Write that date down somewhere visible, the same discipline this whole
course has asked of every other decision worth keeping. A start date
that only exists in your head is one more good intention. A start date
written down, next to the checklist from this lecture, is the beginning
of an actual ninety days.
