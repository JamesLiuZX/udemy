## Dana said no to one line, and the swelling claim died right there.
lead: That's what a review gate actually looks like. Four checks only work if one specific person, at one specific moment, is actually responsible for running them.

@narrate
The swelling claim didn't die because a checklist document existed
somewhere. It died because Dana looked at it, said no, and everyone on
the account had already agreed her no was final. That's the part a
checklist alone never guarantees.

Everything from the last four lectures, claims, disclosure, rights,
accuracy, is only real if someone is actually accountable for checking
it. A checklist that exists in a document but belongs to nobody gets
skipped the first time a deadline gets tight, which is exactly when it
matters most.

So this lecture answers two plain questions. Who signs off. And at what
point in the process that sign-off actually happens, before generation
starts, or right before spend.

Why does this need its own lecture, rather than a line at the bottom of
each earlier one? Because a check without an owner tends to quietly
become everyone's job, which in practice means it's nobody's job. The
four checks from this section are genuinely good ones. They still fail
in the field constantly, and almost never because the check itself was
wrong. They fail because nobody was actually on the hook for running
them.

@slide table
kicker: Who, and when
## One name, one moment, per check

| Check | Who signs off | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Claims | Whoever owns the product truth doc, Dana on Harlan's account | Before the script is finalised |
| Rights | Whoever runs the ad account, Sam on Harlan's account | Before generation, and again before spend |
| Accuracy | The person who built the brief | Right after generation, before the batch moves on |
| Disclosure | Whoever uploads the final ad | At upload, as part of the platform flow itself |

@narrate
Notice this table assigns a name to every row, not a vague "the team"
or "whoever has time." On a two-person account like Harlan's, that's
Dana for claims, because she's the one who actually knows what the
product can defend, and Sam for rights and disclosure, because he's the
one touching the ad account where those checks actually bite.

If you're running this solo, all four rows collapse to one name: yours.
That's not a smaller job, it just means building the discipline to run
all four checks yourself, deliberately, rather than assuming a second set
of eyes will catch what you missed.

Notice, too, that the table splits ownership by expertise, not by
seniority. Dana owns claims because she's the one who actually knows the
product, not because she's the founder. Sam owns rights because he's the
one inside the ad account daily, not because it's beneath anyone else.
Assign each check to whoever's closest to the actual knowledge it
requires, and the check gets faster and more accurate as a side effect.

## Three checkpoints, not one

```text
BEFORE GENERATION
Claims checked against the product truth doc.
Rights confirmed: avatar tier, music licence.

RIGHT AFTER GENERATION
Accuracy checked against the real product.
Regenerate anything that fails, don't ship it flagged.

BEFORE UPLOAD
Disclosure added. Final rights sweep for anything
a generator added without being asked.
```

@narrate
Here's why this happens at three separate points instead of one big
review at the end. Catching a claims problem before generation costs you
a sentence. Catching the same problem after a batch is fully cut costs
you a reshoot, a re-render, and the time already spent on assembly.

The disclosure checkpoint sitting at upload, rather than earlier, is
deliberate too. It's the one check that genuinely doesn't need to slow
anything down before it, because adding a caption doesn't touch the
script or the shot. Putting it last keeps the earlier checkpoints focused
on the things that actually do require a redo if they're wrong.

Three checkpoints isn't more work than one. It's the same total checking,
moved earlier, where each individual catch is cheaper. That's the entire
logic behind pre-flight QC: not doing more checking, doing the same
checking sooner.

Ask yourself where your own current process actually catches problems
today. If the honest answer is "whenever someone happens to notice,"
that's not a checkpoint, it's luck with a good track record so far. Luck
runs out eventually, usually on the exact batch you were rushing to
ship.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
A review gate only works if it's actually enforced, not just documented.
==If nobody has the authority to hold a batch back, you don't have a
review gate. You have a suggestion==.
:::

@narrate
One honest distinction worth being blunt about.

A checklist written down somewhere is not the same thing as a checklist
that can actually stop a batch from shipping. On Harlan's account, Dana's
sign-off genuinely blocks a claim from going live, because everyone
involved has agreed that's how it works. If your version of this is just
a document nobody's actually empowered to enforce, it's decoration, not a
gate.

This matters especially once you're moving fast. Section nine's weekly
sprint asks you to generate and ship at a real pace, and pace is exactly
when an unenforced checklist gets quietly skipped first. Build the
authority into the gate now, while there's no deadline pressure testing
it, not later, once there is.

Think about what "enforced" actually requires in practice. It doesn't
need a legal department or a formal process document. It needs one
simple rule everyone involved has actually agreed to out loud: nothing
ships until the named owner says yes. That's the whole mechanism. It
works because everyone knows it's real, not because it's elaborate.
