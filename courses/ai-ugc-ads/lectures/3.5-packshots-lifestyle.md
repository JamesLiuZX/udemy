## Sam had two more images open, a clean product shot and a break-room scene. Only one of them was safe to generate blind.
lead: A packshot and a lifestyle scene fail differently when they fail.

@narrate
Sam had two more images open side by side: a clean shot of the sock alone,
and a scene of someone mid-shift adjusting one. Both had come out of the
same batch, both looked fine at a glance, and his first instinct was to
run identical checks on both. That instinct turned out to be wrong.

Nothing about the two images announced the difference. They were the same
resolution, the same brief, generated the same afternoon. The difference
only showed up once he asked what each one was actually for, which is
exactly the question this lecture wants you asking before you generate
either kind again.

One more generation technique before the workshop, and it's really a
distinction worth having clearly in your head: a packshot, the product
alone doing the work of a specification sheet, and a lifestyle scene, the
product in a moment doing the work of a story, are not the same job
wearing different lighting. Confuse which one you're generating, and
you'll brief the wrong thing every time.

Which one is which usually isn't hard to tell once you ask what job the
image is doing. If it's answering "what exactly am I buying", it's a
packshot. If it's answering "what would this feel like to own", it's a
lifestyle scene. Different question, different standard of accuracy.

@slide two-col
kicker: Two jobs, two different risk profiles
## What each one actually has to get right

::: cols
:: card bad
### Packshot
- Has to be exactly, literally accurate
- Often doubles as the product listing image
- Wrong here means a customer receives something that doesn't match
:: card good
### Lifestyle scene
- Has to feel true, not be literally documentary
- Nobody expects a stock scene to be a specific real moment
- Wrong here is a missed vibe, not a broken promise
:::

@narrate
Here's why the distinction matters more than it sounds like it should.

A packshot often does double duty: it's the ad creative, and it's close
enough to what shows on the product page that a mismatch reads as false
advertising, not just an off day. A lifestyle scene carries a completely
different kind of risk. Nobody assumes the person in a stock-style lifestyle
shot is a specific, real customer having a specific, real moment. It has to
feel true. It doesn't have to be literally true the way a packshot does.

Would a customer feel misled if they knew exactly how this image was made?
For a lifestyle scene, almost never, because nobody assumed it was a
documentary in the first place. For a packshot standing in as the product
photo, the honest answer is often yes, and that's the test worth running
before you decide where a given image is allowed to come from.

Run that same question down your own angle map before you generate
anything. Most rows sort themselves in a few seconds once you ask what
job the image is doing, and the ones that don't sort easily are usually
the ones worth a second look before you decide.

## The honest split for Harlan

| Image type | Safe to generate | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ad packshot, ordinary use | Yes, from a locked reference | Testing creative, not the listing itself |
| Product listing photo | No | This is what the customer actually receives |
| Lifestyle scene, no face | Yes | Feeling, not documentation |
| Lifestyle scene, with a face | Yes, with 3.3's checks | Same uncanny-valley risk applies |

@narrate
Here's the honest split, applied directly to Harlan's account.

An ad packshot, generated from the same locked reference from Section
3.3, is safe territory, because it's testing creative, not standing in for
the actual purchase decision. The product listing photo itself is where
this course draws a hard line: that image is effectively a promise about
what arrives in the box, and it should be a real photo of the real
product, every time. Lifestyle scenes, with or without a face, are safe to
generate, carrying forward exactly the checks Section 3.3 already gave
you.

Notice this table doesn't introduce a single new check. Every "yes" in it
still owes you the colour lock, the product-accuracy read, and the honest
face-or-no-face call from last lecture. What changes here is only the
question of which image type this course lets you generate at all, not
how carefully you check the ones it does.

Notice also there's exactly one "no" on the whole table, and it's not a
grudging exception. The listing photo is the one image in this entire
workflow where the cost of being wrong isn't a wasted test, it's a
customer holding something in their hands that doesn't match what sold
them. Everything else on this table earns its "yes" precisely because
getting it wrong costs a lot less.

kicker: A working prompt pattern
## Anchoring a lifestyle scene to the real product

```text
Lifestyle scene: person mid-shift, seated briefly, adjusting
a Harlan Crew Sock, hex #1B2A41, matched to reference image.
Natural break-room lighting, no studio gloss.
No overlay text. No face close-up.
```

@narrate
Here's what a working prompt actually looks like, carrying forward every
discipline from the last two lectures rather than starting fresh.

The hex lock and reference image from Section 3.3 come along unchanged.
No overlay text, because anything worth saying exactly goes through
Section 3.4's workflow instead. And notice the instruction against a face
close-up: this scene doesn't need one to do its job, so the safest version
of it doesn't generate one to find out.

Read this prompt as a template, not a one-off. Every lifestyle angle on
Harlan's map can start from this same skeleton, with only the moment and
the setting changed: a different break room, a different point in the
shift, the same locked colour and the same discipline about faces and
text underneath every version.

That's the actual efficiency win here, and it's easy to miss under all the
detail. Writing one disciplined template once is what lets twenty lifestyle
variants take minutes instead of twenty separate negotiations with a
generator over what "natural lighting" or "not too posed" should mean each
time.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest limit

::: callout Be straight about this
Generating a lifestyle scene doesn't replace real product photography
everywhere. ==The one photo a customer's purchase decision actually rests
on stays real==, every time, no exceptions.
:::

@narrate
One line worth holding onto after this lecture, because it's the one rule
in this section with no exceptions.

Everything else here is genuinely safe to generate, checked properly. The
product listing photo is not, and that's not caution for its own sake.
It's the one image where "close enough" becomes a promise you can't keep
if the real product doesn't match what a customer saw before they bought
it.

If you're ever unsure which side of the line a specific image sits on,
default to the stricter answer. Treating a borderline case as ad creative
when it's really acting as the listing photo is a mistake that surfaces
as a return, a complaint, or worse, only after real money has already
changed hands.

The test is always the same one from earlier in this lecture: does a
customer's actual purchase decision rest on this specific image being
accurate. If yes, it's a listing photo wearing an ad's clothing, and it
gets a camera, not a generator, no matter how good the generated version
looks sitting next to the real ones.
