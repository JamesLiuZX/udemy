## Two failures, caught before the batch was used

```text
STATIC BATCH (Section 3.2-3.4)
Twenty statics generated from the brief.
Failure one: Harlan Navy rendered as royal blue on
several variants. Caught, regenerated correctly.
Failure two: the "15-20 mmHg" spec overlay garbled
into unreadable text. Caught, fixed before use.

VIDEO (Section 4.1-4.3)
A 15-second "12-hour shift" hook, a 30-second founder
explainer. First avatar draft used a stock avatar whose
licence didn't clear paid social. Recast before spend.
```

@narrate
Notice all three failures share something. None of them were caught by
luck. Brand control checking, text-on-image review, and licence
verification are specific disciplines Sections 3 and 4 taught for
exactly this reason, and all three caught a real problem before it
ever reached an audience.

This is also the moment worth sitting with if your own capstone hasn't
hit a failure like this yet. It doesn't mean your process is worse. It
might mean you haven't generated enough variants yet to find where
the tool's limits actually are. These failures are common, not a sign
Harlan's batch was unusually troubled.

Look again at how each failure was actually caught. Brand colour got
checked against the product truth doc's real values, not eyeballed for
"close enough." The spec overlay got read at full size, not glanced at
in a thumbnail grid. The avatar's licence terms got checked line by
line before anything was recast around it. None of these catches
happened automatically. Each one needed someone to actually apply the
specific check Sections 3 and 4 taught, deliberately, on every single
variant in the batch.

That's worth remembering the next time a batch of twenty looks mostly
fine at a glance. Mostly fine is exactly the condition these checks
exist for. A batch that's plainly broken doesn't need a checklist to
catch it.

## What Section 6's checklist actually caught

| Draft | The problem | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An early static claim | "Clinically proven to reduce swelling by 40%" | Rewritten to the 15-20 mmHg spec claim, per 6.2 |
| The AI avatar video | No synthetic-media disclosure | Shipped with an on-screen caption, per 6.3 |

@narrate
This is the batch's most important catch. A forty percent swelling
claim nobody ever tested, invented somewhere in the drafting process,
would have been the single fastest way to a policy strike or worse if
it had shipped. Section 6.6's checklist caught it before a dollar of
spend went near it.

Notice what replaced it: not a softer version of the same invented
number, a completely different claim, one Dana can actually defend. The
compression spec was always defensible. The forty percent never was.
That's the difference pre-flight exists to catch, not just soften.

Notice, too, that this catch happened at the human review gate Section
6.5 built, not automatically. A checklist only works if someone
actually runs it, on every draft, including the ones that read
convincingly. The forty percent claim was written fluently and
confidently. It was still false, and confidence was never the test that
mattered. The product truth doc was.

The disclosure caption on the avatar video is a smaller catch by
comparison, but the same discipline produced it. A synthetic performer
appearing in a paid ad, without disclosure, was a real gap Section 6.3
exists to close, checked and fixed the same way the claim was, before
either one ever reached a real audience.
