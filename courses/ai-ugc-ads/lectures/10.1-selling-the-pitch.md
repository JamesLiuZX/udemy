## Most creative pitches sell certainty nobody actually has. Sell the system instead, and the noise becomes proof, not a liability.
lead: The honest reporting Section 9.5 built is the actual pitch. You don't need a better story than the truth.

@narrate
Here's what most competing pitches sound like: guaranteed results, a
portfolio of nothing but winners, promises about what will "go viral."
None of that is a system. It's a hope dressed up as a plan, and anyone
who's run ads for more than a month has already been burned by exactly
this kind of pitch once.

The pitch this lecture teaches sounds almost boring by comparison, and
that's precisely why it lands. It leads with the testing architecture,
the honest winner-loser-noise ratio, the fatigue watch, all the
unglamorous discipline the last three sections actually built. Nobody
else selling creative work is pitching a process. Most are pitching
luck and calling it expertise.

Sam's redacted Harlan report from Section 9.5 is the actual proof this
pitch runs on, not a script written in the abstract. A real testing
architecture, real numbers, one clear winner against several honest
noise results. That report is the pitch, almost unedited.

Notice what "almost unedited" is doing there. Redacting Harlan's brand
name and specific numbers protects the client relationship. It doesn't
soften a single honest line about how the account actually performed.
The structure of the report, the ratio, the discipline, travels intact
into the pitch exactly as written.

@slide table
kicker: Two different pitches
## What sells confidence versus what sells competence

| Most pitches lead with | This pitch leads with |
| --- | --- |
| "We'll make ads that go viral" | "We'll build a testing structure that finds what actually converts" |
| A portfolio of only winning creative | A redacted report showing the honest ratio, winner included |
| A promise about results | A process, with kill rules and a CAC ceiling decided in advance |
| Vague claims about "AI-powered" work | The specific artifacts this course actually produces, named |

@narrate
Read the left column as a warning, not a rival strategy to borrow from.
A prospect who's been pitched the left column before, and most have,
recognises it instantly and has learned not to trust it. Leading with
the right column signals something different: you know what a testing
programme actually looks like, including the parts that aren't
flattering.

The third row is worth dwelling on. A promise about results is exactly
what Section 8's whole tree exists to avoid making about your own
account, and it's not honest to make one about someone else's either. A
kill rule and a CAC ceiling, decided before any spend happens, is a
credible commitment. A results guarantee is a marketing line no
structure actually backs.

The fourth row matters more than it looks. "AI-powered" describes
almost every competitor by now and means nothing specific. Naming the
actual artifacts, a brief system, a pre-flight checklist, a testing
architecture, tells a prospect exactly what they're buying, which is
the opposite of a vague promise.

Run a quick test on this table before you use it. Read the left column
aloud and notice how familiar it sounds, because you've likely heard
some version of it pitched to you already. That recognition, in the
room, does real work before you've said a word of your own pitch.

@slide code
kicker: The pitch, structured
## Four parts, in this order

```text
1. THE PROBLEM: "AI made creative volume cheap. It didn't make
   picking winners any easier. Most teams still guess."

2. THE SYSTEM: name the actual pieces, brief, pre-flight,
   testing architecture, honest read, weekly sprint.

3. THE PROOF: a redacted report (9.5's format), winner and
   noise both shown, not just the win.

4. THE ASK: what the first sprint actually costs and delivers,
   decided before work starts, per Section 10.2.
```

@narrate
Four parts, and notice the proof comes before the ask, not after.
Leading with price invites a prospect to evaluate you against a number
before they understand what the number buys. Leading with a real,
honest report earns the right to name a price afterward.

The problem statement in part one is doing careful work. It names the
actual shift, cheap generation, expensive judgement, the same argument
Section 1 opened this whole course with. A prospect who recognises that
problem in their own account is already most of the way to trusting
the system that solves it.

Notice what's missing from all four parts: a single invented success
story. Everything here traces back to a report you can actually
produce, not a claim you'd have to defend under questioning. That's
deliberate, and it's the entire reason this pitch survives scrutiny
that a flashier one wouldn't.

Practice all four parts out loud before you use them for real, not just
read silently. A pitch that reads well on a page can still trip on
"the honest ratio" or "kill rule" the first time you say them under
pressure, and stumbling over your own system's vocabulary undercuts the
exact credibility this structure is built to earn.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trap

::: callout Be straight about this
This pitch sounds less exciting than a competitor's guarantee. ==That's
the trade you're making on purpose. A less exciting pitch that survives
the first hard question beats an exciting one that doesn't==.
:::

@narrate
One honest trade worth naming before you use this pitch for real.

A guarantee is exciting to hear and impossible to keep. The first time
a prospect's account produces mostly noise, which Section 8.2 already
told you is the honest normal, a guaranteed-results pitch collapses
under its own promise. This pitch never made that promise, so it has
nothing to collapse.

Would you rather win a pitch you can't actually deliver on, or a
slightly harder pitch you can defend for as long as the relationship
lasts? The honest ratio is a harder sell in the room and a much easier
one to live with six months later, when the account's actual results
have to match whatever you said at the start.

Expect to lose some pitches to the exciting version of this
conversation, especially early on. That's not a sign the honest pitch
is wrong, it's the cost of refusing to promise something you can't
keep. The clients this pitch does win are the ones worth having for six
months, not the ones who leave the moment the first noise result
arrives.
