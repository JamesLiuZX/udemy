## This used to be a platform's house rule. As of this year, in some places, it's actually the law.
lead: Sam had the avatar clip from Section 4.3 ready to upload, licence confirmed, script clean, when one flag stopped him for five seconds.

@narrate
The question was simple: does this need a synthetic-media disclosure,
a caption telling the viewer plainly that the person on screen isn't
real, before it goes anywhere near an audience? It did. Adding it took
less time than reading this paragraph.

Back in Section 4.1, that flag was a line item, one more thing to check
before an avatar clip shipped. It deserves its own lecture now, because
the ground underneath it moved substantially this year.

As of this recording, Meta requires disclosure on AI-generated or
AI-modified ad content as a matter of enforced policy, not a courtesy.
TikTok requires the same for anything significantly AI-edited. And this
is no longer only a platform preference. Regulators have started writing
some version of the same requirement into actual law.

What does that mean for you, given everything you've built since Section
three? Almost every asset in your library qualifies: a generated static,
an AI avatar, an AI-touched-up product shot. This isn't a corner case.
It's the default condition of the creative this course has taught you to
make.

That's not a reason for alarm, it's a reason for a habit. Treated as the
default assumption, the way this lecture shows you, disclosure is a
five-second addition to a workflow you've already built, not a redesign
of it.

## Platform policy, and where it's already become law

| Where | What's required | What happens if you skip it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meta ads | Visible label on AI-generated or AI-modified creative | Rejected in review; repeated misses risk account restriction |
| TikTok ads | Label or disclosure on significantly AI-edited content | Same category of enforcement as any other policy violation |
| New York consumers, from June 2026 | Disclosure for synthetic performers depicting real humans | A real financial penalty, not just a platform strike |
| EU, from August 2026 | Transparency rules under the EU AI Act | A legal obligation, not a platform's house rule |

@narrate
Notice the direction this table moves in, left to right. The platform
rows are policy, enforced by rejection and account risk. The regulation
rows are law, enforced by an actual penalty. That's the genuinely new
development this year: the same underlying idea, don't let a viewer
mistake synthetic content for something real, has started showing up in
statute, not just a platform's terms of service.

Ask yourself which row actually applies to you. If your audience is
anywhere near New York, or if you're running ads that reach the EU, the
right-hand rows aren't hypothetical, they're your actual exposure, on top
of whatever platform policy already asks of you. Check where your
audience actually is before you assume only the platform rows matter to
your account.

Notice, too, that these rows don't require you to run separate creative
per jurisdiction. A disclosure caption honest enough to satisfy New
York's law is also honest enough to satisfy Meta's policy and the EU's
transparency rules, because all four rows are converging on roughly the
same underlying ask: tell a viewer plainly when something's synthetic.
Build to the strictest row you're actually exposed to, and the looser
ones take care of themselves.

## The avatar video, disclosed properly

```text
THE CLIP
Fifteen-second avatar ad, generated in Section 4.3,
properly licensed on the commercial tier.

THE FLAG
Synthetic-media disclosure required: a generated
avatar performing a script, not a real person.

WHAT SHIPS
Same clip. On-screen caption, visible for the full
runtime: "Made with AI." Plus the platform's own
label, applied automatically once flagged correctly.
```

@narrate
Here's what disclosure actually looks like on a real ad, not as an
abstract requirement but as one line of on-screen text.

The clip itself doesn't change. Nothing about the script, the delivery,
or the assembly from Section 4.5 gets touched. What changes is one
caption, visible for the whole runtime, stating plainly that this is
AI-made. Properly flagged in the ad platform, the platform's own label
gets applied on top of that, automatically, the same way a caption
carries a claim the way Section 4.5 already taught you to treat one.

Notice what this costs you in practice: almost nothing. One line of
on-screen text and one honest answer to a flag in the ads platform's
upload flow. Compare that to the cost of skipping it, a rejected ad at
best, a real penalty at worst, and this becomes one of the cheapest
checks in this entire section relative to what it protects you from.

@narrate
I want to be precise about why this lecture exists at all, rather than
folding disclosure into a single bullet point back in Section four.

A platform account restriction is a real cost. A penalty under an actual
law is a different order of cost entirely, and as of this recording, more
than one jurisdiction has started applying real financial penalties to
exactly this failure. Whatever the exact numbers are by the time you're
watching this, the direction is the one thing worth trusting completely:
disclosure moved from optional, to platform-enforced, to legally
required, inside a single year. Bet on that direction continuing, not on
today's specific numbers staying put.

What should you actually do with a trend like that, rather than just
noting it? Build the habit now, while the cost of skipping it is still
mostly a rejected ad, so it's already automatic by the time it's a legal
one. Nobody builds a new habit well under the pressure of an actual
penalty. You build it now, cheaply, while the stakes are still forgiving
enough to learn from a mistake instead of paying for one.
