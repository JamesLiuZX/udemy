@slide statement
kicker: Section 1 · The shape of the data
## If you think ad performance clusters around an average, you're using the wrong shape.
lead: It doesn't cluster. It piles up at zero, with a long, thin tail stretching past it.

@narrate
Quick question before the idea. When you hear "the average ad in this test
got a two percent click-through rate", what picture forms in your head?

Most people picture a bell curve: a hump in the middle, most ads somewhere
near that two percent, a few better, a few worse. That picture is wrong for
almost every creative test you'll ever run, and believing it costs real
money.

It's not a small error either. It's the kind of wrong assumption that
quietly shapes every decision downstream of it: how you split budget, how
many variants you bother making, even how you read a dashboard at a glance.
Get the shape wrong and every one of those decisions inherits the mistake.

That's not a bell curve. That's a pile at the bottom with a long thin tail
running off to one side, and it's the shape almost every real creative test
actually takes, whether the product is socks, software, or anything else
sold through paid social.

Notice something else about this picture. If you'd stopped the test after
looking at the first six or seven variants, you'd have seen nothing but the
pile. The interesting result was waiting at the far end, and nothing about
the early data would have told you it was coming. That's a separate problem
from the shape itself, and it's exactly what the next lecture's volume maths
solves.

@slide definition
kicker: Naming it properly
## Heavy-tailed, not normal

::: definition Key idea
A **normal distribution** clusters around a mean: most values are close to
average, extremes are rare and roughly symmetric. A **heavy-tailed
distribution** piles most values near zero, with occasional extreme results
far out in the tail. Ad performance across a batch of variants is heavy-tailed.
:::

::: example Try this yourself
Rank your last real test by result, not by date. If the top result is worth
several times the median result, you're looking at a tail, not an average.
:::

@narrate
Let's name it properly, because the name tells you what to do differently.

A normal distribution is the bell curve: most things are close to typical,
and the further you get from typical in either direction, the rarer it gets,
symmetrically. A heavy-tailed distribution doesn't behave like that. Most
outcomes sit near zero, and every so often, one sits far out on its own,
disproportionate to everything around it.

You'll have met this shape before, even if nobody named it. It's the shape
of book sales, where a handful of titles outsell everything else combined.
It's the shape of a venture portfolio, where one investment returns the fund
and the rest mostly don't. Creative testing follows the same pattern, and
once you know to expect it, a lot of otherwise confusing results stop being
confusing.

Rank your own last test by actual result rather than by launch date, and
look at the gap between your best performer and your middle performer. If
that gap is large, you already have a heavy tail. Most testing programmes
do, whether anyone's named it or not.

@slide two-col
kicker: Why the average lies
## What "average CTR" hides

::: cols
:: card bad
### What the average tells you
- A single number: "this batch averaged 1.1% CTR"
- Implies most variants performed near that number
- Makes every variant look like a modest, similar result
:: card good
### What actually happened
- Ten variants near zero, one or two doing almost everything
- No variant performed anywhere near the average
- The average describes a variant that doesn't exist
:::

@narrate
Here's why that average is actively misleading, not just imprecise.

When ten variants barely move and one dominates, the average sits somewhere
in between, at a number none of the twelve variants actually hit. You could
report "1.1 percent average CTR" and it would be true, and it would also
describe a variant that does not exist anywhere in your data.

This is exactly how a dashboard can tell two different stories to two
different people. Someone glancing at "1.1 percent average" concludes the
batch was mediocre across the board and moves on. Someone who ranks the
same twelve variants individually finds a result worth scaling immediately.
Same data. Completely different decision, depending on whether you looked at
the summary or the shape.

If you make decisions off that average, treating every variant as
roughly equivalent, you'll under-invest in the one that's actually working
and over-invest in eleven that were never going anywhere.

@slide statement
kicker: What this changes
class: center
## Don't spread budget evenly across variants. Find the tail, then feed it.
lead: A heavy-tailed result rewards concentration, not fairness.

@narrate
So here's what this actually changes about how you run a test.

An even split, the same budget to all twenty variants, feels fair and feels
careful. Against a heavy-tailed result, it's neither. It spends real money
proving that most of your variants are exactly as unremarkable as the shape
predicted, and it slows down how fast you find and feed the one that isn't.

The better move, and you'll build the actual mechanics of it in Section 7, is
a fast, cheap first pass across everything, followed by concentrating
spend hard on whatever's already pulling ahead. You're not being unfair to
the losing variants. You're respecting the shape of the data instead of
fighting it.

This also changes what a good test plan looks like on paper. If someone
hands you a plan that spends identically across every variant for the full
test window, that's a plan built for a bell curve. A plan built for a heavy
tail front-loads a cheap screening pass and reserves most of the budget for
whatever the screen finds.

@slide callout
kicker: The honest trade

::: callout Be straight about this
This means most of what you generate will look like it failed. It didn't
fail. ==Losing was the expected outcome for most of the batch==, and it's
what makes the one that doesn't lose worth finding.
:::

@narrate
I want to be honest about the part of this that's genuinely uncomfortable.

If you run a batch of twenty and eighteen of them underperform, that can
feel like eighteen failures, especially to anyone paying for the media spend.
It isn't. In a heavy-tailed world, that's what a normal, healthy test looks
like. The failure mode isn't generating variants that lose. It's stopping
before you've found the one that doesn't.

You will have to explain this to someone eventually: a founder, a client, a
boss, watching a spend report full of small losses and asking whether the
programme is working. The honest answer is that you can't tell from the
losses. You can only tell from whether a tail has shown up yet, and that
means holding your nerve through a report that looks worse than it is.
